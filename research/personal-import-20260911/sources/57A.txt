                                                   attention these conjectures have attracted over the past decades both in the combinatorial and algebraic
                                                   communities, for more than 40 years the best known lower bound for the maximum     length of a cycle (path)

                                           1       Introduction
                                           Hamiltonicity is one of the oldest and most fundamental notions in graph theory and has been studied
                                           since the beginnings of the subject. A Hamilton cycle (path) in a graph is a cycle (path) which covers all
                                           vertices, and a graph is called Hamiltonian or traceable if it contains a Hamilton cycle (path). Apart from
                                           being a very natural object of study in combinatorics, Hamiltonicity also has strong ties to more applied
                                           algorithmic problems such as the Traveling Salesman Problem in theoretical computer science. Deciding
                                           whether a graph has a Hamiltonian cycle (path) is one of the well-known 21 NP-hard problems from Karp’s
                                           list of such problems [14], and thus easily checkable sufficient and necessary conditions for Hamiltonicity
                                           are of high interest and have been extensively studied. Probably the most famous such condition is Dirac’s
                                           theorem from 1952, showing that a minimum degree bound of n/2 in an n-vertex graph is sufficient (and
                                           in general best possible) to guarantee a Hamilton cycle in a graph. While many generalizations of Dirac’s
                                           condition have been discovered, there remains a surprising scarcity of sufficient conditions for Hamiltonicity
                                           that apply to very sparse graphs of constant degree, say, and that in particular include the cycle graphs Cn for
                                           n ≥ 3. One such potential condition which stands out in this sense was formulated conjecturally by Lovász
                                           in 1969 [19]. His conjecture, nowadays famous as the Lovász conjecture, states that every connected vertex-
                                           transitive graph admits a Hamiltonian path. Vertex-transitive graphs (also known as symmetric graphs)
                                           can have a rich variety of structure and densities, and include large complicated classes of cubic graphs.
                                           Thus this condition, if true, would be quite orthogonal to the known sufficient conditions for Hamiltonicity

often attributed to Thomassen (see [2]) states that with finitely many exceptions, all connected vertex-
transitive graphs even admit a Hamilton cycle. Variants of Lovász’ and Thomassen’s conjectures, such as
their restriction to the important special case of Cayley graphs of finite groups, trace back even further, for
instance to the work of Rankin [23] in 1948 and the work of Rapaport-Strasser in 1959 [24]. We remark in
passing that also the corresponding questions for directed paths and cycles in vertex-transitive digraphs are
equally interesting, see [7] for a summary of the history of and some recent progress on these questions.
    Lovász’ and Thomassen’s conjectures have attracted widespread attention in the last 50 years, with
many partial results on special cases, such as graphs of a particular order or Cayley graphs of specific
groups, see [1, 16, 29] for three surveys on these conjectures and [3, 6, 10, 21] for some recent positive
results confirming the conjecture for Kneser graphs and for moderately dense and random Cayley graphs,
respectively.
    While these results all only apply to specific types of vertex-transitive graphs, such as those whose order
is a particular type of number or those that correspond to specific Cayley graphs, general positive results on
the conjectures which apply to all vertex-transitive graphs have remained few. Probably the most general
such line of attack has been to give lower bounds on the function ℓ(n), defined as the maximum integer such
that every connected vertex-transitive graph of order n ≥ 3 contains a cycle of length at least ℓ(n). While
the aforementioned stronger form of Lovász’ conjecture claims that ℓ(n) = n for sufficiently large n, for
                                                                            √
over 40 years the best known lower bound remained stuck at ℓ(n) ≥ Ω( n) due to Babai [2]. But recently,
the problem has regained momentum with some exciting new advances. Namely, in a very nice paper from
2023, DeVos [9] improved Babai’s bound to ℓ(n) ≥ Ω(n3/5 ). This was further improved to ℓ(n) ≥ Ω(n13/21 )
by Groenland et al. [12] and then to ℓ(n) ≥ Ω(n9/14 ) by Norin et al. [22], which has remained the state of
the art thus far.

Our results. In this paper, we improve the lower bound towards Lovász’ and Thomassen’s conjectures
further to ℓ(n) ≥ n2/3−o(1) , as formally stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1. Let ε > 0. Then, for every sufficiently large number n, every connected vertex-transitive
graph of order n contains a cycle of length at least n2/3−ε .

   Importantly, Theorem 1.1 represents more than just another small nudge towards the conjectures—it
actually achieves the maximum that was theoretically possible with the current approaches from previous
work towards Lovász’ and Thomassen’s conjectures, and thus hits a natural barrier of the existing methods.
To make this clear, in the next two paragraphs we summarize the two existing approaches towards improved
lower bounds for ℓ(n) from the previous works [9, 12, 20, 22] which are all based on structural results about
longest paths and cycles in general graphs, explain how our approach relates to these frameworks and how it
exhausts the natural limits of both approaches. Along the way, we also state an additional result on longest
path transversals implied by our proof.

Intersections of and hitting sets for longest paths and cycles. There are many fundamental open
problems about basic properties of longest paths and cycles in graphs that remain wide open. The probably
earliest question in this direction was raised already in 1966 by Gallai. To state the question formally, let us
define a longest path (cycle) transversal in a graph G as a subset of vertices intersecting every longest path
(cycle) in the graph, and let us denote by lpt(G), lct(G), respectively, the minimum size of such a longest
path (cycle) transversal in G. Motivated by the well-known folklore fact that any two longest paths in a
connected graph must intersect, Gallai [11] asked whether every connected graph G satisfies lpt(G) = 1.
While this was shown to be false by Walther [26], the relaxed question [27, 30] whether one can bound lpt(G)
by an absolute constant for all connected graphs (and analogously, whether one can bound lct(G) by an
absolute constant for all 2-connected graphs) has remained open for 50 years. The best known upper bounds
on these parameters in terms of the order of the graph are due to Norin et al. [22], who improved several

    For Smith’s conjecture, the connection to bounds on ℓ(n) is more subtle. In fact, a direct relation only
exists to a natural “local” variant of Smith’s problem: For every k ∈ N, let us denote by s(k) the smallest
integer such that for every pair C1 , C2 of longest cycles in a graph intersecting in at most k vertices, there
exists a vertex-separator of size at most s(k) in G that separates C1 from C2 . Clearly, if s(k) < r for some
values of k, r ∈ N then any two longest cycles in an r-connected graph intersect in more than k vertices. In

                                                      3
fact, all the progress towards Smith’s conjecture thus far has worked in this local setting and has proved
corresponding upper bounds for s(k), culminating in the aforementioned state of the art bound due to Ma
and Zhao [20] who obtained s(k) = O(k 3/2 ). The trivial lower bound for s(k) is s(k) ≥ k, and it seems
possible that s(k) grows linearly in k.
    The connection between this “local” variant of Smith’s conjecture and lower bounds on ℓ(n) is as follows:
Groenland et al. [12] implicitly proved that any upper bound of the form s(k) = O(k α ) for some α ∈ [1, ∞)
immediately yields a lower bound of ℓ(n) ≥ Ω(n(1+α)/(1+2α) ) for long cycles in connected vertex-transitive
graphs. Hence, the hard limit for a lower bound on ℓ(n) that could potentially be derived from this approach
is ℓ(n) ≥ Ω(n2/3 ). Our Theorem 1.1 asymptotically achieves this theoretical limit via a different approach.

Key technical result and overview. To prove Theorem 1.2 and thus our main result Theorem 1.1, we
improve a key lemma from the previous work of Norin et al. [22] about finding a path using many edges of
a matching spanned within a cycle. Concretely, we prove the following asymptotically tight result.

Theorem 1.3. For every fixed ε > 0 and every sufficiently large n ∈ N, the following holds. Let L be a
cycle with cyclic vertex-sequence v1 , . . . , v2n and M a perfect matching between v1 , . . . , vn and vn+1 , . . . , v2n .
Then, there exists a path in the graph L ∪ M which uses at least n1−ε edges of M .

    Norin et al. [22, Lemma 8] proved an analogous result, but only for the fixed value ε = 0.2. The main
novelty of this paper lies in proving this key result (Theorem 1.3). The reduction to Theorem 1.3 has already
been observed in the work of Norin et al. Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness of the presentation and
since there are a few formal differences, we decided to include the complete deduction of Theorem 1.2 from
Theorem 1.3 in the appendix of this paper.
    Our proof of Theorem 1.3 combines recent embedding techniques for paths in sublinear expanders as well
as the tool of expander decompositions of almost-regular graphs from the recent work of Letzter et al. [17]
with an array of new combinatorial ideas to glue many short path segments into one big path which uses
many edges of the matching M . In Section 2 we prepare the proof of our main result, Theorem 1.3, with
several auxiliary results about embedding vertex-disjoint paths into sublinear expanders. In Section 3 we
then prove a key lemma about cycle decompositions of Eulerian graphs, which will later be instrumental for
proving Theorem 1.3 (which is done in Section 4): Here, given as input a graph L ∪ M as in the statement
of Theorem 1.3, we first contract some segments of the cycle L into single vertices to increase the average
degree of the graph at hand to be polynomial in n. We then apply the tools from Section 2 to decompose
this auxiliary graph into disjoint sublinear expander pieces, within which we can find suitable collections
of disjoint paths, exhausting a significant amount of vertices of the expander pieces. Finally, we use the
technical lemma from Section 3 to glue these paths from different pieces together into one path in the
auxiliary graph such that it expands to a path in the original graph L ∪ M using many edges of M . This
overview is extremely oversimplified, but we still think it is helpful to keep it in mind as a guide when
reading through the following sections.

    Given a graph G, we denote by V (G) and E(G) its vertex- and edge-set, respectively. For a subset

we denote by eG (A, B) the number of edges in G with one end in A and one end in B.

3     Key technical lemma
In this section, we prove a key technical lemma about cycle decompositions of Eulerian graphs. Recall that
a cycle decomposition C of a (multi)graph G is a collection of cycles in G containing every edge of G exactly
once. As is well-known, a (multi)graph G admits a cycle decomposition if and only if all its vertices have
even degree. In the following, we will call connected multigraphs with this property Eulerian.
    In the remainder of this paper, given a collection C of cycles in a graph and some p ∈ [0, 1], we write Cp to
denote a random collection of cycles obtained by selecting every cycle of C with probability p independently
of all other cycles. With a slight abuse of notation, for a collection of cycles C in a graph G we will sometimes
also use C to refer to the subgraph of G formed by the union of all the cycles in C. In particular, V (C) will
refer to the vertex-set of said subgraph, and ∆(C) to its maximum degree.
    In the following, given a weight P function w : V (G) → Z≥0 on the vertices of a graph G and a subset
of vertices X, we will use w(X) := v∈X w(v) to denote the total weight of X. Similarly, we will use the
notation w(H) := w(V (H)) for the total weight of vertices in a subgraph H of G.
    The following is the aforementioned key technical lemma that we aim to prove in this section.

distinct endpoints both in V (Ω) and all internal vertices outside of V (Ω). A transaction of order k in (G, Ω)
is a set of k pairwise disjoint Ω-paths for which there exist two disjoint segments A, B of Ω such that each
of the k paths has one endpoint in A and the other in B.
    The following lemma was implicitly proved by Norin et al., which can be verified by inspecting the proof
of Claim 1 within the proof of Theorem 1 of the paper [22].

Lemma A.4. Let G be a connected graph, and let C be a cycle in G intersecting all longest paths. Let Ω
denote the society in G corresponding to the natural cyclic order on the cycle C in G. Let k ∈ N be the
largest integer such that (G, Ω) admits a transaction of order k. Then, we have
