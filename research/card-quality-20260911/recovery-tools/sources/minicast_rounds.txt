      Abstract. The reliable broadcast protocol with the best communication complexity for long messages
      to date is the MiniCast protocol of Locher & Shoup (2024). To reliably broadcast a message m to n
      parties, MiniCast has communication complexity ∼ 1.5|m|n when the size |m| of m is large. However, the
      round complexity of MiniCast is 4, which is worse than the 3 rounds of the classical protocol of Bracha.
      We give a new reliable broadcast protocol whose communication complexity is essentially the same as
      that of MiniCast, but whose round complexity is just 3. For large |m|, the communication complexity of
      our new protocol is essentially optimal (for 3-round protocols with somewhat balanced communication).
      Like MiniCast, our new protocol does not rely on any cryptography other than hash functions. We also
      give two new 2-round protocols that rely on signatures. The communication complexity of the first
      protocol is also ∼ 1.5|m|n, unless the sender provably misbehaves, in which case its communication
      complexity may degrade to ∼ 2|m|n. The communication complexity of the second protocol is ∼
      1.5|m|n, even in the worst case, but (unlike our other protocols) the communication is unbalanced.


1    Introduction

A reliable broadcast protocol allows a sender S to broadcast a single message m to parties
P1 , . . . , Pn (where S is one of these parties). We assume at most t of the n parties are corrupt,
where t < n/3, and that the parties are connected by authenticated point-to-point channels. The
communication network is completely asynchronous (i.e., there is no bound on the time it takes
to deliver messages) and the scheduling of message delivery is entirely under the control of an
adversary. Such a protocol should satisfy the following correctness property (informally stated):
    All honest parties that output a message must output the same message. Moreover, if the
    sender S is honest, that message is the one input by S.
It should also satisfy the following completeness property (informally stated):
    If any honest party outputs a message or an honest sender S inputs a message, then even-
    tually all honest parties output a message.
Here, “eventually” means if and when all protocol messages sent from honest parties to honest
parties have been delivered.
   The problem of reliable broadcast was first studied by Bracha [Bra87], who also gave a simple
protocol for it. Reliable broadcast has proved to be a fundamental building block in many other
protocols, too numerous to mention here.
    An important metric in evaluating any reliable broadcast protocol is its communication com-
plexity, which is defined as the total number of bits transmitted by all honest parties over the
network in the worst case. Bracha’s protocol has a communication complexity of O(|m|n2 ), where
|m| is the length (in bits) of the message m to be broadcast—essentially, every party echoes m to
every other party. For large |(|m), this communication complexity can be impractical.
    Another important metric is the round complexity of the protocol, which we define as the number
of rounds of communication required to complete the protocol when the sender is honest. Bracha’s
protocol has a round complexity of 3.
    For long messages, the protocol with the best communication complexity is the MiniCast pro-
tocol [LS25]. The MiniCast protocol has a communication complexity of

                                     1.5|m|n + O(κn2 log(n)),

where κ is the output length of a hash function. Note that the term O(κn2 log(n)) becomes irrelevant
when |m| is sufficiently large (when |m| ≫ κn log(n)). Indeed, the communication complexity is
∼ 1.5|m|n as |m| → ∞. Moreover, the communication of MiniCast is well balanced—it can be
implemented so that every party, including the sender, transmits (and receives) ∼ 1.5|m| bits. This
balance property can be important in asymmetric applications where just one party broadcasts in
each stage of the protocol (such as leader-based atomic broadcast protocols, like PBFT [CL99] and
its many descendants), and where communication bandwidth is limited. Unfortunately, the round
complexity of MiniCast is 4, which is worse than the round complexity of Bracha’s protocol.
    In this paper, we present a variation of MiniCast, called MiniCast∗ , which achieves essentially
the same communication complexity as MiniCast, but with a round complexity of just 3, matching
the round complexity of Bracha’s protocol. While the communication of MiniCast∗ is somewhat
balanced, it is not quite as balanced as that of MiniCast: the sender transmits ∼ 4.5|m| bits, while
every other party transmits (and receives) ∼ 1.5|m| bits. This modest imbalance may potentially
reduce the usefulness of MiniCast∗ in asymmetric applications. However, in symmetric applications
where all parties simultaneously broadcast in each stage of the protocol this imbalance has no
practical impact. Examples of such applications include DAG-based atomic broadcast protocols, like
DAG-rider [KKNS21] and Narwhal&Tusk [DKSS22], as well as other leaderless atomic broadcast
protocols, such as HoneyBadgerBFT [MXC+ 16].
    For large |m|, the communication complexity of MiniCast∗ is essentially optimal. Indeed, as
shown in [Loc24], any protocol with a round complexity of 3 and with somewhat balanced commu-
nication must have a communication complexity of at least ∼ 1.5|m|n.
    Protocol MiniCast∗ relies on no cryptography other than hash functions. Using signatures, it is
possible to design a reliable broadcast with a round complexity of just 2, as was shown in [ANRX21].
We give two such protocols.
    The first is an “optimistic” variation of MiniCast∗ , called OptMiniCast∗ . The communication
complexity of OptMiniCast∗ is also ∼ 1.5|m|n, unless the sender provably misbehaves, in which
case its communication complexity may degrade to ∼ 2|m|n. Moreover, the communication of
OptMiniCast∗ is also somewhat balanced: the sender transmits ∼ 6|m| bits, while every other
party transmits (and receives) ∼ 1.5|m| bits, unless the sender provably misbehaves, in which case
some parties may transmit ∼ 3|m| bits (and receive ∼ 2.5|m| bits).
    The second is an “unbalanced” version of the original MiniCast protocol, called UB-MiniCast.
Unlike OptMiniCast∗ , the UB-MiniCast protocol has a worst-case communication complexity of
∼ 1.5|m|n. Moreover, the communication of UB-MiniCast is completely unbalanced, with the sender

transmitting ∼ |m|n bits, while every other party transmits at most ∼ 0.5|m| bits. As mentioned
above, in symmetric applications, such an imbalance has no practical impact. In fact, for one
variant of UB-MiniCast, in the “happy case”, where the sender is honest, and there are no faults
(or just a few faults) among the other parties, and the network is well behaved, the protocol’s
communication complexity is ∼ |m|n, adding essentially no communication overhead to the trivial
(insecure) broadcast protocol in which the sender just sends its message to all parties. As we shall
discuss, despite the better worst-case communication complexity of UB-MiniCast, there are still
practical tradeoffs to be made in choosing between OptMiniCast∗ over UB-MiniCast.
    We stress that while all of the protocols we present here rely heavily on erasure codes, the use of
such codes should not, in practice, add significant overhead to the protocols. Indeed, based on re-
ported benchmarks [LS25], well-engineered implementations3 of modern algorithms [LAHC16,LC12]
can encode and decode at rates faster than typical network speeds.


1.1    Related work

As already mentioned, Bracha’s reliable broadcast protocol has a communication complexity of
O(|m|n2 ) and a round complexity of 3. Bracha’s protocol uses no cryptography. As was shown in
[ANRX21], using signatures, one can reduce the round complexity of Bracha’s protocol from 3 to
2, while maintaining the same communication complexity (as |m| → ∞).
    The first significant improvement to the communication complexity of reliable broadcast was
made by Cachin and Tessaro [CT05], who gave a protocol with communication complexity O(|m|n+
κn2 log(n)), where, again, κ is the output length of a hash function. Note that the security of their
protocol relies on the collision resistance of this hash function (unlike Bracha’s protocol, which
makes no other assumptions beyond authenticated channels). Their protocol can be implemented
so that it attains a (well-balanced) communication complexity of ∼ 3|m|n (as |m| → ∞) and a
round complexity of 3. See, for example, the discussion in [ADVZ22], as well as [SS24] for a detailed
description of this implementation, and Section 4.3 of [LS25] (or Section 4.1.2 of the full version)
for a simple “balancing technique” that makes it truly well balanced by reducing the amount of
data transmitted by the sender.
    As was shown in [CDH+ 22], using signatures along the same lines as in [ANRX21], one can
reduce the round complexity of Cachin and Tessaro’s protocol from 3 to 2, while maintaining the
same communication complexity (as |m| → ∞).
    Many other papers have been written on reducing the term O(κn2 log(n)) in the communication
complexity of Cachin and Tessaro’s protocol (see again [ADVZ22] for a discussion). However, this
line of research does not lead to any practical improvement for large |m|. Indeed, for large |m|, the
interesting question is to reduce the constant 3 in the term 3|m|n. This constant arises from the
erasure coding technique used in these protocols. The message m is encoded as a vector f1 , . . . , fn
of “fragments”, from any n − 2t of which it is possible to reconstruct m. The reason for the
reconstruction threshold of n − 2t is a bit technical. Roughly speaking, in these protocols, the
sender gives each party Pi its corresponding fragment fi . Upon receiving its fragment, Pi will
announce to all other parties that it is holding its fragment. Each party will wait for n − t such
announcements, but this only guarantees that n − 2t honest parties are holding fragments.
    If we could use the higher reconstruction threshold of n − t, we could hope to reduce the
communication complexity. However, using this higher threshold creates technical difficulties in

    Subsequent to [LS25], Alhaddad, Reyzin, and Varia [ARV24] apply the same fragment/mini-
fragment technique to the problem of AVID (asynchronous verifiable information dispersal). An
AVID protocol has two distinct stages: dispersal and retrieval. In the dispersal stage, the sender
S ensures that its message m is disseminated among the parties P1 , . . . , Pn in such a way that,
later, some other party R can reliably retrieve m. It is not required that S or R are among the
parties P1 , . . . , Pn or that each party Pi stores the complete message m. Of course, one can always
implement reliable broadcast using AVID, by just running the dispersal stage, and then having
each party Pi retrieve the message running a separate instance of the retrieval stage.
    The main goal of [ARV24] was to reduce the storage complexity of AVID protocols, and was
not focused per se on the communication or round complexity of reliable broadcast protocols.
While [ARV24] makes use of the same fragment/mini-fragment technique introduced in [LS25],
they employ the technique in a slightly different way. In [LS25], the sender disperses fragments to
all parties, and mini-fragments are only generated by parties holding the message so as to allow
other parties to recover their missing fragments as necessary. By contrast, in [ARV24], the sender
disperses mini-fragments directly, and the other parties reconstruct their fragments from these
mini-fragments.
    To be fair, our protocol MiniCast∗ can essentially be seen as an implementation (as discussed
above) of reliable broadcast using the AVID protocol in [ARV24], but with an optimization in which
the retrieval stage piggybacks on the last flow of the dispersal stage of the AVID protocol (to get the
stated round complexity). While this might be seen as a rather minor observation, we still believe it
is worthwhile to present MiniCast∗ (and OptMiniCast∗ ) in order to paint a more complete picture
of the reliable broadcast landscape. For MiniCast∗ (and OptMiniCast∗ ), the fact that the sender
is directly dispersing mini-fragments instead of fragments is what leads to the modest imbalance
in communication, as the mini-fragment encoding is inherently more redundant. By contrast, the
protocol UB-MiniCast has a very different structure than MiniCast∗ (and OptMiniCast∗ ).

1.2    Open questions
We raise two open questions. First, can we generalize the lower bound of [Loc24] so that it also
applies to protocols that are not necessarily balanced in any way? Even a lower bound of this
type that applies only to protocols with round complexity 2 would be interesting. Second, is there
a reliable broadcast protocol with round complexity 3, where each party, including the sender,
transmits ∼ 1.5|m| bits as |m| → ∞?

2.2   Merkle trees
As in [LS25], our reliable broadcast protocol will also make use of Merkle trees. Recall that a Merkle
tree allows one party P to commit to a vector of values (v1 , . . . , vk ) using a collision-resistant hash
function by building a (full) binary tree whose leaves are the hashes of v1 , . . . , vk , and where each
internal node of the tree is the hash of its two children. The root r of the tree is the commitment.
Party P may “open” the commitment at a position i ∈ [k] by revealing vi along with a “validation
path” πi , which consists of the siblings of all nodes along the path in the tree from the hash of vi to
the root r. We call πi a validation path for vi under r at position i. Such a validation path is
checked by recomputing the nodes along the corresponding path in the tree, and verifying that the
recomputed root is equal to the given commitment r. The collision resistance of the hash function
ensures that P cannot open the commitment to two different values at a given position.
    As in [LS25], we shall actually make use of a two-level Merkle tree structure. For a given message
m, the top level Merkle tree will be rooted at r and have leaves r1 , . . . , rn , where each ri is itself
the root of a Merkle tree whose leaves are the hashes of mini-fragments ϕi1 , . . . , ϕin of the fragment
fi of m. We will therefore have validation paths πi for ri under r at position i, as well as validation
paths πij for ϕij under ri at position j.
    Each validation path πi and πij will have size O(κ log(n)), where κ is the output length of the
hash function.

 – fi has the correct length of a fragment for a message of length ℓ, and
 – when we compute the mini-fragments ϕi1 , . . . , ϕin for fi and build the Merkle tree rooted at ri

3   The main protocol

Our new reliable broadcast protocol, which we call ΠMiniCast∗ , is presented in Fig. 1. We express the
logic for the sender as a separate process, even though the sending party is also one of the receiving
parties. For each receiving party, the main logic is presented as a loop, and in each loop iteration
the party waits for one of several conditions to hold, triggering a reaction. These conditions are
based on local state plus an implicit pool of received messages.
    This protocol has the same basic structure as Bracha’s original broadcast protocol [Bra87], with
three rounds, which we call disperse, echo, and vote. The main difference between this and Bracha’s
protocol is that here, data is disseminated as outlined in Section 2.4. At a high level, the protocol
proceeds as follows:
1. In the disperse round, the sender sends to each party Pi the tag τ along with the collection of
    certified mini-fragments {(ϕji , πji , πj )}nj=1 .
2. In the echo round, each party Pi “echoes τ ” by broadcasting the message (echo, τ ), and sends
    to each party Pj the certified mini-fragment (ϕji , πji , πj ).
3. In the vote round, each Pi will “vote for τ ” by broadcasting the message (vote, τ ). It does so
    only after two conditions hold:
   (a) it has received either n − t echoes of τ or t + 1 votes for τ , and
  (b) it has received a set of n − 2t certified mini-fragments (ϕij , πij , πi ) for τ , which allows it to
        reconstruct its fragment fi for τ .
    In addition to voting for τ , it also broadcasts its certified fragment (fi , πi ).
4. Upon receiving n−t votes for τ , as well as n−t certified fragments for τ , each party reconstructs
    a message m from these fragments and then outputs m.

Theorem 3.1. Assuming collision resistance of the underlying hash functions, Protocol ΠMiniCast∗
satisfies the correctness and completeness property (with overwhelming probability, for any
polynomial-time adversary).

Proof. We first deal with the case where the sender is corrupt. In this case, we need to prove the
following: if any honest party outputs a message, then all honest parties eventually output the same
message.
    In proving this, we first recall a basic property of Bracha’s protocol, which applies equally well
here: if any honest party votes for a tag τ , then
  (i) all honest parties vote for the same tag τ , and
 (ii) at least n − t − t′ honest parties must have echoed τ , where t′ is the number of corrupt parties.
Note that (i) follows directly from (ii).

party received n − t votes for τ , it must be the case that n − 2t ≥ t + 1 honest parties already
voted for τ . Therefore, all honest parties will eventually receive t + 1 votes for some tag, and by
property (i) above, this tag must be τ . Moreover, by property (ii) above, each honest party will
eventually receive the n − 2t certified mini-fragments for τ it needs to obtain its fragment for τ .
Therefore, each honest party Pi will eventually vote for τ — collision resistance ensures that when
Pi executes MiniDecode, it will recover the same fragment fi that P constructed when it performed

The message complexity of ΠMiniCast∗ is obviously O(n2 ).
   As sketched in Section 2, the communication complexity of ΠMiniCast∗ is at most
                                     1.5ℓn + 9ℓ + O(κn2 log(n)),                                     (3)

4     An optimistic 2-round variant
We now present an “optimistic” version of our protocol ΠMiniCast∗ that has better round
complexity—just 2 rounds in the “good case” where the sender is honest, instead of 3. This protocol
is based on the reliable broadcast protocol in [ANRX21], which makes use of threshold signatures.
When the sender is honest, its communication complexity is ∼ 1.5|m|n as |m| → ∞, but this may
increase to ∼ 2|m|n if the sender provably misbehaves.
    The protocol makes use of an (n − t)-out-of-n threshold signature scheme. For such a scheme,
we refer to a signature share and a signature certificate: signature shares from n − t distinct parties
on a given message may be combined to form a signature certificate on that message. This can
be implemented as just a set of ordinary signatures, or as an aggregate signature scheme (such
as one based on BLS signatures [BLS01] as in [BDN18]) or as a threshold version of an ordinary
signature scheme (such as one again based on BLS signatures as in [Bol03]). The second and third
implementations will result in much more compact threshold signatures. The third implementation
requires a set-up phase to distribute shares of a signing key.
    The security property for such a threshold signature scheme may be stated as follows.

                                                   11
Quorum Size Property: It is infeasible to produce a signature certificate on a message m, unless
  n − t − t′ honest parties have issued signature shares on m, where t′ ≤ t is the number of corrupt
  parties.
    Under our assumption that the number of corrupt parties is strictly less than n/3, one can
easily establish the following standard property.
Quorum Intersection Property: It is infeasible to produce signature certificates on two distinct
  messages m and m′ , unless at least one honest party issued signature shares on both m and m′ .
   Our optimistic reliable broadcast protocol, which we call ΠOptMiniCast∗ , is presented in Fig. 2.
The protocol collapses the round structure of ΠMiniCast∗ to just two rounds: disperse and echo. The
main ideas of the protocol are as follows:
1. In the disperse round, the sender includes the fragment fi in the message it sends to Pi , along
   with all the other information sent in ΠMiniCast∗ .
2. In the echo round, in addition to all the other information sent in ΠMiniCast∗ , each party Pi
   broadcasts its fragment fi as well as a signature share σi on a message consisting of the tag
   τ as well as a protocol instance ID. For brevity, in the protocol description, we refer to this
   simply as a signature share on τ (and similarly, a signature certificate on τ is really a signature
   certificate on a message consisting of τ and a protocol instance ID). Note that broadcasting fi
   at this stage is rather optimistic, as we have not yet reached agreement on a tag, and so this
   may well be the “wrong” fragment.
3. If a party receives n−t signature shares or a signature certificate on a tag τ ∗ , then τ ∗ is considered
   certified. In either case, the party broadcasts a signature certificate on τ ∗ (computing a signature
   certificate from signature shares, if necessary). By the Quorum Intersection Property, all honest
   parties will agree on the certified tag τ ∗ . Moreover, by the Quorum Size Property, if any honest
   party sees a certified tag τ ∗ , we can be sure that at least n − 2t honest parties have sent out
   corresponding mini-fragments to all parties, which ensures that all honest parties can eventually
   recover their own fragment for τ ∗ even if they do not receive one from the sender.
4. If a party Pi obtains its fragment fi for τ ∗ through the above recovery mechanism, it will
   broadcast fi . Of course, Pi takes care not to broadcast the same fragment twice. However, it
   could broadcast two fragments in total—a fragment for the “wrong” tag in the echo round and
   a fragment for the “right” tag in the recovery mechanism. This can only happen if the sender
   “equivocates”—sending different tags to different honest parties in the disperse round.

                                 2ℓn + 10.5ℓ + O(κn2 log(n) + λn2 ).

on message length. As mentioned before, such a bound may be enforced by honest parties.

sending different tags to different honest parties in the disperse round. One can limit the impact of
this in two ways:

1. The party that broadcasts two fragments will detect that the sender is corrupt, and can ignore
   all messages from that sender in future executions of the protocol.

2. If we modify the protocol so that the sender must include a signature on the tag (and protocol
   instance ID) in the disperse round, and this signature is passed along in the echo round, then
   any such equivocation will eventually result in all honest parties obtaining an “equivocation
   proof”, consisting of signatures on conflicting tags. Such an equivocation proof may be used to
   take punitive action against the sender, which presumably makes any such equivocation unlikely
   to happen in the first place.


5   An unbalanced 2-round variant

The protocols we have presented so far are somewhat balanced. In this section, we consider a
completely unbalanced protocol, in which most of the data is transmitted by the sender. As already
mentioned in the introduction, in symmetric applications where all parties simultaneously broadcast
in each stage of the protocol, such imbalance has no practical impact. Like protocol ΠOptMiniCast∗ ,
the protocol we present here relies on threshold signatures. It also has a round complexity of just
2 in the “good case” where the sender is honest. However, unlike ΠOptMiniCast∗ , its communication
complexity in the worst case is just ∼ 1.5|m|n as |m| → ∞. That said, we do not necessarily
recommend it over ΠOptMiniCast∗ in all situations, for reasons we discuss below.
    Like protocol ΠOptMiniCast∗ , we need an (n − t)-out-of n threshold signature scheme. However,
to achieve the desired worst-case bound on communication complexity, we need an accountable
threshold signature scheme [BPR24]. Roughly speaking, this means that the set of signatories can
be computed from a signature. We write this computation as J ← Signatories(σ). An essential
security property is that if σ is a valid signature on a message and j ∈ J is the index of an honest
party Pj , then Pj must have issued a signature share on the signed message.
    We can implement accountable threshold signatures as either a set of signatures or as an aggre-
gate signature scheme. Such implementations may lead to signature certificates of size linear in n.
For the case of BLS-based aggregate signatures, we need just n bits to identify the set of signatories.
    Our unbalanced 2-round reliable broadcast protocol, which we call ΠUB-MiniCast , is presented in
Fig. 3. The main ideas of the protocol are as follows.

1. In the disperse round, given a message m, the sender sends each party the message (disperse, m).
2. In the echo round, upon receiving (disperse, m) from the sender, each party Pi computes

5.2   Some practical concerns

As mentioned above, we do not necessarily recommend ΠUB-MiniCast over ΠOptMiniCast∗ in all situ-
ations.
    A first concern is that ΠUB-MiniCast is quite imbalanced. As already mentioned, in symmetric
applications, this imbalance is not a problem, but in asymmetric applications, it may be entirely
unacceptable.

                                                   17
    A second concern is that the “trick” we used to achieve the desired worst-case complexity
bound actually introduces another type of imbalance, which may be problematic, even in symmetric
applications. Each party sends its fragment to less than n/3 parties. This ensures that each party
(other than S) transmits ∼ 12 ℓ bits. However, there may well be parties that end up downloading
fragments from all parties. For such “unlucky” parties, the total number of bits that they download
can be as high as ∼ 2.5ℓ. Worse, this can happen even when all parties, even S, are honest. Even
in symmetric applications, a party could be “unlucky” in all instances of the reliable broadcast
protocol, and potentially saturate its download bandwidth. (The same issue arises in the protocols
in [Loc24], which use the same “trick”).
    One possible mitigation to this problem is to use an “on demand” strategy in which each party
explicitly requests fragments from their peers. In addition, each party can choose to wait a certain
amount of time after seeing a certified tag before making any requests, and only if it has not
received the corresponding message directly from S by that time. (This idea was used in a similar
setting by [Loc24].) This adds extra latency in the worst case, but not in the common case. The
nice thing about this approach is that in the “happy case”, when S is honest and the network
is well behaved, no honest party requests any fragments, and no fragments are sent by honest
parties except to corrupt parties who request them just to waste bandwidth. In this case, if t∗ ≤ t
is the actual number of corrupt parties, the total communication complexity is ∼ ℓ(n + 1.5t∗ ).
In particular, if there are no faults (other than crash failures) and the network is well behaved,
the total communication complexity is ∼ ℓn, and the protocol adds essentially no communication
overhead to the trivial (insecure) broadcast protocol in which S just sends its message to all parties.
    The above “on demand” strategy is reminiscent of what is often done in practice in many
consensus protocols (for example, [DKSS22]), where some form of simple “consistent broadcast”
protocol is used, which does not guarantee completeness. To get around this lack of completeness,
parties may request a missing message from other parties. However, in the worst case, this simple
strategy can lead to communication complexity ≫ ℓn2 , since all corrupt parties may request mes-
sages from all honest parties. Using our more refined approach, the total communication complexity
is at most ∼ 1.5ℓn, even in the worst case.
    A third concern is that the protocol requires an accountable threshold signature scheme, which
may result in larger signature certificates. We have already noted that if we use BLS-based aggregate
signatures, the additional overhead is just n bits, which for values of n up to several hundred is not
a practical concern. However, as all parties broadcast these signature certificates, a term O(n3 ) is
added to the communication complexity, resulting in a worse (theoretical) bound with respect to
n.
    One could also consider a variant of ΠUB-MiniCast in which we do not use an accountable threshold
signature scheme, and each party sends its fragment to all other parties. For this variant, the worst-
case communication complexity is ∼ 2.5ℓn. However, when combined with the above “on demand”
strategy, the communication complexity in the “happy case” is exactly the same as in the version
that uses an accountable scheme.
