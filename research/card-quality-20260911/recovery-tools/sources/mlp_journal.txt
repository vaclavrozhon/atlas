     We introduce the notion of monotone linear programming circuits (MLP circuits), a model of computation for partial Boolean functions.
8
     Using this model, we prove the following results1 .
9
10       (1) MLP circuits are superpolynomially stronger than monotone Boolean circuits.
11       (2) MLP circuits are exponentially stronger than monotone span programs over the reals.
12       (3) MLP circuits can be used to provide monotone feasibility interpolation theorems for Lovász-Schrijver proof systems and for
13
             mixed Lovász-Schrijver proof systems.
14
         (4) The Lovász-Schrijver proof system cannot be polynomially simulated by the cutting planes proof system.
15
16       Finally, we establish connections between the problem of proving lower bounds for the size of MLP circuits and the field of extension
17   complexity of polytopes.

     1    INTRODUCTION
27
28   Superpolynomial lower bounds on the size of Boolean circuits computing explicit Boolean functions have only been
29
     proved for circuits from some specific families of circuits. A prominent role among these families is played by monotone
30
     Boolean circuits. Exponential lower bounds for monotone Boolean circuits were proved already in 1985 by Razborov [30].
31
32   In 1995 Krajíček showed that lower bounds on the monotone complexity of particular partial Boolean functions can be
33   used to prove lower bounds for Resolution, and for some other proof systems such as cutting-planes with bounded
34
     coefficients; these results were published in [21]. A similar idea appeared in the same year in a preprint of Bonet et
35
     al. which was later published as [4].2 Incidentally, the functions used in Razborov’s lower bound were just of the
36
37   form needed for resolution lower bounds. Exponential lower bounds on resolution proofs had been proved before
38   (coincidentally about at the same time as Razborov’s lower bounds). However, Krajíček came up with a new general
39
     method, the so called feasible interpolation, that potentially could be used for other proof systems. Indeed, soon after his

62        The results listed above suggest that proving lower bounds on stronger and stronger models of monotone computation
63    may be a promising approach towards proving lower bounds on stronger proof systems. Indeed, in his survey article
64

67        In this work we introduce several computational models based on the notion of monotone linear program. In particular,
68    we introduce the notion of monotone linear programming gate (MLP gate). In its most basic form, an MLP gate is a
69
      partial function ℓ : Rn → R ∪ {∗} of the form ℓ(y) = max{c · x | Ax ≤ b + By, x ≥ 0} where y is a string of n input
70
71
      real variables, and B is a nonnegative matrix. The complexity of such a gate is defined as the number of rows plus the
72    number of columns in the matrix A. For each assignment α ∈ Rn of the variables y the value ℓ(α) is the optimal value of
73    the linear program with objective function c · x, and constraints Ax ≤ b + Bα. The requirement that B ≥ 0 guarantees
74
      monotonicity, i.e., that ℓ(α) ≤ ℓ(α ′ ) whenever ℓ(α) and ℓ(α ′ ) are defined and α ≤ α ′ . We note that the value ℓ(α) is
75
76
      considered to be undefined if the associated linear program max{c · x | Ax ≤ b + Bα } has no solution. In this case, we
77    set ℓ(α) = ∗. Other variants of MLP gates are defined in a similar way by allowing the input variables to occur in the
78    objective function, and by allowing the corresponding linear programs to be minimizing or maximizing. We say that an
79
      MLP gate is weak if the input variables occur either only in the objective function or only in the constraints. We say
80
81
      that an MLP gate is strong if the input variables occur both in the objective function and in the constraints.
82        MLP circuits are the straightforward generalization of unbounded-fan-in monotone Boolean circuits where gates are
83    MLP gates, instead of Boolean gates. In Theorem 4.3 we show that if all gates of an MLP circuit C are weak, then this
84
      circuit can be simulated by a single weak MLP gate ℓC whose size is polynomial on the size of C. Since the AND and
85
86
      OR gates can be faithfully simulated by weak MLP gates, we have that monotone Boolean circuits can be polynomially
87    simulated by weak MLP gates (Theorem 5.1). In contrast, we show that weak MLP gates are superpolynomially stronger
88    than monotone Boolean circuits. On the one hand, Razborov has shown that any monotone Boolean circuit computing
      the bipartite perfect matching function BPMn : {0, 1}n → {0, 1} must have size at least n Ω(log n) [29]. On the other

92    by weak MLP gates of polynomial size.

99
      polynomially simulate the other. In Theorem 5.4 we show that a particular type of weak MLP gate can polynomially

      simulate monotone span programs over the reals. On the other hand, by combining the results in [33] with Theorem 5.4,

109       Next we turn to the problem of proving a monotone interpolation theorem for Lovász-Schrijver proof systems [23].
110   Currently, size lower bounds for these systems have been proved only with respect to tree-like proofs [24], and to static
111
      proofs [15, 20]. Therefore, it seems reasonable that a monotone interpolation theorem for this system may be a first
112
113
      step towards proving size lower bounds for general LS proof systems. Towards this goal we show that MLP circuits
114   which are constituted by strong MLP gates can be used to provide a monotone feasible interpolation theorem for LS
115   proof systems. In other words, we reduce the problem of proving superpolynomial lower bounds for the size of LS
116
      proofs, to the problem of proving lower bounds on the size of MLP circuits with strong gates.
117
118
          It is worth noting that we do not know how to collapse MLP circuits with strong gates into a single strong gate.
119   Nevertheless, in Theorem 6.2 we show that a single weak MLP gate suffices in a monotone interpolation theorem
120   for LS proofs of unsatisfiable sets of mixed inequalities of a certain form. Here, a mixed inequality is an inequality
121
      which involves both Boolean variables and real variables. Using this interpolation theorem together with a size lower
122
123
      bound for monotone real circuits due to Fu [10], we can show that MLP-circuits cannot be polynomially simulated by
124   monotone real circuits (Corollary 6.11).
125       We show that the cutting-planes proof system cannot polynomially simulate the LS proof system (Corollary 6.9).
126
      Understanding the mutual relation between the power of the cutting-planes proof system and the LS proof system is
127
128
      a longstanding open problem in proof complexity theory. Our result solves one direction of this mutual relation by
129   showing that for some unsatisfiable set of inequalities, LS proofs can be superpolynomially more concise than cutting-
130   planes proofs. Concerning the other direction, Pitassi and Segerlind have shown that tree-like LS does not polynomially
131
      simulate cutting-planes [24]. The problem whether the LS proof system with DAG-like proofs can polynomially simulate
132
133
      the cutting-planes proof system remains open.
134       Monotone linear programs may be regarded as a simultaneous generalization of monotone Boolean circuits and
135   monotone span programs. Nevertheless, currently there is no lower bound technique that can be used to prove lower
136
      bounds both for the size of monotone Boolean circuits and for the size of monotone span programs. Therefore, proving
137
138
      lower bounds for the size of MLP circuits will likely require the development of substantially new techniques. A possible
139   approach is to strengthen lower bound techniques for the size of extended formulations of explicit polytopes. A lower
140   bound on extended formulations is a lower bound on the number of inequalities needed to define an extension of a
141
      polytope to some higher dimension. Such lower bounds have been proved, in particular, for polytopes spanned by the
142
143
      0-1 vectors representing minterms of certain monotone Boolean functions [5, 6, 9, 34]. Nevertheless, to prove a lower
144   bound on the size of weak MLP gates, it will be necessary to prove lower bounds on the size of extended formulations
145   for all polytopes of a certain form that separate minterms from maxterms. This seems to be a much harder problem than
146
      proving a lower bound for a given polytope, but there are results on extended formulations that go in this direction [5, 6].
147
148
      However, Theorem 6.10 suggests that this will surely not be easy. It gives an example of a monotone function where
149   the convex-hull of the set of ones requires exponentially large extended formulation, but where the set of ones can be
150   separated from a large set of maxterms by a weak MLP representation of polynomial size.

      In this section we define the notion of monotone linear programming gate, or breifly MLP gate.
183
184       Definition 3.1 (MLP Gate). Let A be a matrix in Rm×k , b be a vector in Rm , c be a vector in Rk , and B and
185   C be matrices in Rm×n with B ≥ 0 and C ≥ 0. An MLP gate is a partial function ℓ : Rn → R ∪ {∗} whose value at
      each point y ∈ Rn is specified by a monotone linear program. More precisely, we consider the following six types of MLP gates.
186
187
188
189
           max-right:        ℓ(y) = max{c · x | Ax ≤ b + By, x ≥ 0}
190
191        min-right:        ℓ(y) = min{c · x | Ax ≥ b + By, x ≥ 0}
192
193
194
           max-left:         ℓ(y) = max{(c + Cy) · x | Ax ≤ b, x ≥ 0}
195
196        min-left:         ℓ(y) = min{(c + Cy) · x | Ax ≥ b, x ≥ 0}
197
198
199
           max:              ℓ(y) = max{(c + Cy) · x | Ax ≤ b + By, x ≥ 0}
200
201        min:              ℓ(y) = min{(c + Cy) · x | Ax ≥ b + By, x ≥ 0}
202
203       Intuitively, the variables y should be regarded as input variables, while the variables x should be regarded as internal
      variables. If the linear program specifying a gate ℓ(y) has no solution when setting y to a particular point α ∈ Rn , then
204
205
206   we set ℓ(α) = ∗. In other words, in this case we regard the value ℓ(α) as being undefined. We note that the requirement
207   that B ≥ 0 and C ≥ 0 guarantees that the gates introduced above are monotone. More precisely, if α ≤ α ′ , and both
208   Manuscript submitted to ACM
      Representations of Monotone Boolean Functions by Linear Programs                                                                  5

209   ℓ(α) and ℓ(α ′ ) are well defined, then ℓ(α) ≤ ℓ(α ′ ). The size |ℓ| of an MLP gate ℓ is defined as the number of rows plus
210
      the number of columns in the matrix A.
211
212
         The gates of type max-right, max-left, min-right and min-left are called weak gates. Note that in these gates, the
213   input variables y occur either only in the objective function, or only in the constraints. The gates of type max and min
214   are called strong gates. The input variables in strong gates occur both in the constraints and in the objective function.
215
216      Definition 3.2 (MLP-Gate Representation). We say that an MLP gate ℓ : Rn → R ∪ {∗} represents a partial Boolean
217
      function F : {0, 1}n → {0, 1, ∗} if the following holds true for each a ∈ {0, 1}n .
218
219      (1) ℓ(a) > 0 if F (a) = 1,
220      (2) ℓ(a) ≤ 0 if F (a) = 0.
221
222   3.1   Sign Representations
223
224   We say that an MLP gate ℓ sign-represents a partial Boolean function F : {0, 1}n → {0, 1, ∗} if the following conditions
225   can be verified for each a ∈ {0, 1}n .
226
         (1) ℓ(a) > 0 if F (a) = 1.
227
228      (2) ℓ(a) < 0 if F (a) = 0.
229
230      Proposition 3.3. Let F : {0, 1}n → {0, 1, ∗} be a partial Boolean function and assume that F can be represented by an
231   MLP gate of type τ and size s. Then F can be sign-represented by an MLP gate of type τ and size O(s).
232
233      We leave the proof to the reader as an easy exercise.

236
237

238   constraints. On the other hand, strong MLP gates are gates where input variables are allowed to occur both in the
239   objective function and in the constraints.
240

241

      Monotone linear programming circuits (MLP circuits) may be defined as the straightforward generalization of unbounded
332
333
      fan-in monotone Boolean circuits where monotone linear programming gates are used instead of Boolean gates. Formally,
334   it will be convenient for us to define MLP circuits using the notation of straight-line programs, i.e., as a sequence of
335   instructions of a suitable form.
336
337       Definition 4.1 (MLP Circuit). An MLP circuit is a sequence of instructions C = (I 1, I 2, ..., Ir ) where each instruction
338
      Ii has one of the following forms:
339
340       (1) Ii ≡ Input(yi ), where yi is a variable.
341       (2) Ii ≡ yi ← c i , where yi is a variable and c i ∈ R.
342
          (3) Ii ≡ yi ← ℓi (yi 1 , ..., yi ni ) where yi is a variable and ℓi (yi 1 , ..., yi ni ) is an MLP gate with input variables yi 1 , ..., yi ni
343
              such that i j < i for each j ∈ [ni ].
344

          We say that instructions of the third form are MLP instructions. We assume that the last instruction, Ir , is an MLP
345
346
347   instruction. We say that the variable yr , which occurs in the left-hand side of Ir is the output variable of C. For each i
348   such that Ii ≡ Input(yi ), we say that yi is an input variable.

      y jl = al for each l ∈ {1, ..., n}. For each i ∈ {1, ..., r }, the value induced by a on variable yi , which is denoted by val a (yi ),

707   6.2     Feasible Interpolation
708
709
      Feasible interpolation is a method that can sometimes be used to translate circuit lower bounds into lower bounds for
710   the size of refutations of Boolean formulas and linear inequalities. Let Ψ(p, q, r ) be an unsatisfiable Boolean formula
711   which is a conjunction of formulas Φ(p, q) and Γ(p, r ) where q and r are disjoint sets of variables. Since Ψ(p, q, r ) is
712
      unsatisfiable, it must be the case that for each assignment a of the variables p, either Φ(a, q) or Γ(a, r ) is unsatisfiable,
713
714
      or both. Given a proof Π of unsatisfiability for Ψ(p, q, r ), an interpolant is a Boolean circuit C(p) such that for every
715   assignment a to the variables p,
716
           (1) if C(a) = 1, then Φ(a, q) is unsatisfiable,
717
718        (2) if C(a) = 0, then Γ(a, r ) is unsatisfiable.
719
           If both formulas are unsatisfiable, then C(a) can be either of the two values. Krajíček has shown that given a resolution
720
721   refutation Π of a CNF formula, one can construct an interpolant C(p) whose size is polynomial in the size of Π [21].
722   Krajíček’s interpolation theorem has been generalized, by himself and some other authors, to other proof systems such
723
      as the cutting-planes proof system and the Lovász-Schrijver proof system [25, 26].
724
           In principle, such feasible interpolation theorems could be used to prove lower bounds on the size of proofs if we could
725
726   prove lower bounds on circuits computing some particular functions. But since we are not able to prove essentially
727   any lower bounds on general Boolean circuits, feasible interpolation gives us only conditional lower bounds. For
728   Manuscript submitted to ACM
      Representations of Monotone Boolean Functions by Linear Programs                                                                                      15

729   instance, the assumption that P , NP ∩ coNP, an apparently weaker assumption than NP , coNP, implies that certain
730
      tautologies require superpolynomial-size proofs on systems that admit feasible interpolation.
731
732
         However, in some cases, one can show that there exist monotone interpolating circuits (of some kind) of polynomial
733   size (in the size of the proof) provided that all variables p appear negatively in Φ(p, q), (or positively in Γ(p, r )). In
734   the case of resolution proofs, the interpolating circuits are simply monotone Boolean circuits [21, 22]. In the case of
735
      cutting-planes proofs, the interpolants are monotone real circuits [25]. Monotone real circuits are circuits with Boolean
736
737
      inputs and outputs, but whose gates are allowed to be arbitrary 2-input functions over the reals. Razborov’s lower
738   bound on the clique function has been generalized to monotone real circuits [17, 25]. Another proof system for which
739   one can prove lower bounds (although only on the degree of refutations) using monotone feasible interpolation is the
740
      Nullstellensatz Proof System [27]. In this proof system, the monotone interpolants are given in terms of monotone span
741
742
      programs4 [27].
743      The results mentioned above suggest that if a proof system has the feasible interpolation property, then it may also
744   have monotone feasible interpolation property for a suitable kind of monotone computation. We will show that the
745
      Lovász-Schrijver proof system has the monotone feasible interpolation property with the interpolants computed by
746
747
      MLP circuits with strong gates.

1344
1345   In this section we establish connections between monotone linear programs and the theory of extended formulations
1346   for polytopes. In particular, we define the notion of monotone extension complexity of a polytope and show that this
1347
       complexity measure can be used to characterize the size of weak monotone representations of monotone Boolean
1348
1349
       functions. Since such representations can be used to interpolate mixed Lovász-Schrijver proofs, we may regard the task
1350   of proving superpolynomial lower bounds on the monotone extension complexity of polytopes as a first step towards
1351   proving lower bounds for the size of mixed Lovász-Schrijver proofs.
1352   Manuscript submitted to ACM
       Representations of Monotone Boolean Functions by Linear Programs                                                                 27

1353      A polytope is the convex hull of a nonempty finite set of vectors in Rn ; in particular, a polytope is nonempty and
1354
       bounded. If a polytope P ⊆ Rn is given by a polynomial number of inequalities8 , then we can easily decide whether a
       vector v ∈ Rn belongs to P. An important observation is that even if P requires an exponential number of inequalities
1355
1356
1357   to be defined, we may still be able to test whether v ∈ P efficiently if we can find a polytope P ′ ⊆ Rn+m in a higher
1358   dimension with m = nO (1) such that P is a projection of P ′ and P ′ can be described by a polynomial number of
1359
       inequalities8 . More precisely, let P ⊆ Rn be a polytope, and let P ′ ⊆ Rn+m be a polytope defined by a system of
       inequalities9 A(v, y) ≤ b. Then we say that the system A(v, y) ≤ b is an extended formulation of P if for each v ∈ Rn ,
1360
1361
1362   v ∈ P ⇔ ∃y ∈ Rm , A(v, y) ≤ b. We define the size of such extended formulation as the number of rows plus the
1363   number of columns in A. For instance, it can be shown that the permutahedron polytope Pn ⊆ Rn , which is defined as
1364
       the convex-hull of all permutations of the set [n] = {1, ..., n}, requires exponentially many inequalities to be defined.
1365
1366
       Nevertheless, Pn has extended formulations of size O(n log n) [13]. On the other hand, it has been shown that for some
1367   polytopes, such as the cut polytope, the TSP polytope, etc., even extended formulations require exponentially many
1368   inequalities [9, 34].

1408
1409   The process of defining partial Boolean functions by linear programs is closely related, but not equivalent, to the process
1410
       of defining polytopes by extended formulations. For a partial Boolean function F , let Ones(F ), and Zeros(F ) denote the

1411
1412
1413   P F0 denote the convex hull of Zeros(F ). Defining F by a linear program is equivalent to finding an extended formulation
1414   of some polyhedron Q 1 that contains P F1 and is disjoint from Zeros(F ), or an extended formulation of some polyhedron
1415
       Q 0 that contains P F0 and is disjoint from Ones(F ). Finding such an extended formulation for such a polyhedron Q 1
1416
       (resp. Q 0 ) with a small number of inequalities is a simpler task than finding a small extended formulation for the
1417
1418   polyhedron P F1 (resp. P F0 ) itself. For instance, if F is the matching function for general graphs, then F is computable by a
1419   polynomial-size Boolean circuit (containing negation gates), and hence this function can be defined by (not necessarily
1420
       monotone) linear programs of polynomial size10 . Nevertheless, the corresponding polytope P F1 requires extended
1421
       formulations of exponential size [34].

       8     CONCLUSION
1526   In this work we have introduced several models of computation based on the notion of monotone linear programs. In
1527
       particular, we introduced the notions of weak and strong MLP gates. We reduced the problem of proving lower bounds
1528
1529   for the size of LS proofs to the problem of proving lower bounds for the size of MLP circuits with strong gates, and the
1530   problem of proving lower bounds on the size of mixed LS proofs to the problem of proving lower bounds on the size of
1531   single weak MLP gates.
1532
            When it comes to comparing MLP gates with other models of computation, we have shown that weak MLP gates are
1533
1534   strictly more powerful than monotone Boolean circuits and monotone span programs. Additionally, these gates cannot
1535   be polynomially simulated by monotone real circuits. Finally, by combining some results mentioned above, we proved
1536   that the cutting-planes proof system is not powerful enough to polynomially simulate the LS proof system. This is the
1537
       first result showing a separation between the power of these two systems.
1538
1539        The results mentioned above indicate that the study of monotone models of computation based on linear programming
1540   has the potential to shed new light on deep questions in circuit complexity and in proof complexity. We note however,
1541   that when proposing a new model of monotone computation, there is always a danger that the model is too strong. So
1542
       strong that proving size lower bounds on this model for explicit Boolean functions would imply a major breakthrough in
1543
1544   computational complexity. For instance, a nondeterministic monotone circuit for a Boolean function F (p) is a monotone
1545   circuit C(p, q, r ), where q and r are strings of variables of equal length such that
1546
1547                                                  F (p) = 1 ⇔ ∃q C(p, q, ¬q) = 1.
1548
       Note that this is a fully syntactic definition—the form of the circuit ensures that the function it computes is monotone.
1549
1550   Yet this kind of circuits are equivalent to general nondeterministic circuits.
1551        Nevertheless, we conjecture that the models we have introduced in this work do not suffer from this excess of
1552
       computational power.
1553
            We conclude this work by stating some open problems whose solution could lead to the development of more
1554
1555   powerful techniques for proving explicit size lower bounds for monotone models of computation and proof systems.
1556
            (1) Prove superpolynomial lower bounds for the size of weak MLP gates representing an explicit partial function F .
1557
1558        (2) Since proving superpolynomial lower bounds on the size of MLP circuits seems extremely difficult, the tantalizing
1559            question is: Is it possible to interpolate Lovász-Schrijver refutations by a single monotone LP gate (similarly as it
1560   Manuscript submitted to ACM
       Representations of Monotone Boolean Functions by Linear Programs                                                                                         31

1561           is in Theorem 6.2 for a set of mixed inequalities)? We believe that it should be possible to improve Theorem 6.1,
1562
               because, for instance, our proof does not use the property that quadratic terms with variables p must cancel.
1563
1564
          (3) Is it possible to bound the coefficients occurring in MLP gates without increasing too much the size of represen-
1565           tations? More specifically, given an MLP gate ℓ of polynomial size representing a function F , can one modify it
1566           in such a way that all coefficients in the inequalities and objective function defining ℓ are integers of polynomial
1567
               magnitude? Note that a similar question is open in the context of monotone span programs.
