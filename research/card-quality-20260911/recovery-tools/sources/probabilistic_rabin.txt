                                                                                         Abstract
                                                       The Rabin tree theorem yields an algorithm to solve the satisfiability problem for
                                                   monadic second-order logic over infinite trees. Here we solve the probabilistic variant
                                                   of this problem. Namely, we show how to compute the probability that a randomly
                                                   chosen tree satisfies a given formula. We additionally show that this probability is
                                                   an algebraic number. This closes a line of research where similar results were shown
                                                   for formalisms weaker than the full monadic second-order logic.


                                         1         Introduction
                                         The Rabin tree theorem had an essential impact on formal methods in computer science,
                                         thanks to both the large applicability of the result and the strength of techniques intro-
                                         duced in the proof [Rab69]. Basically, it reduces the satisfiability problem for monadic
                                         second-order logic (MSO) over the infinite binary tree to the non-emptiness problem of
                                         finite automata running over labelled infinite trees, which yields decidability. The accep-
                                         tance criterion used in the automata generalises previous ideas of Büchi [Büc62]. The
                                         result gave rise to the whole area of automata-based methods in verification, whose realm
                                         of applications goes far beyond the original theory of the tree, see e.g., [Tho96, FGW08,
                                         DGL16, Pin21] for surveys.
                                             The potential of automata on infinite trees for probabilistic questions in verification has
                                         been noted by several authors [CDK12, Mio12, CHS14]. The space of trees can be naturally
                                         equipped with a Cantor-like topology as well as the probability measure, where we assume
                                         that each letter is chosen independently at random (called coin-flipping measure). The
                                         following question can be viewed as a probabilistic counterpart of the problem solved by
                                         Rabin.
                                         Problem 1.1. Given a representation of a regular tree language L, find the probability that
                                         a randomly generated tree belongs to L.

    An analogous question for regular languages of ω-words can be traced back to the
early work on verification of finite-state probabilistic systems initiated, among others, by
Vardi [Var85]. An efficient solution follows from the work by Courcoubetis and Yan-
nakakis [CY95] (see also [Var99]). It has been noted by several authors that, for the
coin-flipping measure, the probability in question is rational (see, e.g., [CJH04, Izu01]).
    In the case of infinite trees, it is not even evident that the probability in question always
exists, as a regular tree language need not in general be Borel [NW03]. However, it was
established by Gogacz et al. [GMMS17] that all regular languages of trees are measurable.
    Chen et al. [CDK12] addressed the question for trees in the case where the tree lan-
guage L is recognised by a deterministic top-down parity automaton and the measure
is induced by a stochastic branching process, which then makes also a part of the input
data. Their algorithm compares the probability with any given rational number in poly-
nomial space and with 0 or 1 in polynomial time.
    Michalewski and Mio [MM15] gave an algorithm for a larger class of languages L given
by so-called game automata. Their algorithm (given for the coin-flipping measure) reduces
the problem to computing the value of a Markov branching play, and uses Tarski’s decision
procedure for the theory of reals. These authors also discovered that the measure of a reg-
ular tree language can be irrational. Later on, Niwiński et al. [NPS20] solved the problem
for weak alternating automata, which capture precisely weak MSO logic (with quantifiers
restricted to finite sets).
    In this paper we ultimately solve Problem 1.1 for nondeterministic parity tree automata,
which capture the whole MSO.
Theorem 1.2. There is an algorithm that inputs a nondeterministic parity tree automa-
ton A and computes the coin-flipping measure of the language of trees recognised by A.
Moreover, this measure is an algebraic number.
    As a consequence, given additionally a rational (or just algebraic) number q, we can
decide if the resulting measure is equal, smaller, or greater than q.
    Our algorithm is able to produce a representation of the above measure in three-fold
exponential time. The decision problems with a fixed rational number q can be solved
in two-fold exponential space. These complexity upper bounds coincide with the ones
provided by Przybyłko and Skrzypczak [PS20] in the case of safety automata.
    Instead of considering the coin-flipping measure over a full binary tree, one may also be
interested in trees generated by a given stochastic branching process [CDK12, NPS20]. It
was already observed by Niwiński et al. [NPS20, Section 7.1] that such a situation reduces
to the basic case of the coin-flipping measure: essentially, instead of an automaton A
reading a tree generated by a branching process P, we may consider an automaton being a
product of A and P, reading a binary tree chosen randomly according to the coin-flipping
measure. We thus have the following corollary, with the same complexity bounds as for
Theorem 1.2.
Corollary 1.3. There is an algorithm that inputs a stochastic branching process P and
a nondeterministic parity tree automaton A and computes the measure induced by P of the
language of trees recognised by A. Again, this measure is an algebraic number.

                                               2
    It is worth to note that natural stronger probabilistic variants of the satisfiability prob-
lem are known to be undecidable. For example, both the non-emptiness problem [Paz71]
and the value-1 problem [GO10] for probabilistic finite automata, even for finite words, are
undecidable. Moreover, Baier et al. [BBG08] showed that it is undecidable whether there
exists an ω-word that is accepted by a given probabilistic Büchi automaton with a positive
probability. So presumably we are close to the frontiers of decidability here.
    One should note that our result does not entail the Rabin tree theorem—the satis-
fiability problem does not directly reduce to its probabilistic variant. Also, the core of
the construction of Rabin was a translation between an MSO formula and an automaton,
whereas we begin our construction already with a given automaton.
    The main difficulty while considering probabilistic questions about automata lies in
nondeterminism, which breaks a correspondence between input objects and runs of the
automaton. It is known that in the case of infinite trees nondeterminism is inherent. As
a remedy, we take the µ-calculus perspective on tree automata. Indeed, it is well-known
that µ-calculus (even in a restricted form) interpreted over trees captures the full power of
tree automata [Niw88] (the opposite direction follows easily from the Rabin tree theorem).
    This helps to a large degree, but not fully. Having two random events A, B we can speak
about their union A ∪ B and intersection A ∩ B, but knowing only the probabilities P(A),
P(B) we cannot say what is the probability of P(A∪B) or P(A∩B). A similar problem
arises with other functions of arity greater than 1. In order to overcome this difficulty,
we introduce a new formalism: unary µ-calculus. We believe that this formalism is of
independent interest, and can potentially find other applications.

2       Basic concepts
Trees and automata
A tree over an alphabet Σ is a function t : {L, R}∗ → Σ. Here L, R represent the directions
leading from a node v ∈ {L, R}∗ to its children v L and v R. The empty word ε is the root
of the tree. A subtree of a tree t starting in a node v is a tree denoted t.v defined by
t.v(w) = t(vw), for all w ∈ {L, R}∗ . The set of all trees over Σ is denoted TrΣ .
    A nondeterministic max-parity tree automaton is defined to be A = ⟨Σ, Q, qI , γ, Ω⟩,
where Σ is a finite input alphabet, Q a finite set of states, qI ∈ Q an initial state, γ ⊆
Q × Σ × Q × Q a transition relation, and Ω : Q → {0, . . . , d−1} assigns a priority to each
state.1 We assume without loss of generality that d > 0 is an even number. A run of
such an automaton A over a tree t ∈ TrΣ is a function ρ : {L, R}∗ → Q that assigns a state
to every node of t, such that (ρ(v), t(v), ρ(v L), ρ(v R)) ∈ γ for all v ∈ {L, R}∗ . A run ρ is
accepting if, for any branch w ∈ {L, R}ω , the maximal priority occurring infinitely often is
even, that is, lim supn→∞ Ω ρ(wn ) is even, where wn is the prefix of w of length n. A tree

Orders and fixed points
Let (X, ≤) be a partial order. The least upper bound of a subset A ⊆ X (if exists) is
also called the supremum of A and denoted by sup A, and the greatest lower bound is the
infimum denoted by inf A. A complete lattice is a partial order such that suprema and
infima exist for all subsets. In particular sup X = ⊤ and inf X = ⊥ are the greatest and
least elements of X. The supremum and infimum of a two-element set {a, b} are denoted
a∨b and a∧b, respectively.
    The celebrated Knaster-Tarski theorem states that if (X, ≤) is a complete lattice then
any monotone (i.e., order-preserving) function f : X → X has the least (µ) and the greatest
(ν) fixed points satisfying
                                               
                               µx. f (x) = inf a | f (a) ≤ a ,
                                               
                                νx. f (x) = sup a | a ≤ f (a) .

For a monotone function of several arguments, we can apply fixed-point operators succes-
sively. For example, νy. µx. g(x, y) is the greatest fixed point of the mapping y 7→ µx. g(x, y)
(it is easy to observe that this mapping is also monotone).
     A chain in a partial order (X, ≤) is any non-empty subset C ⊆ X linearly ordered
by ≤. A subset A ⊆ X is called a chain-complete subset of X if the supremum and the
infimum of every chain C ⊆ A exists and remains in A. We write Pcc (X) for the set of all
chain-complete subsets of X.
     We say that a monotone function f : X → Y between two complete lattices, (X, ≤)
and (Y, ≤), is chain-continuous if for every chain C ⊆ X it holds sup(f (C)) = f (sup C)
and inf(f (C)) = f (inf C).
     Whenever we consider a space of the form X n for a partial order (X, ≤) and a natural
number n ≥ 0, we assume that X n is ordered coordinate-wise, that is, (x0 , . . . , xn−1 ) ≤
(y0 , . . . , yn−1 ) if xi ≤ yi for all i ∈ {0, . . . , n−1}.

3     Fixed-point approach
Our proof relies on fixed-point definitions of regular tree languages in the powerset lattice
of TrΣ . Such a definition can be derived from a parity automaton (either nondeterministic
or alternating) recognising the given language [Niw88, Niw97]. The operations involved
are a(·, ·), for each a ∈ Σ, the binary set union ∪ and intersection ∩, and the least (µ)
and greatest (ν) fixed-point operators. The aforementioned operation a(LL , LR ) sends the
languages LL and LR to the set of trees t, such that t.L ∈ LL , t.R ∈ LR , and t(ε) = a. For
example, an expression µx. νy. a(x, x) ∪ b(y, y) defines the set of trees over the alphabet
{a, b} where, on each branch, a occurs only finitely often. In general, it is convenient to
use vectorial fixed-point expressions of the form

                        µxd−1 . νxd−2 . . . νx2 . µx1 . νx0 . F (x0 , x1 , . . . , xd−1 ),           (3.1)

where the vectorial variables xi = (xi,1 , . . . , xi,k ), for i ∈ {0, 1, . . . , d−1}, range over k-tuples
of subsets of TrΣ , and F is a k-tuple of terms constructed from the basic operations
mentioned above. If we start with a parity tree automaton, the parameter k corresponds
to the number of states, and the parameter d to the number of priorities (see, e.g., [AN01]).
More specifically, if the j-th state has priority i, it becomes a variable xi,j . Moreover, if
the automaton is nondeterministic then no intersection ∩ is used in the expression.

3.2    Unary µ-calculus
Is it possible to apply a fixed-point approach when, instead of non-emptiness, we are
interested in the measure of a set?

lattice {0, 1}, so that an appropriate interpretation of Expression (3.1) would yield the re-
searched measure. A natural candidate is the space [0, 1] along with the monotone mapping

    However, the measures of neither the union ∪ nor the intersection ∩ of two languages are
determined by the measures of the languages themselves, so it is not possible to interpret
∪ nor ∩ here. This reflects a more general phenomenon: the knowledge of distributions
of two random variables does not determine their joint distribution. Thus, when working
with measures, we should avoid using functions with more than one argument.
    To overcome this obstacle, we introduce the main technical invention of our paper:
unary µ-calculus.
    In the classical µ-calculus, an application of a fixed-point operator, say µ, to a mapping
F : X → X returns an element µx. F (x) of X, and if applied to a distinguished argument
of a (k+1)-ary function, it returns a k-ary function, for example µx0 . G(x0 , x1 , . . . , xk ), and
so on. In unary µ-calculus, an application of a fixed-point operator to a unary monotone
function returns another unary monotone function. More precisely, when applied to a func-
tion F : X → X, it returns a (partial) function F ↑ : X ⇀ X, that sends an element x ∈ X
to the least fixed point of F above x (if exists). Similarly, F ↓ : X ⇀ X sends an element
x ∈ X to the greatest fixed point of F below x (if exists). Such fixed points need not
always exist even if X is a complete lattice: although the respective subsets are complete
sublattices, the function F need not preserve them. However, when F (x) ≥ x then F ↑(x)
exists and can be obtained by taking a limit of an ascending chain of approximations (as
in Knaster-Tarski theorem); similarly for F ↓(x), whenever F (x) ≤ x.
    An essential step of our construction is to define a formula of unary µ-calculus which,
in the domain of tree languages, is equivalent to Expression (3.1). Since transitions of
the considered automaton are already black-boxed into a single function F , the exact type
of the automaton does not matter: the construction works in the same manner for both
nondeterministic and alternating automata. We further show that the derived formula
interpreted in an appropriate probabilistic domain yields the researched measure.

of trees. We come back to trees in Section 10 and verify that all the sets of interest for
us are measurable. This concerns not only the regular tree languages whose measurability
is known [GMMS17], but also the sets obtained in the iterative approximation of fixed
points. Knowing this, we can switch our perspective from profiles to probability distribu-

purchased distribution of the random variable τA associated with the automaton can be
obtained by reinterpreting our formula of unary µ-calculus in the probabilistic powerdo-

that formulae of unary µ-calculus interpreted in the probabilistic powerdomain can be ex-
pressed using Tarski’s first-order theory over reals. Therefore, decidability of this theory
implies decidability of our question expressed by a unary µ-calculus formula. Finally, in
Section 13 we treat the case of trees generated by a stochastic branching process, proving
Corollary 1.3.

4.1    Alternating automata
An alternating tree automaton is defined analogously to a nondeterministic one, except

5.2    Computing limits
For an ordinal η > 0 consider a sequence (xζ )ζ<η of elements of X. We say that such
a sequence is increasing if xζ ≤ xζ ′ whenever ζ < ζ ′ < η. We define the limit of such
a sequence, denoted limζ<η xζ , as the supremum of the chain {xζ | ζ < η}. Analogously
we define a decreasing sequence, whose limit is inf{xζ | ζ < η}. A sequence is monotonic
if it is either increasing or decreasing.
Definition 5.2. We say that a partial function F : X ⇀ X is ascending on A ⊆ X if: F
is monotone, A ⊆ dom(F ), A is a chain-complete subset of X, and for each x ∈ A we
have F (x) ∈ A and F (x) ≥ x.
   F is descending on A if the analogue conditions are met with F (x) ≤ x.
   Assume that F : X ⇀ X is either ascending or descending on A ⊆ X and x ∈ A. For
an ordinal η define inductively F η (x) to be
   • x if η = 0,

   • F (F η−1 (x)) if η is a successor ordinal, and

   • limζ<η F ζ (x) if η is a limit ordinal.

(descending) on A and A is chain complete; in particular it is increasing (decreasing,
respectively) and all its elements remain in A. Then, for some ordinal η∞ (at latest for the
first ordinal of cardinality greater than |X|) the values must stabilise: F η∞ +1 (x) = F η∞ (x).

8    µ-calculus toolbox
In this section we recall some facts concerning the standard µ-calculus and develop some
equalities, which are used in the next section, for the sake of proving Proposition 9.1, that
is, that Φd−1 is equivalent to the formula from Proposition 4.2.
     Let (V, ≤) be any complete lattice—the tools developed here are generic µ-calculus
equalities.
     First, by the Knaster-Tarski theorem (cf. Section 2), for an element τ ∈ V, to prove
µx. f (x) ≤ τ , it is enough to show f (τ ) ≤ τ . Similarly, to prove τ ≤ νx. f (x), it is enough
to show τ ≤ f (τ ). Second, we have the following fact.

Fact 8.1 ([AN01, Corollary 1.3.8]). Let f : V n → V be monotone, let θ0 , . . . , θn−1 ∈ {µ, ν},
and let σ⊥ , σ⊤ ∈ V. If σ⊥ ≤ θn−1 xn−1 . . . θ0 x0 . f (x0 , . . . , xn−1 ) ≤ σ⊤ then

                        θn−1 xn−1 . . . θ0 x0 . f (x0           , x1 , . . . , xn−1 )
                      = θn−1 xn−1 . . . θ0 x0 . f ((x0 ∧σ⊤ )∨σ⊥ , x1 , . . . , xn−1 ).

   We now have a series of lemmata, which allow us to simplify some expressions of
µ-calculus. The first lemma can be shown using directly the Knaster-Tarski theorem.

Lemma 8.2. For every monotone function f : V 2 → V, we have

10.2     Unary µ-calculus preserves measurability
Let F be a signature and assume that (W, ≤) is a structure over F in such a way that for
every function name H ∈ F the function HW : W → W is local and preserves measurability
on the whole W. Our goal is to prove that each formula of unary µ-calculus is also local
and preserves measurability on a certain domain, as stated in Lemmata 10.13 and 10.14.
   As shown in Lemma 10.3, the above assumptions are met by our structure with functions
∆, Bidn , and Cutn , but results of this subsection are not specific to this structure.

Lemma 10.13. If we can derive F :: A → B for a formula F of unary µ-calculus then the
partial function F : W ⇀ W is local on A.

Proof. Induction on the structure of the formula. Base functions H ∈ F are local on the
whole W by the assumption. For F ; G we directly use the induction hypothesis. Consider
a formula of the form F ↑. We use Lemma 5.3 to see that F ↑(τ ) = F ∞ (τ ) for τ ∈ A.
We then recall that F ∞ (τ ) is defined by applying F and by taking limits of monotonic
sequences. Every application of F is local: if F η−1 (τ ) and F η−1 (τ ′ ) agree on all the
subtrees of some t0 , then also F η (τ ) = F (F η−1 (τ )) and F η (τ ′ ) = F (F η−1 (τ ′ )) agree on all
the subtrees of t0 . For limits we have an even stronger property: if F ζ (τ ) and F ζ (τ ′ ) agree
on a tree t (a subtree of t0 ) for every ζ < η, where η is a limit ordinal, then also the limits
F η (τ ) and F η (τ ′ ) agree on t, because the order considers every tree t separately. It follows
that if τ, τ ′ ∈ A agree on all the subtrees of t0 , then F ↑(τ ) and F ↑(τ ′ ) agree on all the
subtrees of t0 , that is, F ↑ is local on A. Finally, the case of F ↓ is symmetric.

Lemma 10.14. If we can derive F :: A → B for a formula F of unary µ-calculus then the
partial function F : W ⇀ W preserves measurability on A.

Proof. We proceed inductively over the shape of F . For H ∈ F the thesis follows from
the assumption on H, and for F ; G it is a direct consequence of the induction hypothesis.
For a formula of the form F ↑ we know that F is ascending on A because we can derive
F ↑ :: A → B; that F is local on A by Lemma 10.13; and that F preserves measurability

                                                  38
on A by the induction hypothesis. Then Proposition 10.4 says in particular that if τ is
a random variable then F ∞ (τ ) (which equals (F ↑)(τ ) by Lemma 5.3) is a random variable;
this is exactly what we need. The case of F ↓ is analogous.

In this section we explain the reasons for the shape of the formula Φd−1 . The source of
difficulty of our construction can be explained already for d = 2 when Ω : Q → {0, 1}

When we move to distributions of random variables, it is clearly not enough to consider
separately a distribution of x0 and a distribution of x1 , because the distribution of δ(x0 , x1 )
depends on the joint distribution of its arguments.
   Thus, one could try to work with pairs of profiles and consider functions V 2 → V 2 .
This leads to a function                                          

which is a monotone and chain-continuous function on V 2 . Moreover, the fixed point
νx0 . δ(x0 , x1 ) can be computed in a descending way, that is, as the first coordinate of
U2 ↓(⊤, x1 ).
    The problem here is that again the operation U2 cannot be simulated on distributions
of random variables: the events used to compute the value of δ(b              τ0 , τb1 ) (i.e., the root, the
left subtree, and the right subtree of a random tree) and the event used to compute the
value of τb1 (i.e., the whole tree) are not independent, unlike in the proof of Lemma 11.7.
Roughly speaking, to compute U2 one has to look simultaneously at the whole randomly
generated tree  t as well as its root label t(ε) and its two subtrees t.L and t.R. The variables
 t(ε), t.L, t.R are independent as a vector, but stop being independent when we also consider
the whole tree t.
    The above problem would not arise, if the involved operator had the form

                               U2′ : (x0 , x1 ) 7→ δ(x0 , x1 ), δ(x1 , x1 ) .
                                                                           

This operation indeed can be translated to distributions in such a way that it commutes
with computing distributions. Moreover, if τ1 is any fixed point of x1 7→ δ(x1 , x1 ) then
obviously U2′ (τ0 , τ1 ) = U2 (τ0 , τ1 ).
    This explains the motivation standing behind our formula Φ1 : on some coordinates,
instead of applying the identity τ1 7→ τ1 , we can apply a proper variant of δ if we make sure
in advance that the current value of the given coordinate is already a fixed point of it (see
the definition of a 2-saturated tuple (τ0 , τ1 ) from Section 6.2). To achieve this, we need to
add additional nested fixed-point operations on variants of δ, as in the inductive definition
of Φn from Φn−1 . The operations Bidn and Cutn , moving values between coordinates, give
us a technical tool for switching between computations of particular fixed points from the
original formula.

Lemma 12.7. The translation of Φd−1 into first-order logic over reals given by Proposi-

14      Conclusions
We have shown that the following question can be effectively solved.
      What is the probability that a randomly chosen tree satisfies a given formula
      of MSO?
This can be viewed as a completion of the famous Rabin tree theorem by its probabilistic
aspect. If a formula is replaced by an equivalent nondeterministic or alternating parity
automaton A, our algorithm works in elementary time.
    We believe that our results or techniques may find some further applications although—
as mentioned in Introduction—a number of related problems in probabilistic verification
of finite-state system is known to be undecidable. One of the research directions points to
logics whose original version reduces to MSO logic over trees, but the decidability status
of the probabilistic version is unknown as, for instance PCTL*.
    On the technical level, our work confirms the usefulness of the construction of the
probabilistic powerdomain D that comprises all the necessary information and is still man-
ageable. We have also discovered that the framework of the µ-calculus—originally based on
complete lattices—can be transposed to more general partial orders. The unary µ-calculus
that we have introduced for this purpose has a potential of incorporating other formalisms,
going beyond the MSO theory of the tree. As a matter of fact, our construction provides a
way to translate a generic formula of unary µ-calculus into a formula of Tarski’s first-order
theory of reals, which yields decidability of the formalism in question.
