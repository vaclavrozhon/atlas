                                                                                                Abstract

                                                         Several new accelerated methods in minimax optimization and fixed-point iterations
                                                     have recently been discovered, and, interestingly, they rely on a mechanism distinct from
                                                     Nesterov’s momentum-based acceleration. In this work, we show that these accelerated
                                                     algorithms exhibit what we call the merging path (MP) property; the trajectories of these
                                                     algorithms merge quickly. Using this novel MP property, we establish point convergence of
                                                     existing accelerated minimax algorithms and derive new state-of-the-art algorithms for the
                                                     strongly-convex-strongly-concave setup and for the prox-grad setup.

1.1    Organization and contribution
In this work, we identify the merging path (MP) property among anchoring-based algorithms,
which states that the trajectories of these algorithms quickly merge, and use this novel property
to develop new accelerated algorithms. First, we show that known accelerated minimax algo-
rithms [108, 51, 98] have O(1/k 2 )-merging paths with the optimized Halpern iteration [36, 53]
and thereby establish their point convergence1 . Second, we present a new accelerated minimax
algorithm for smooth strongly-convex-strongly-concave setup designed to approximate, in the
MP sense, the OC-Halpern method, which has an optimal accelerated rate. Third, we present
a near-optimal accelerated proximal gradient type algorithm, designed to approximate, in the
MP sense, the Halpern-accelerated Douglas-Rachford splitting [28, 56].


1.2    The MP property
Let A be a deterministic algorithm. Write A(x0 ; P) = (x0 , x1 , x2 , . . . ) to denote that A applied
to problem P with starting point x0 produces iterates x1 , x2 , . . . . We say two algorithms A1
and A2 have O(r(k))-merging paths if for any problem P and x0 ∈ Rd , the iterates Aℓ (x0 ; P) =
       (ℓ) (ℓ)                             (1)  (2) 2
 x0 , x1 , x2 , . . . for ℓ = 1, 2 satisfy xk − xk     = O(r(k)). More concisely, we say A1 and
A2 are O(r(k))-MP if they have O(r(k))-merging paths.
     The MP property precisely formalizes a notion of near-equivalence of algorithms. In par-
ticular, it is a stronger notion of approximation compared to the conceptual ones of prior work
[66, 1]. Classical first-order algorithms for smooth convex minimization are not O(r(k))-MP
with r(k) → 0. Fig. 1 shows that accelerated gradient methods with different momentum co-
efficients have divergent paths, even though their function values (of course) converge to the
same optimal value. In this paper, we show that the accelerated minimax algorithms for smooth
convex-concave minimax optimization are O(1/k 2 )-MP (which is faster than the o(1)-point con-
vergence). Fig. 1b shows that the algorithms’ paths indeed merge far before they converge to
the limit.

1.3   Related work


The extragradient (EG) [49] and Popov’s algorithm [83], also known as optimistic gradient
(OG) descent [85, 22], have initiated the long stream of research in the optimization literature
on the closely interconnected topics of minimax optimization [71, 39, 47, 64, 66, 67, 5, 37, 99],
variational inequalities [77, 73, 74, 43, 10, 11, 62, 61, 17, 40, 59], and monotone inclusion
problems [92, 100, 68, 69, 57]. A number of advances in minimax optimization have been
made in the context of practical machine learning applications, including the multi-player game
dynamics [21, 93, 32, 41] and GANs [104, 31, 15, 52, 64, 90, 82]. A recent trend of the field
focuses on achieving near-optimal efficiency with respect to primal-dual gap or distance to the
optimum for convex-concave minimax problems with distinct strong convexity, strong concavity
or smoothness parameters for each variable [95, 55, 102, 2, 106, 97, 42], closely matching the
lower bound results [79, 109]. Another line of works focuses on finding approximate stationary
points of nonconvex-strongly-concave (or Polyak-Lojasiewicz) minimax problems, which can be
viewed as reducing generalized versions of the objective’s gradient magnitude [7, 54, 48, 78, 107].

    The proximal point method (PPM) [70, 63, 88] is an implicit algorithm for solving con-
vex minimization, minimax optimization, and, more generally, monotone inclusion problems.
Although PPM is not always directly applicable, it has been used as the conceptual basis for
developing many algorithms with approximate proximal evaluations [87, 35, 92, 71, 38, 14, 29,
24, 48, 78, 84]. Several prior works [66, 1] interpreted prominent forward algorithms such as
Nesterov’s accelerated gradient method (AGM), EG, and OG as conceptual approximations of
PPM.

    Since the seminal work of Nesterov [72], acceleration of first-order algorithms has been
extensively studied for convex minimization problems [76, 9, 45, 6, 80, 94]. Lately, there has
been a rapid progress in the discovery of accelerated algorithms with respect to the gradient
magnitude of the objective for smooth convex problems [46, 75, 26, 50, 110] and smooth convex-
concave minimax problems [25, 108, 51, 98]. Acceleration of PPM has been achieved for convex
minimization [35] and more recently for monotone inclusions in terms of the fixed-point residual
[44, 81]. Notably, the latter has been shown [81, 89] to be an instance of the Halpern iteration
[36] with optimal parameters [53]. While the accelerated minimax algorithms bear an apparent
resemblance to the Halpern iteration [25, 98], the latest results with optimal rates [108, 51, 98]
were not formally understood through this connection.

    The particular case of bilinearly-coupled minimax problems, L(x, y) = f (x) + ⟨x, Ay⟩ − g(y)
for some convex functions f and g and a linear operator A, have been studied by many re-
searchers. A line of works [76, 103, 96] considered the setups where f and g are smooth and one
can only access their gradients. Another line of works, more closely connected to the setup we
study in Section 5, considered the cases when f or g is proximable, and used primal-dual splitting
algorithms to solve those problems [13, 19, 101, 16, 39, 47, 105, 89]. Primal-dual problem setups
can often be reformulated into a more flexible format of composite monotone inclusion problems
and addressed via forward-backward splitting algorithms [100, 18, 19, 61, 20, 60], which also
extend the prox-grad-type methods including the iterative shrinkage-thresholding algorithms
(ISTA) [23, 9]. Our result of Section 5 can be understood as acceleration of forward-backward
algorithms with respect to the forward-backward residual, which generalizes the gradient mag-
nitude. For the special case of the projected-gradient setup, a near-optimal acceleration has
been achieved in prior work [25].

2    Background and preliminaries
A function L(·, ·) : X × Y → R is convex-concave if X and Y are convex sets, L(x, y) is
convex as a function of x for all y, and L(x, y) is concave as a function of y for all x. A point
(x⋆ , y⋆ ) ∈ X ×Y is a saddle point, or (minimax) solution of (1) if L(x⋆ , y) ≤ L(x⋆ , y⋆ ) ≤ L(x, y⋆ )
for all (x, y) ∈ X × Y.
     An operator 𝔸 on Rd , denoted 𝔸 : Rd ⇒ Rd , is a set-valued function, i.e., 𝔸(z) ⊆ Rd for all
z ∈ Rd . For simplicity, we often write 𝔸z = 𝔸(z). If 𝔸z contains exactly one element for all
z ∈ Rd , then we say 𝔸 is single-valued and view it as a function. An operator 𝔸 : Rd ⇒ Rd is
monotone if
                              ⟨𝔸z − 𝔸z ′ , z − z ′ ⟩ ≥ 0,          ∀ z, z ′ ∈ Rd ,
where the notation means that ⟨u − v, z − z ′ ⟩ ≥ 0 for any u ∈ 𝔸z and v ∈ 𝔸z ′ . For µ > 0, an
operator 𝔸 is µ-strongly monotone if
                         ⟨𝔸z − 𝔸z ′ , z − z ′ ⟩ ≥ µ∥z − z ′ ∥2 ,        ∀ z, z ′ ∈ Rd .
The graph of 𝔸 is denoted Gra 𝔸 = {(z, u) | u ∈ 𝔸z}. A monotone operator 𝔸 is maximally
monotone if there is no monotone operator 𝔸′ such that Gra 𝔸 ⊂ Gra 𝔸′ strictly. In particular,
if 𝔸 is monotone, single-valued, and continuous as a function, then it is maximally monotone
[8, Corollary 20.28]. Define
         (𝔸 + 𝔹)(z) = {u + v ∈ Rd | u ∈ 𝔸z, v ∈ 𝔹z},               (α𝔸)(z) = {αu ∈ Rd | u ∈ 𝔸z}
for operators 𝔸 and 𝔹 and scalar α ∈ R. The inverse of an operator 𝔸 is the operator
𝔸−1 : Rd ⇒ Rd defined by 𝔸−1 (u) = {z ∈ Rd | u ∈ 𝔸z}.

                               ∇± L(x, y) = (∇x L(x, y), −∇y L(x, y))
and call it the saddle operator of L. Then ∇± L is monotone [87], and z⋆ = (x⋆ , y⋆ ) is a
solution of (1) if and only if ∇± L(z⋆ ) = 0. Therefore, a convex-concave minimax optimization
problem (1) can be reformulated as a monotone inclusion problem (3) with 𝔸 = ∇± L and as a
fixed-point problem (2) with 𝕋 = 𝕁∇± L .
    The fixed-point residual norm ∥z − 𝕋z∥ quantifies the rate of convergence of an algorithm
for finding fixed points. When 𝕋 = 𝕁α𝔹 , we have z − 𝕋z = z − 𝕁α𝔹 (z) ∈ α𝔹(𝕁α𝔹 (z)), because
                 u = 𝕁α𝔹 (z) = (𝕀 + α𝔹)−1 (z) ⇐⇒ z ∈ (𝕀 + α𝔹)(u) = u + α𝔹u.
Thus, when 𝕋 = 𝕁α𝔹 and 𝔹 is single-valued, a convergence rate on ∥z − 𝕋z∥ is equivalent to a
convergence rate on ∥𝔹(·)∥.

3     Accelerated smooth minimax algorithms are O(1/k 2 )-MP
In this section, we prove the O(1/k 2 )-MP property among accelerated minimax algorithms and
the optimized Halpern iteration. The problem setup is unconstrained minimax problems (so
X = Rn and Y = Rm ) with convex-concave L such that ∇± L is L-Lipschitz. For notational
convenience, write 𝔹 = ∇± L : Rd → Rd , with d = n + m. Our MP result provides new
insight into these acceleration mechanisms and allows us to establish their point convergence
zk → z⋆ ∈ Zer 𝔹.

5.1   Preliminaries: Subdifferential operators and forward-backward residual
If Lp : Rn × Rm → R is a finite-valued, not necessarily differentiable convex-concave function,
then its (saddle) subdifferential operator ∂± Lp : Rn × Rm ⇒ Rn × Rm defined by

              ∂± Lp (x, y) = {(v, w) ∈ Rn × Rm | v ∈ ∂x Lp (x, y), w ∈ ∂y (−Lp )(x, y)}

is maximal monotone [86, Corollary 37.5.2], where ∂x and ∂y respectively denote the convex

7    Conclusion
In this paper, we identified the novel merging path (MP) property among accelerated minimax
algorithms, EAG [108], FEG [51], and APS [98], and the accelerated proximal algorithm OHM
[36, 53, 44]. We then used this MP property to design new state-of-the-art algorithms.
     We quickly mention that our results straightforwardly extend to the setup where the under-
lying space Rd is replaced with an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, since our proofs do not rely
on the finite-dimensionality of Rd . In particular, the point convergence results of Corollary 3.3
and Corollary 5.2 hold as strong convergence.
     The MP property provides the insight that the four different accelerated algorithms, EAG,
FEG, APS, and OHM, actually reduce to one single acceleration mechanism. However, elu-
cidating the fundamental principle behind this single anchoring-based acceleration mechanism
still remains an open problem, and further investigating the phenomenon from multiple different
viewpoints, as was done for Nesterov’s acceleration, would be an interesting direction of future
work.
     The presence or applicability of the MP property in other setups is another interesting
direction of future work. One question is whether other prior optimization algorithms exhibit the
MP property. In our analysis, we found that anchoring induces the MP property, so algorithms
using similar regularizations, such as the recursive regularization [4] or the Katyusha momentum
[3], may be approximating some other conceptual algorithm. Another question is whether the
MP property can be used to design new algorithms in other setups. The strategy of designing
efficient algorithms by approximating a conceptual algorithm, in the MP sense, may be widely
applicable in setups beyond non-stochastic minimax optimization.

A.1    Classical minimax optimization algorithms
Simultaneous gradient descent-ascent for smooth minimax optimization is defined by
                                     xk+1 = xk − α∇x L(xk , yk )
                                     yk+1 = yk + α∇y L(xk , yk )
where α > 0 is the step-size. Writing zk = (xk , yk ) and using the ∇± notation, we can more
concisely rewrite this as
                                      zk+1 = zk − α∇± L(zk ).
The extragradient (EG) algorithm is defined by
                                   zk+1/2 = zk − α∇± L(zk ),
                                     zk+1 = zk − α∇± L(zk+1/2 ).
The optimistic gradient descent (OG) algorithm is defined by
                      zk+1 = zk − α∇± L(zk ) − α (∇± L(zk ) − ∇± L(zk−1 )) ,
where z0 = z−1 are the starting points.

A.2    Nesterov’s AGM and its momentum parameters
We specify the versions of Nesterov’s AGM used in Fig. 1a. Given f : Rn → R which is convex
and L-smooth, AGM takes the form
                                 xk+1 = yk − α∇f (yk )
                                               tk − 1
                                 yk+1 = xk+1 +         (xk+1 − xk )
                                                tk+1
for k = 0, 1, . . . . It converges at the accelerated rate f (xk ) − f⋆ ≤ O(∥x0 − x⋆ ∥2 /k 2 ) provided
that α ≤ L1 and t2k+1 − tk+1 − t2k ≤ 0 [72, 12]. In particular, we take tk = k+a−1   a     (which was
used in [12]) and refer to a as the momentum parameter in Fig. 1a.

F     Regularity of convex and convex-concave functions
Consider an extended real-valued convex function f : Rn → R ∪ {±∞}. We say f is proper if
f (x) > −∞ for all x ∈ Rn and f (x) < +∞ for at least one x ∈ Rn . The closure f , written as
cl f , is defined as the constant function cl f ≡ −∞ if f (x) = −∞ for some x ∈ Rn and otherwise

                                     cl f (x) := lim
                                                   ′
                                                     inf f (x′ ).
                                                  x →x


                                                 42
We say f is closed if cl f = f (when f is proper, this is equivalent to lower semicontinuity). If f
is closed, convex, and proper (CCP), its convex subdifferential operator ∂f : Rn ⇒ Rn defined
by

                    ∂f (x) = {v ∈ Rn | f (x′ ) ≥ f (x) + ⟨v, x′ − x⟩, ∀ x′ ∈ Rn }

is maximal monotone [86, Corollary 31.5.2]. For an extended real-valued concave function g,
we define cl g = −cl (−g) and say it is proper (resp. closed) if −g is proper (resp. closed) as a
convex function.
    Let K : Rn × Rm → R ∪ {±∞} be an extended real-valued convex-concave function. Define

                         domx K = {x ∈ Rn | K(x, y) < +∞, ∀ y ∈ Rm }
                         domy K = {y ∈ Rn | K(x, y) > −∞, ∀ x ∈ Rn }
                                  dom K = domx K × domy K.

We say K is proper if dom K ̸= ∅. Define clx K(x, y) as the function obtained by taking the
convex closure of K(x, y) with respect to x for each fixed y, and define cly K(x, y) analogously.
Then both clx K and cly K are convex-concave [86, Corollary 33.1.1]. Respectively define the
lower and upper closures of K by

                                  K = clx cly K,        K = cly clx K,

which satisfy K ≤ K. We say K is closed if clx K = clx cly K and cly K = cly clx K.
   The saddle subdifferential operator ∂± K : Rn × Rm ⇒ Rn × Rm is defined by

              ∂± K(x, y) = {(v, w) ∈ Rn × Rm | v ∈ ∂x K(x, y), w ∈ ∂y (−K)(x, y)},

where ∂x K(x, y) denotes the convex subdifferential of K(x, y) as a convex function of x (for
each fixed y), and ∂y (−K)(x, y) denotes the convex subdifferential of −K(x, y) as a convex
function of y (for each fixed x). If K is closed, convex-concave, and proper (CCCP), then ∂± K
is maximal monotone [86, Corollary 37.5.2] and (x⋆ , y⋆ ) is a minimax solution to

                                   minimize
                                        n
                                            maximize
                                                m
                                                     K(x, y)
                                     x∈R        y∈R

     We say two convex-concave functions K, L are equivalent if clx K = clx L and cly K = cly L.
If K, L are equivalent, then they share the set of minimax solutions, the function values at the
solutions (if any) are equal, and ∂± K = ∂± L [86, Theorem 36.4, Corollary 37.4.1]. If K is a
closed convex-concave function, then the equivalence class of closed convex-concave functions
containing K is precisely characterized [86, Theorem 34.2] by

           L : Rn × Rm → R ∪ {±∞} | L is closed, convex-concave, and K ≤ L ≤ K .
