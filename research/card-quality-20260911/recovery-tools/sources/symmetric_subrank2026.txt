                                               SYMMETRIC SUBRANK AND ITS BORDER ANALOGUE


                                           BENJAMIN BIAGGI, JAN DRAISMA, KOEN DE NOOIJ, AND IMMANUEL VAN SANTEN
arXiv:2604.12801v1 [math.AG] 14 Apr 2026




                                                    Abstract. The symmetric subrank of homogeneous polynomial is the largest
                                                    number of terms in a diagonal form to which it can be specialized by a (typ-
                                                    ically non-invertible) linear variable substitution. Building on earlier work
                                                    by Derksen-Makam-Zuiddam and Biaggi-Chang-Draisma-Rupniewski for or-
                                                    dinary tensors, we determine the asymptotic behavior of symmetric subrank
                                                    and symmetric border subrank of degree-d forms as the number of variables
                                                    tends to infinity. Furthermore, by using results from geometric invariant the-
                                                    ory we show that for cubic (resp. quartic) forms the symmetric subrank and
                                                    symmetric border subrank coincide if the latter is at most three (resp. two).

                                                                   1. Introduction and main results
                                               Throughout, we let V be an n-dimensional vector space over an algebraically
                                           closed field K. Let d be a nonnegative integer. We denote by S d V the d-symmetric
                                           power of V , the quotient space of V ⊗d by the vector subspace spanned by all
                                           differences T − σ(T ), where T ∈ V ⊗d and σ ∈ Sd is a permutation of {1, . . . , d}.
                                           The elements of S d V are called symmetric tensors in this note, and we use the
                                           notation v1 v2 · · · vd for the image in S d V of v1 ⊗ · · · ⊗ vd ; these symmetric tensors
                                           are called simple. The action of a linear map φ : V → W on symmetric tensors in
                                           S d V is determined by φ(v1 · · · vd ) := (φv1 ) · · · (φvd ). If V = W and φ ∈ GL(V ),
                                           this yields the usual left action of GL(V ) on S d V and we sometimes denote φ · f
                                           to emphasize this fact. Given two tensors f ∈ S d V and h ∈ S d W and a linear
                                           map φ ∈ Hom(V, W ) with φf = h, we say that h is a symmetric restriction of f ,
                                           denoted by h ≤s f .

f is defined as
                Qs (f ) := max{r | ∃ linear map φ : V → K r ; φf = Ir },
where Ir := ed1 + · · · + edr is the r-th unit tensor and e1 . . . , er is the standard basis

   The assumption (*) implies that, for r ≥ 2, Ir is not the d-th power of an element

   The symmetric subrank was introduced in [CFTZ22]. Note that d = 2 is not
allowed by our condition (*), but otherwise, for a quadratic form over a quadrat-

equal the rank of its Gram matrix.

In some settings, it is more convenient to work with homogeneous polynomials of
degree d, which are elements of S d ((K n )∗ ), and with a more geometric description of
the subrank. For a homogeneous polynomial f ∈ K[x0 , . . . , xn−1 ]d , the symmetric

specialized by a linear variable substitution
      Qs (f ) = max{r | ∃ linear map φ : K r → K n ; f ◦ φ = xd0 + · · · + xdr−1 }.
Given a homogeneous polynomial f , the zero set V(f ) defines a projective hypersur-

Pr−1 is, up to the action of GLr , defined by the Fermat form xd0 + · · · + xdr−1 = Ir :

We will use this geometric description in Section 6.
1.2. Generic (symmetric) subrank. The locus of tensors in V1 ⊗ · · · ⊗ Vd with

this locus is dense. We call that r the generic subrank of tensors in V1 ⊗ · · · ⊗ Vd .

1.3. (Generic) symmetric border subrank. The border subrank of a tensor in
V1 ⊗ · · · ⊗ Vd is the maximal r for which Ir lies in the closure of the set

subrank of f is defined as
                   Qs (f ) := max{r | Ir ∈ {φf | φ ∈ Hom(V, K r )}}.

1.4. Comparing symmetric subrank and symmetric border subrank. In
Section 5, we prove several results relating symmetric subrank and symmetric bor-
der subrank. We recall that the generic border subrank of order 3 tensors is, for
large enough n, strictly larger than the generic subrank, see [BCDR25, Theorem 4].
We were not able to show a similar result in our setting. The issue is the following:
there is a natural notion of Hilbert-Mumford subrank QHM (T ) of a tensor T that
satisfies Q(T ) ≤ QHM (T ) ≤ Q(T ), and [BCDR25, Proof of Theorem 4] shows that
in fact the first inequality is strict for sufficiently general order-3 tensors T when n
is large enough. This is not true for its symmetric analogue (we define QHM,s (f )
in Section 5):
Theorem 1.6. For any f ∈ S d V , the symmetric Hilbert-Mumford subrank QHM,s (f )
equals the symmetric subrank Qs (f ).
  Nevertheless, we do give an example of a quintic f with Qs (f ) < Qs (f ), see
Example 5.3. Finally, Section 6 is concerned with d = 3, 4, i.e., with cubic and
quartic forms. For low values of the symmetric border subrank, we show that Qs
and Qs coincide on cubics and quartics. Specifically:
Theorem 1.7. Assume that K = C. Suppose that f ∈ S 3 V and Qs (f ) ≤ 3 or
f ∈ S 4 V and Qs (f ) ≤ 2. Then in fact Qs (f ) = Qs (f ).

By the induction hypothesis applied to X ∩ H in H ≃ Pn we get C X∩H = P1 . Since
C X∩H ⊆ C X , we conclude that C X = P1 .                                     □
6.7. Proof of Theorem 1.7.
Proof of Theorem 1.7 using Theorem 1.8.           (1) Let f ∈ S 3 V have Qs (f ) ≤ 3. If
        Qs (f ) = 0, then f = 0 and also Qs (f ) = 0. If Qs (f ) = 1, then f ̸= 0 and
        for a sufficiently general linear map φ : V → C we have φf ̸= 0, so that φf
        is (a nonzero scalar multiple of) e31 . Hence Qs (f ) = 1. If Qs (f ) = 2, then
        the locus {φf | φ ∈ Hom(V, C2 )} has I2 := e31 + e32 in its closure. But the
        GL2 -orbit of this latter polynomial is dense in S 3 C2 , and hence that orbit
        intersects said locus. We conclude that Qs (f ) = 2.
            It remains to study the most interesting case, namely, Qs (f ) = 3. If
        dim(V ) = 3, then f has maximal symmetric border subrank, and hence
        maximal symmetric subrank 3 by Lemma 5.2. This also applies if dim(V ) ≥
        4 and f uses only 3 variables. So we may assume that dim(V ) ≥ 4 and f
        uses at least 4 variables. Let X ⊆ PV ∗ be the cubic hypersurface defined
        by f . For a linear surjection φ : V → C3 that does not map f to zero, φf
        is the equation for the projective curve in P2 that is the (scheme-theoretic)
        pre-image of X under the dual injection P2 → PV ∗ induced by φ. The
        assumption Qs (f ) ≥ 3 means that the Fermat cubic e31 + e22 + e33 lies in
        the closure of the locus of all such φf . In particular, X is normal, because
        otherwise all φf would define singular curves, and the Fermat cubic is
        smooth. Hence the conditions of Theorem 1.8 are satisfied, and we conclude
        that there exists a φ such that φf equals the Fermat cubic.
   (2) Now let f ∈ S 4 V have Qs (f ) ≤ 2. If Qs (f ) ≤ 1, then we can argue as
        above to show that Qs (f ) = Qs (f ). So suppose that Qs (f ) = 2. For
        a linear surjection φ : V → C2 that does not map f to zero, φf is the
        equation of the finite set of points on P1 that is the pre-image of X under
        the dual injection P1 → PV ∗ . The assumption that Qs (f ) = 2 implies
        that the equation e41 + e42 lies in the closure of the locus of all such φf . In
        particular, X is reduced. Hence all conditions of Theorem 1.8 are satisfied,
        and we conclude that there exists a φ such that φf = e41 + e42 .              □
Remark 6.18. The proof above shows how much stronger Theorem 1.8 is than
Theorem 1.7: in the latter part of the proof, not only the Fermat cubic, but any
smooth cubic curve can be obtained from f by a suitable φ : V → C3 ; and this
applies similarly to quartic hypersurfaces and (crossratios of) 4 points in P1 . ♢
