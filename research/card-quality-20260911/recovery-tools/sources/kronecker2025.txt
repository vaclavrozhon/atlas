                                                                                           Abstract
                                                   We study circuits for computing linear transforms defined by Kronecker power matrices.
                                               The best-known (unbounded-depth) circuits, including the widely-applied fast Walsh–Hadamard
                                               transform and Yates’ algorithm, can be derived from the best-known depth-2 circuits using
                                               known constructions, so we focus particularly on the depth-2 case. Recent work [JS13, Alm21,
                                               AGP23, Ser22] has improved on decades-old constructions in this area using a new rebalancing
                                               approach, but it was unclear how to apply this approach optimally, and the previous versions
                                               had complicated technical requirements.
                                                   We find that Strassen’s theory of asymptotic spectra can be applied to capture the design
                                               of these circuits. This theory was designed to generalize the known techniques behind matrix
                                               multiplication algorithms as well as a variety of other algorithms with recursive structure, and it
                                               brings a number of new tools to use for designing depth-2 circuits. In particular, in hindsight, we
                                               find that the techniques of recent work on rebalancing were proving special cases of the duality
                                               theorem which is central to Strassen’s theory. We carefully outline a collection of obstructions
                                               to designing small depth-2 circuits using a rebalancing approach, and apply Strassen’s theory
                                               to show that our obstructions are complete.
                                                   Using this connection, combined with other algorithmic techniques (including matrix rigidity
                                               upper bounds, constant-weight binary codes, and a “hole-fixing lemma” from recent matrix mul-
                                               tiplication algorithms), we give new improved circuit constructions as well as other applications,
                                               including:
                                                  • The N ×N disjointness matrix has a depth-2 linear circuit of size O(N 1.2495 ) over any field.
                                                    This is the first construction which surpasses exponent 1.25, and thus yields smaller circuits
                                                    for many families of matrices using reductions to disjointness, including all Kronecker
                                                    products of 2 × 2 matrices, without using matrix rigidity upper bounds.
                                                  • Barriers to further improvements, including that the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis
                                                    implies an N 1+Ω(1) size lower bound for depth-2 linear circuits computing the Walsh–
                                                    Hadamard transform (and the disjointness matrix with a technical caveat), and that prov-
                                                    ing such a N 1+Ω(1) depth-2 size lower bound would imply breakthrough threshold circuit
                                                    lower bounds.
                                                  • The Orthogonal Vectors (OV) problem in moderate dimension d can be solved in determin-
                                                    istic time Õ(n · 1.155d ), derandomizing an algorithm of Nederlof and Węgrzycki [NW21],
                                                    and the counting problem can be solved in time Õ(n · 1.26d ), improving an algorithm of
                                                    Williams [Wil24] which runs in time Õ(n · 1.35d ). We design these new algorithms by
                                                    noticing that prior algorithms for OV can be viewed as corresponding to depth-2 circuits
                                                    for the disjointness matrix, then using our framework for further improvements.

1     Introduction
For an na × ma matrix A, and a nb × mb matrix B, their Kronecker product, denoted A ⊗ B, is an
(na nb ) × (na mb ) matrix, whose entries are given by, for ia , ∈ [na ], ja ∈ [ma ], ib ∈ [nb ], jb ∈ [mb ],
A ⊗ B[(ia , ib ), (ja , jb )] = A[ia , ja ] · B[ib , jb ]. For a positive integer k, the Kronecker power A⊗k is the
nka × mka matrix formed by taking the Kronecker product of k copies of A.
    We study circuits for computing the linear transform represented by such a matrix. In other
words, for the matrix family A⊗k for fixed n×n matrix A and growing k, the circuit is given as input
a vector v of length N = nk , and should output the length-N vector A⊗k v. We focus in particular on
linear circuits, whose gates compute fixed linear combinations of their inputs. The straightforward
approach for such a matrix-vector multiplication would use Θ(N 2 ) arithmetic operations, but it’s
known, using the approach of Yates’ algorithm from nearly a century ago [Yat37], that such matrices
have depth-2 linear circuits of size O(N 1.5 ) and depth-O(log N ) linear circuits of size O(N log N ).
    Two of the most prominent families of Kronecker powers are the Walsh–Hadamard transform
Hk := H1⊗k , and the disjointness matrix Rk := R1⊗k , for the fixed matrices

Yates’ algorithm applied to Hk is known as the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform, and has numerous
applications in algorithm design and complexity theory, particularly in compression, encryption, and
signal processing. It is also used as a subroutine in the fastest Fourier transform algorithm [AR23].
Rk is also naturally widespread, particularly in communication complexity, where it is the commu-
nication matrix of disjointness. Its linear transform, sometimes known as the “zeta transform for the
lattice (2[n] , ⊆)”, is used throughout lattice algorithms, as well as in algorithms for solving systems
of polynomial equations [BKW19].

1.1    Depth-2 Linear Circuits
We focus in this paper specifically on depth-2 circuits for a few reasons. First, there is a known way
to convert depth-2 circuits for Kronecker powers into smaller, higher-depth circuits:
Lemma 1.1 (See [AGP23, Lemma 1.1] and [Alm21, Theorem 3.4]). If A is a fixed n × n matrix,
and there is a constant c > 0 such that the matrix A⊗k has a depth-2 circuit of size O(n(1+c)k ) for
all k, then for all integers d > 0, the matrix A⊗k has a depth-2d circuit of size O(d · 2(1+c/d)k ).
All the smallest-known (unbounded-depth) circuits for Kronecker powers can be achieved in this
way (by appropriately picking d = O(k) in Lemma 1.1). Thus, improved depth-2 circuits also
improve the best unbounded-depth circuits, and in particular, the leading constants of algorithms
like Yates’ algorithm and the fast Walsh–Hadamard transform, which are used prominently in both
theory and practice, are directly improved by combining smaller depth-2 circuits with Lemma 1.1.
    Low-depth circuits are also of interest in their own right, for instance because they are easily
evaluated in parallel. Finally, beyond the goal of computing linear transforms, we will later take
advantage of the structure of depth-2 circuits when designing algorithms for other problems.
    A depth-2 linear circuit for an N × N matrix M can be viewed equivalently as a factorization
M = U × V T for N × G matrices U, V . Its size is nnz(U ) + nnz(V ), where nnz denotes the number
of nonzero entries in a matrix; given an input vector v, we may multiply M v = U (V T v) using
O(nnz(U ) + nnz(V )) operations in the straightforward way. Here, G is P  the number of linear gates
in the middle layer of the circuit. We will often equivalently write M = G  i=1 Ui × Vi , where Ui , Vi

are the ith column of U and V respectively, for emphasis: Vi is the linear combination computed
by the ith gate of the circuit, and Ui shows how the circuit outputs use the result of that gate.

1.2   Constructions and Recent Improvements
Yates’ algorithm gives a depth-2 circuit for any Kronecker power of size O(N 1.5 ). For the sparser
disjointness matrix Rn , it gives a smaller depth-2 circuit of size O(N log2 (6)/2 ) < O(N 1.293 ).
     Until recently, it was believed that these Yates’ algorithm circuits were essentially optimal,
and barriers were proved against improving them. Pudlák [Pud00] proved that the Yates’ algo-
rithm circuit for Hk is optimal among “bounded-coefficient” circuits (recent improvements used
unbounded coefficients). Valiant [Val77] also introduced the notion of matrix rigidity toward
proving the optimality of these circuits. A matrix is called rigid if it cannot be written as the
sum of a low-rank matrix and a sparse matrix. Valiant proved that rigid matrices do not have
O(N )-size linear circuits, even up to O(log N )-depth, but it was recently shown that Hk , Rk ,
and many other important families are actually not rigid enough to prove lower bounds in this
way [AW17, DL20, Alm21, Kiv21, BGKM23].
     However, a new line of work gives the first improvements: a circuit for Hk of size O(N 1.443 ) [Alm21,
AGP23], improving size O(N 1.5 ) from Yates’ algorithm, and a circuit for Rk of size O(N 1.251 ) [JS13,
AGP23, Ser22], improving size O(N 1.293 ) from Yates’ algorithm. Both constructions work over every
field F, and come from an improved version of the same high-level approach as Yates’ algorithm:
    1. Start with a fixed depth-2 circuit for a constant Kronecker power, then
    2. Give an approach for converting a circuit for a fixed power into a circuit for all powers.
The new constructions for fixed matrices (component 1) are, with one exception, based on new
matrix rigidity upper bounds. Alman [Alm21] used a new rigidity upper bound for the Kronecker
cube A⊗3 of any fixed 2 × 2 matrix A, then Alman, Guan, and Padaki [AGP23] later extended this
to constant Kronecker powers of any square A (not just 2 × 2). The one exception is Rk . Jukna and
Sergeev [JS13] used the sparsity of Rk to find a much simpler decomposition based on partitioning
the 1s of R1 into two small rectangles. Later work by Alman, Guan, and Padaki [AGP23] and
Sergeev [Ser22] used similar combinatorial decompositions of larger disjointness matrices.
     The approach (component 2) of Yates’ algorithm (and [Alm21]) uses a simple application of the
mixed product property of the Kronecker product:

U, V such that A⊗k0 = U × V T for a fixed k0 . Then for all k, A⊗k has a depth-2 circuit of size

1.3   Unified Approach via Asymptotic Spectrum
In this paper, we give a new approach to designing algorithms and circuits for Kronecker powers,
which both unifies and improves the prior work. A key idea behind our approach is to show that
an appropriate preordered semiring can be defined to capture such depth-2 circuits, so that we may
make use of tools from Strassen’s theory of the asymptotic spectrum.
    Strassen developed this theory in a sequence of works [Str86, Str87, Str88, Str91] toward un-
derstanding the landscape of possible improvements to his breakthrough matrix multiplication al-
gorithm [Str69]. This theory is designed in a very general way, and applies to any complexity
measure on problems which can be captured by a preordered semiring. Many problems with recur-
sive structures can be described in this way, including in graph theory, communication complexity,
and quantum information theory. Ideas from the theory underlie the best known algorithms and
barriers for matrix multiplication today, as well as results in these other areas.
    A centerpiece of the theory is a duality theorem, a generalization of LP duality which can be used
to compute the complexity of problems. Beyond this, many other results about matrix multiplication
and other instances of the asymptotic spectrum also generalize to hold for any instantiation of the
theory. We refer the unfamiliar reader to the recent survey by Wigderson and Zuiddam [WZ25]
which discusses the theory, its applications, and recent developments in great detail.
    We give a new application of Strassen’s theory to capture the design of linear circuits for Kro-
necker power matrix linear transforms. The connection requires a more abstract semiring than in
previous applications of the theory to tensors [Str88] and graphs [Zui19], and is inspired by insights
from rebalancing. Using it, we give improved circuit constructions and find new applications for
them. Among other improvements, we remove the technical restrictions of prior instantiations of
rebalancing (including the need to start with a “sufficiently imbalanced” circuit in Lemma 1.3), and
can thus make use of any fixed constructions. In hindsight, we find that the implementations of
rebalancing from recent work were proving special cases of Strassen’s duality theorem. We will
describe our new approach in more detail shortly in Section 2 below.
    Our approach has applications in a number of different settings which we describe next.

1.4   Application: Circuits for Disjointness

a depth-2 linear circuit of size O(N 1.249424 ), and more generally, for any even d, a depth-d linear
circuit of size O(N 1+0.498848/d ).

1.5    Application: Circuits for Non-Kronecker-Power Matrices
Perhaps surprisingly, our Theorem 1.1 achieves a better exponent than 1.25. The authors previ-
ously believed this wouldn’t be possible for two reasons. First, numerically, the exponents of prior
constructions appeared to be converging to 1.25 (see Table 1). Second, because of the generality of
Rn , many other problems can be reduced to designing circuits for Rn , and 1.25 is the threshold at
which these reductions give new, general results. Indeed, using our construction, we prove:
Corollary 1.1 (Corollary 5.3). For any positive integer n, any field F, and any function f : {0, 1}n →
F, let N = 2n , and consider the matrix Mf ∈ FN ×N which is given by, for x, y ∈ {0, 1}n ,
                               Mf [x, y] = f (x1 ∨ y1 , x2 ∨ y2 , . . . , xn ∨ yn ).
Then, Mf has a depth-4 circuit of size O(N 1.249424 ), and more generally, for any even d, a depth-2d
linear circuit of size O(N 1+0.498848/d ).
    Many prominent families of matrices can be written in the form Mf for different choices of f .
For instance, Kronecker products of sequences of 2×2 matrices can be expressed in this form. Yates’
algorithm gives a depth-4 circuit for these Kronecker products of size O(N 1.25 ), and our Corollary 1.1
gives a new proof of the recent fact that Kronecker powers have smaller depth-4 circuits than what
is given by Yates’ algorithm. The previous proof of this [Alm21] critically used matrix rigidity upper
bounds; we instead combine a combinatorial decomposition of Rn with our asymptotic spectrum
approach. We also note that the previous constructions had unbounded coefficients in all four
layers of the circuit, whereas our new construction only has unbounded entries in one of the layers.
(Pudlák’s barrier [Pud00] shows that unbounded coefficients are needed somewhere in the circuit
to beat Yates’ algorithm.)
    That said, many matrices which are not Kronecker products can also be expressed as Mf . For
one example, picking f to be the majority function gives Hamming distance matrices previously
studied in the context of communication complexity and matrix rigidity [AL25], and whose linear
transform can be used to solve a basic nearest neighbor search problem. Our Corollary 1.1 gives a
generic improvement to O(N 1.2495 ).

Conjecture 1.1 (Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC)). For every ε > 0, there is a c > 0 such
that OV for dimension d = c log n requires time Ω(n2−ε ).

    OVC is implied by the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) [IP01, Wil05], and most
known consequences of SETH in fine-grained complexity are proved using OVC; see the survey by
Vassilevska Williams for many examples in diverse areas of algorithm design [VW18].

    OV has an equivalent formulation using Rd : Given input vectors x, y ∈ {0, 1}2 with n nonzero
entries each, the counting version #OV asks us to compute y T Rd x, while the decision version OV
asks whether this quantity is nonzero. Applying Yates’ algorithm yields another folklore bound of
O(n + d · 2d ), which is faster in small dimensions where d < 2 log n. A faster, alternative analysis
has also been given when the inputs are low Hamming weight vectors [BHKK09].
    Recently, two different papers have given algorithms for OV focusing on an intermediate dimen-
sion regime. They improve the previous algorithms when d = c log n for 1.3 < c < 4.8.
    Nederlof and Węgrzycki [NW21, Section 6] provided a randomized algorithm for a special case
of OV, but their approach generalizes to give a randomized algorithm for OV in dimension d running
in time Õ(n · 1.155d ). Their algorithm is based on a random hashing approach, and they use it as
a critical subroutine of a fast, space-efficient algorithm for subset sum. See [NW21, Section 1.1] for
a discussion of how many prior algorithms for subset sum can also be interpreted as reducing to
moderate-dimension OV.
    Williams [Wil24] gave a new deterministic algorithm running in time Õ(n · 1.35d ) for OV, and
Õ(n · 1.38d ) for #OV. His algorithm is based on a new measure of complexity of a matrix called
its equality rank ; he uses SAT solvers to find equality rank upper bounds for fixed Rd matrices,
then combines these with a Yates’ algorithm-like approach. Based on these algorithms and other
connections with threshold circuits, Williams expresses optimism that Rd could have small enough
equality rank that this approach would yield an algorithm with running time n·2o(d) and thus refute
the OVC; see [Wil24, page 4].
    We observe that depth-2 circuits for Rd can be used to design OV algorithms in this intermediate
dimension regime, and furthermore that both prior algorithms [NW21, Wil24] can (with work) be
interpreted as giving depth-2 circuits for Rd . We approach OV as a special case of the sparse vector-
matrix-vector multiplication problem for Rd (see Sections 2 and 7). This problem is also known as
the “pair problem” in previous literature [Ned20a, Section 5], where alternative matrices are studied
in the context of Hamiltonian path problems [BCKN15, CKN18, Ned20b].
    Using our new, improved depth-2 circuit constructions, we improve these algorithms, perhaps
taking a step toward Williams’ goal.

Theorem 1.2 (Corollary 7.1). #OV can be solved in deterministic Õ(n · 1.26d ) time.

   By comparison, for #OV, [Wil24] achieved deterministic O(n · 1.38d ) time.

1.7    Application: Further Algorithms
In a similar way, depth-2 circuits for other matrices are able to give faster algorithms for other
problems. As an example, we also design new algorithms for counting orthogonal vectors modulo m.
In this problem, denoted #OVZm , one is given as input u1 , . . . , un , v1 , . . . , vn ∈ Zdm , and must count
the number of i, j ∈ [n] for which ⟨ui , vj ⟩ ≡ 0 (mod m). #OVZm has applications to finite geometry,
coding theory, and randomness extractors; see [WY14, Section 1]. Again, the straightforward
running time is O(n2 · d) and Yates’ algorithm gives Õ(n + md ). Our approach applied to the
depth-2 circuit from Yates’ algorithm gives Õ(n · md/2 ), but we are able to further improve this:

Theorem 1.4 (Corollary 7.3). The problem #OVZ2 can be solved in Õ(n · 1.36d ) time. Moreover,
the problem #OVZm can be solved in Õ(n · m(1/2−am )d ) time, where am = Ω(1/m2 log m).

     Our algorithm for #OVZm is derived by applying our approach to constructions of depth-2 linear
circuits for the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) from prior work [AGP23]. Notably, these circuits
in turn make use of matrix rigidity upper bounds for discrete Fourier transforms [DL20, AGP23].
     We note that there is a known algorithm running in time O(n · dm−1 ) for the decision problem
OVZm when m is a prime power [WY14]. That algorithmic approach, which uses matrix product ver-
ification to detect orthogonal pairs, does not appear to work for the counting problem #OVZm , and
moreover, a running time of O(n·dm−1 ) for #OVZm would actually refute the OVC (Conjecture 1.1)
by a known reduction [Wil18a, Theorem 5].

1.8    Application: Circuit Lower Bounds
In light of Theorem 1.1 and other recent improved depth-2 circuit constructions for Hn and Rn [JS13,
Alm21, AGP23, Ser22], it is natural to wonder what the true exponent is, and whether a depth-2
circuit of size N 1+o(1) for Hn or Rn may be possible. Using our new connection with the approach
of Williams [Wil24], we prove that this would refute popular conjectures in fine-grained complexity:

Theorem 1.5 (Theorem D.1, D.2). The Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC) or the Strong Ex-
ponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) each imply that the Walsh–Hadamard matrix Hd of side-length
N does not have a depth-2 linear circuit of size N 1+o(1) . A similar result holds for the disjointness
matrix Rd in place of Hd (with a restriction that the Rd circuit is somewhat balanced).

By comparison, the best known unconditional lower bounds on the size of a depth-2 circuit for an
explicit function is Ω(N log2 N/ log log N ) for matrices based on superconcentrator graphs [RTS00],
and the best for Hd is Ω(N log N ) [AKW90].
   We also give evidence that proving such a circuit lower bound would be difficult, by showing
that it would imply a breakthrough lower bound for depth-2 threshold circuits for computing single-
output Boolean functions {0, 1}n → {0, 1}. A SUM ◦ ETHR circuit is a depth-2 circuit where the

                                                      6
bottom gates are exact threshold gates, which test whether a prescribed linear combination of their
inputs over R is equal to a fixed target, and the top gate simply outputs a linear combination of
its inputs. Proving super-polynomial size lower bounds on such circuits has been a major open
problem for decades [ROS94, Wil18b]; see also [Wil24, Section 2]. We show that a depth-2 linear
circuit lower bound would yield a subexponential depth-2 threshold circuit lower bound:
Theorem 1.6 (Theorem D.3, D.4). If Rd of side-length N does not have a depth-2 linear circuit of
size N 1+o(1) , then Boolean Inner Product on n-bit vectors does not have a 2o(n) -size SUM ◦ ETHR
circuit. The same holds for Hd and Boolean Inner Product Mod 2.

Proof via Strassen’s Duality Our proof of the duality theorem begins with the key observation
that the theory of the asymptotic spectrum can capture the optimal balancing of depth-2 circuits.
In brief, the asymptotic spectrum considers a commutative semiring R equipped with a compatible
preorder ⩽ (the Strassen preorder ), which preserves both addition and multiplication (a ≤ b implies
a+c ≤ b+c and a·c ≤ b·c) along with some additional technical conditions we discuss in Section 3.4
below. For this preorder, the rank of an element a ∈ R is defined as R(a) = min{n ∈ N : a ⩽ n},
and this rank encodes the information of interest. See Section 3.4 for the detailed definitions.
    Unlike previous applications of the asymptotic spectrum to tensors [Str88] and graphs [Zui19],
where the semirings involve concrete elements, our approach requires a more abstract semiring. Let

Disjointness Matrix Since Rd is a binary matrix, one approach to finding a decomposition of

Improving the Degree Let C be a depth-2 circuit for M that expresses M as M = U V T . Let
nnz(Ui ) and nnz(Vi ) denote the degrees of the ith output and input gates of C, respectively (i.e.,
the ith row of U and V ). We define the degree of C as the maximum of all these degrees.
    We are interested in the problem of sparse vector-matrix-vector multiplication: given a matrix
M and two vectors x and y, each with at most n nonzero entries, compute xT M y. This problem has
a natural connection to problems like #OV, as the d-bit #OV problem can be reduced to a sparse
vector-matrix-vector multiplication instance as described above. In this reduction, the matrix M is
the disjointness matrix Rd , and x and y are the indicator vectors of the input sets.
    Intuitively, the sparse vector-matrix-vector multiplication problem can be solved in Õ(n · D)
time, where D is the degree of the circuit for M . The idea is to compute the inner product from
two vector-matrix multiplication results: xT M y = (xT U )(y T V )T . Since each row of U and V has at
most D nonzero entries, the vector-matrix multiplication yields at most nD nonzero entries, which
can be computed in Õ(nD) time. To formalize this intuition, we use the Kronecker power structure
of Rd , enabling efficient identification of the nonzero entries in each row of U and V using a fixed
decomposition. Therefore, a low degree decomposition of Rd yields an efficient algorithm for #OV.

Fixing Holes Let M ′ = M ⊗d having N = nd rows and M = md columns, and C ′ be a circuit
computing M ′ |S,T for some |S| ≥ (1 − o(1))N and |T | ≥ (1 − o(1))M, where the rows and columns

to extend the circuit C ′ to a circuit for the whole M ′ .

by permuting the rows and columns of C ′ according to σ. Then the circuit σ(C ′ ) is a circuit for

σ(T ) will overlap with S ′ × T ′ in a large portion, and the circuit σ(C ′ ) can be used to compute the
submatrix M ′ |S ′ ∩σ(S),T ′ ∩σ(T ) . Therefore, the task of computing M ′ over S ′ × T ′ is already resolved
in a large portion, and the rest part breaks into two smaller subrectangles
                              (S ′ ∖ σ(S)) × T ′ ,   (S ′ ∩ σ(S)) × (T ′ ∖ σ(T )).

transform matrices, which have a more general type of full symmetry where one may also need to

In this subsection we introduce Strassen’s theory of asymptotic spectra [Str88]. We follow the
presentation in the survey of Wigderson and Zuiddam [WZ25].
     In this paper, a commutative semiring (R, +, ·, 0, 1) is a set R with two operations + and · such
that
    1. Addition (R, +, 0) is a commutative semigroup with identity 0.
    2. Multiplication (R, ·, 1) is a commutative semigroup with identity 1.
    3. Multiplication distributes over addition, i.e., for any a, b, c ∈ R, we have a · (b + c) = a · b + a · c.
For any nonnegative integer n, we recognize n ∈ R as the sum of n copies of 1. We write ab as a
shorthand for a · b, and an as the n-fold product of a.
     A preorder ⩽ on R is called a semiring preorder if it satisfies the following properties for all
a, b, c ∈ R
    1. 0 ⩽ a.                                                                                (Nonnegative)
    2. a ⩽ a .                                                                                   (Reflexive)
    3. a ⩽ b and b ⩽ c implies a ⩽ c.                                                           (Transitive)
    4. a ⩽ b implies a + c ⩽ b + c.                                                               (Additive)
    5. a ⩽ b implies a · c ⩽ b · c.                                                         (Multiplicative)

Definition 3.1 (Strassen preorder). A semiring preorder ⩽ on a commutative semiring R is called
a Strassen preorder if it satisfies the following additional properties:
   1. For any nonnegative integers n, m, we have n ⩽ m in R if and only if n ≤ m. (Embedding of
      natural numbers)
   2. For any a ∈ R ∖ {0}, there exists a positive integer n such that 1 ⩽ a ⩽ n.       (Strong
      Archimedean property)

such that a ⩽ n, denoted as R(a). The asymptotic rank of a is the limit

4.2   Discussion
In this section, we proved a duality theorem for depth-2 circuits via Strassen’s duality theory.
However, the proof of Strassen’s duality invokes Zorn’s lemma (which is equivalent to the Axiom
of Choice) and is a proof by contradiction, so one cannot directly extract an effective bound on the
circuit size from the proof. In particular, when this is applied to a specific matrix M , one could
deduce the a bound an+o(n) on the size of the depth-2 circuits for M ⊗n , but one cannot write down
an explicit expression for the o(n) in the exponent, and it is not clear how to construct a family of
circuits achieving the asymptotic bound (although, in principle, a brute-force search algorithm can
eventually find the smallest depth-2 circuit).
    This situation is reminiscent of Szemerédi’s theorem [Sze75] in additive combinatorics. Let rk (N )
denote the size of the largest subset of [N ] that avoids k-term arithmetic progressions. Szemerédi’s
theorem asserts that rk (N ) = ok (N ) for any k ≥ 3. Furstenberg [Fur77] later provided an alternative
proof using ergodic theory—a method that has since become a powerful tool in tackling other
combinatorial problems. However, his proof is nonconstructive and does not yield an explicit bound
on rk (N ). Subsequent work [Tao06] has shown that modifying the proof to obtain a constructive
version and extract explicit bounds requires significant additional effort.


                                                     21
   For this reason, we also provide an alternative proof of the duality theorem for depth-2 circuits
by directly analyzing the rebalancing process and arguing that it achieves the desired bound, in
Appendix B below. The proof provided there is constructive and elementary, and thus might be of
independent interest.
   Finally, we emphasize that Theorem 4.1 in this section establishes a connection between the
asymptotic size of depth-2 circuits and the α-volume of all possible decompositions of the matrix.
While this suffices for our primary objective of improving upper bounds on circuit size, it still does
not exactly answer one of our initial questions: If we restrict to a specific family of decompositions,
what is the “best” way to construct a circuit for M ⊗n via the rebalancing process?
   In principle, this refinement can also be addressed using the theory of asymptotic spectra. To
do so, one modifies the definition of the preorder ⩽M by restricting the comparison relations to
the chosen family of decompositions. Under this adaptation, the rank function rM would then
characterize the circuit size achievable through an optimal rebalancing strategy.
   In fact, our alternative proof in Appendix B adopts this perspective. There, we prove a duality
theorem applicable to any finite family of decompositions (indeed, the meaning of constructiveness
becomes unclear when handling infinitely many decompositions). Finally, by employing a standard
topological argument to extend the result to the space of all decompositions, we can also recover
Theorem 4.1 from that constructive proof.

Theorem D.1. The Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture implies that the Walsh–Hadamard matrix Hd
of side-length N does not have a depth-2 linear circuit of size N 1+o(1) .
Proof. Suppose Hd has a depth-2 linear circuit of size N 1+o(1) , i.e., that σ(H) = 2. Since H is the
DFT matrix of Z2 , by Corollary 6.1, we have δ(H) = 1. By Theorem 7.2, #OVZ2 can be solved
in Õ(n2ϵd ) time for any ϵ > 0. In other words, for any c > 0 and ϵ > 0, the #OVZ2 problem
over dimension d = c log n can be solved in time n1+ϵ . Using a reduction from #OV to #OVZ2 of
Williams [Wil18a, Corollary 6], it follows that #OV in dimension O(log n) can be solved in time
n1+o(1) , which contradicts the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture.
