     A Z2–Topological Framework for Sign-rank Lower Bounds
                Florian Frick∗             Kaave Hosseini†                Aliaksei Vasileuski‡

                                                April 9, 2026



                                                   Abstract
        We develop a topological framework for proving lower bounds on sign-rank via Z2 –equivariant
    topology, and use it to resolve the sign-rank of the Gap Hamming Distance problem up to lower-
    order terms.
        For every (partial) sign matrix A, we associate a free Z2 –simplicial complex S(A) and show
    that sign-rank of A is characterized by the linear analog of Z2 -index of S(A). As a conse-
    quence, the classical Z2 –index of S(A) lower bounds the sign-rank of A, which reduces sign-rank
    lower bounds to topological obstructions. This reduction allows us to use various tools from
    Z2 –equivariant topology, particularly in regimes where classical lower-bound techniques break
    down.
        As the main application, we consider the Gap Hamming Distance function GHDnk (defined
    for k < n/2), which distinguishes pairs of strings in {0, 1}n with Hamming distance at most k
    from pairs with distance at least n − k. We prove an essentially tight lower bound and show
    that for any k,
                                    sign-rank(GHDnk ) = (1 − ok (1)) 2k.

    Hosseini, and Meng (STOC 2023) who proved that sign-rank of GHDnk is at least Ω(k/ log(n/k)).
        A key technical ingredient is a new analysis of the Z2 -coindex (which lower bounds Z2 -index)
    of the Vietoris–Rips complex of the hypercube in the sparse regime which yields an essentially

1    Introduction
The sign-rank of a partial matrix A, denoted rank± (A), is the minimum rank of any real matrix B
such that sign(Bij ) = Aij whenever Aij ∈ {−1, +1}. Sign-rank is a fundamental complexity
measure in theoretical computer science and mathematics, yet despite decades of study, it remains
notoriously difficult to analyze. There are three known general lower bound tools for sign-rank,
which can be summarized as VC dimension [PS86], variants of Forster’s method based on spectral
norm/average margin [For02, FKL+ 01, FS06, RS10, She11, LMSS07, HHP+ 22], and rectangle
based methods [APP+ 05, HHP+ 22]. However, these lower bounds are effective only when the given
matrix contains a large pseudorandom piece (i.e., has large VC dimension, or spectral norm is
small, or it does not contain large monochromatic rectangles) and there are N by N matrices of
sign-rank poly(N ) where these methods cannot produce any Ω(1) lower bound [HHP+ 22]. The
reader is referred to [HHP+ 22] for an in-depth discussion of limitations of these methods. This lack
of flexible tools to lower bound sign-rank without requiring pseudorandomness has stalled progress
on many fundamental problems about sign-rank.
     A benchmark problem to study sign-rank is the Gap Hamming Distance problem, which is a
partial function GHDnk : {0, 1}n × {0, 1}n → {−1, 1, ∗} defined for a parameter k < n/2 by:

Here dH (x, y) is the Hamming distance between x and y. Gap Hamming Distance is a fundamental
object and has been intensively studied in different parameter regimes, used as a lower bound prim-
itive in randomized communication complexity and streaming algorithms [IW03, BCR+ 10, CR11,
She12, JW13], separating the power of communication models [PSS14, CLV19, Gav25, BHH+ 25a],
separating partial and total learnability of half-spaces with margin [AHHM22, CMW25, BHH+ 25a,
CCMY24], and lower bounds for dimensionality reduction[HHM23]. Further more, Gap Hamming
Distance is the XOR lift of the approximate majority function with a broad range of applications
in circuit complexity and query complexity, and it is also the discrete analog of the concept class
of half-spaces with margin which is a canonical object in learning theory.
     Next we briefly discuss the sign-rank of GHDnk in different parameter regimes. In the case of odd
n and k = (n − 1)/2, GHDnk is the XOR lift of the exact Majority function, and has VC dimension
n which implies rank± (GHDnk ) = n. More generally, the function GHDnk has been studied in two
parameter regimes:

GHDnk is still mildly pseudorandom in the sense that it does not contain large monochromatic

    As the main technical ingredient, we give a new analysis of the Z2 -coindex of Vietoris–Rips
(VR) complex of the n-hypercube. These are central objects in metric topology which we briefly
discuss now. For a metric space X and scale parameter k ≥ 0, the Vietoris–Rips complex VR(X, k)
is the simplicial complex of all finite subsets of X of diameter at most k. Thus this complex pro-
vides a combinatorial-topological encoding of pairwise metric proximity. It was first introduced
by Vietoris [Vie27] to define a homology theory for metric spaces, and later used by Rips and
Gromov [Gro87] in the context of geometric group theory to study hyperbolic groups. More re-
cently, topological properties of VR complexes have been studied in the computational topology
community in the context of persistent homology [Car09, EH10, Ghr08]. Despite their widespread
use and popularity, the topology of VR complexes is poorly understood: only partial results are
known for VR complexes of various manifolds [ABV25] and of hypercubes, for example. For hyper-
cubes [AA22, AV24, Fen25, Shu23] exact results are only known in the regime k ≤ 3, along with
asymptotic results for k ≥ n/2. Here we prove essentially tight lower bounds for the Z2 -coindex of
VR complexes of n-hypercubes for all k < n/2.
    Another limitation of the Borsuk-Ulam argument of [HHM23] is that it is highly tailored to the
specific structure of GHDnk and it is not clear whether one can use topology to study the sign-rank
of arbitrary sign matrices. This naturally raises the following meta-question:

Is there a systematic topological framework for sign-rank lower bounds of arbitrary sign matrices?

    In this paper we build a framework using Z2 -topology to lower bound the sign-rank of any sign
matrix. First, we introduce a correspondence between partial sign matrices and free Z2 –simplicial
complexes, and reinterpret sign-rank as the linear analogue of Z2 -index. This turns lower bounds
on sign-rank into questions about equivariant topology. Z2 -index is a global topological invariant
amenable to a broad range of equivariant methods far beyond the Borsuk–Ulam theorem, which
will be discussed later in the paper.

complex of a sign matrix. For more details see Section 3. The reader is referred to Section 2.2 for
all the necessary background on Z2 -equivariant topology.

1.1   The main framework
Given a matrix A ∈ {−1, 1, ∗}M ×N with columns indexed by [N ], we construct an abstract simplicial
complex called the sign complex denoted by S(A). This is a simplicial complex built on two copies
of the column set, namely
                                     {1− , 1+ , . . . , N − , N + }.
For each row r ∈ {−1, 1, ∗}N , define the simplex
                              σr+ := { i+ : ri = +1 } ∪ { i− : ri = −1 },

                              σr− := { i− : ri = +1 } ∪ { i+ : ri = −1 }.

    Corollary 2 allows one to use the full machinery of Z2 -equivariant topology to prove sign-rank
lower bounds. Index lower bound techniques include Borsuk–Ulam type arguments [Bor33, Lov78,
Mat03], nerve lemmas and connectivity [Mat03], the Fadell–Husseini cohomological index / Stiefel–
Whitney classes [FH88, BZ17, Mat03], equivariant obstruction theory [BZ17], and discrete Morse
theory [For98]. In particular, our framework allows one to apply topological methods in settings
where classical sign-rank techniques are ineffective.
    Moreover, the correspondence between free Z2 -simplicial complexes and partial sign-matrices is
in fact two-way: every free Z2 –simplicial complex K is isomorphic to S(AK ) for some partial sign
matrix AK . If K has 2N vertices and M facets, then AK has N columns and M rows. Moreover,
AK is a total sign matrix iff all facets of K have size N . Hence, in order to upper bound the index
of K, it is enough to upper bound the sign-rank of AK . This viewpoint can be beneficial in settings
where topology of K is difficult to study but AK is amenable to sign-rank upper bound methods
including sign alternating number [AFR85], polynomial threshold degree [KS01], support rank
[GHIS25], communication protocols [PS86], protocols with Hamming oracles[HHP+ 22, GHIS25],
and analytic parameters such as factorization norm [HHP+ 22].
    As our main application, we combine several tools from Z2 -topology to obtain an essentially
tight lower bound on the sign-rank of GHDnk for all k < n/2 which is discussed next.

1.3   VC dimension and coindex
The V C dimension of a matrix A ∈ {−1, 1, ∗}M ×N is the largest number d for which there exists
a set of columns S ⊆ [N ] with |S| = d such that every sign pattern P ∈ {−1, 1}S appears as the
restriction of some row of A to S. It is well known that by Radon’s theorem, V C(A) provides a
lower bound on sign-rank. However, this bound is typically weak as VC dimension only captures
local combinatorial structure: there exist matrices with constant VC dimension whose sign-rank is
polynomially large [AMY16].
    Here, VC dimension of our sign complex framework has a natural topological interpretation.
Recall that the vertex set of the sign complex S(A) is

                                      {1+ , 1− , . . . , N + , N − },

and that S(A) is a subcomplex of the boundary of the crosspolytope ∂♢N . Let ω♢ (K) of a free
Z2 -complex be the largest d for which the complex K has ∂♢d as an induced free Z2 –subcomplex.
Then it is easy to see that
                                  ω♢ (S(A))
                                            ≤ VC(A) ≤ ω♢ (S(A))
                                      2
See Theorem 26. Since ∂♢d is homeomorphic to the sphere Sd−1 with the antipodal action, its
presence inside S(A) immediately implies a lower bound on the Z2 –coindex, namely, the largest k
for which there is a continuous Z2 -equivariant map from Sk to |S(A)|.

Proposition 6. For every partial sign matrix A,

                                   VC(A) − 1 ≤ coindZ2 (S(A)).

   Overall, we obtain the following chain of inequalities:

                 VC(A) − 1 ≤ coindZ2 (S(A)) ≤ indZ2 (S(A)) ≤ rank± (A) − 1.                     (5)

Hence the classical VC lower bound on sign-rank appears in our framework as a very special
case of a topological obstruction: it corresponds (up to a factor of 2) to the existence of a large
antipodal crosspolytope embedded inside the sign complex. However, the topological viewpoint
is considerably more flexible. While VC dimension only detects the presence of a crosspolytope
boundary inside S(A), the Z2 –index can capture much more complicated global topology. This
additional power is crucial for obtaining strong lower bounds for sign-rank in settings where VC
dimension remains constant.
     A similar inequality as in Proposition 6 has also been independently introduced by [CMW25]
in the context of learning theory where they associate a simplicial complex similar to the sign
complex, to a total concept class and relate its coindex to VC dimension, list replicability, and
many other important learnability parameters. Also in [BHH+ 25b], coindex has been proven to
lower bound simplicial covering dimension which is another fundamental parameter characterizing
list replicability. Both of these works have led to powerful applications in learning theory.

    Nevertheless, even though coindex is useful for lower bounding fundamental learning parameters,
but Z2 -index captures more global obstructions and can give stronger sign-rank lower bounds. In
Section 6.3 we use classical constructions in topology that give sign complexes with coindex equal to
1, but index arbitrarily large. This is done via another topological invariant called Stiefel-Whitney
height that sits between coindex and index.

1.4   Separation results

A basic remaining question we need to understand is to what extent the complexity measures in
the chain of inequalities in Equation (5) can be separated.

Separating Z2 -index and sign-rank. We split to the case of total matrices and the more
general case of partial matrices.

  1. Total matrices. We show that a random N × N total sign matrix A has indZ2 (S(A)) =
     O(log N ) with high probability, while its sign-rank is Ω(N ) [AFR85]. Similarly we show that
     for N = 2n , the N by N Hadamard matrix HN has indZ2 (S(HN )) at most 2 log N . However,√
     it is already known, from seminal work of Forster [For02] that its sign-rank is at least N .
      More generally, we introduce a combinatorial parameter, for an arbitrary free Z2 -complex K,
      as the height of its associated partial sign matrix AK , and show that indZ2 (K) is at most twice
      its height. This provides a systematic way to obtain logarithmic upper bounds on Z2 -index
      even when the underlying linear dimension is large. For more details, see Section 6.1.

  2. Partial matrices. We construct random N ×N partial matrices built on top of the incidence√
     matrix of the finite projective plane that has Z2 -index equal to 1, but has sign-rank Ω( N ).
     For more details, see Section 6.2.

Question 7. Does there exist a function f : N → N such that for every total sign matrix A,

                                    rank± (A) ≤ f (indZ2 (S(A)))?

topology. Some of these problems will be discussed in conclusion in Section 7.

2     Background
In this section we introduce the necessary background on sign-rank, Gap Hamming Distance, and
Z2 -topology.

2.1   Sign-rank
The sign-rank of a total sign matrix AM ×N ∈ {−1, 1}M ×N , is the minimum rank of a real matrix
BM ×N such that
                          sign(Bx,y ) = Ax,y  for all (x, y) ∈ [M ] × [N ].
This definition extends naturally to partial sign matrices: if Ax,y = ∗, then the corresponding entry
Bx,y may be any real number. In other words, the sign-rank of A ∈ {−1, 1, ∗}M ×N , denoted by
rank± (A), is the smallest integer d for which there exist vectors

                      u1 , . . . , um ∈ Rd \ {0}   and       v1 , . . . , vn ∈ Rd \ {0}

such that for every specified entry Aij ∈ {±1} we require
                                                       
                                        sign ⟨ui , vj ⟩ = Aij .

Equivalently, each row vector ui defines the homogeneous hyperplane

                                      Hi := {x ∈ Rd : ⟨ui , x⟩ = 0},

and it strictly separates the column-point sets

                        Ci+ := {vj : Aij = +1},          Ci− := {vj : Aij = −1},

in the sense that ⟨ui , v⟩ > 0 for all v ∈ Ci+ and ⟨ui , v⟩ < 0 for all v ∈ Ci− . This viewpoint is
used in learning theory, where sign-rank is known as dimension complexity, capturing the smallest
dimension d in which a concept class can be realized as a family of half-spaces and points in Rd .

2.2   Background on Z2 -Topology
Here we discuss a minimal background on Z2 -topology that we need to use, and refer the reader to
[Mat03, Koz08, DL13] for more details.

                                                   10
Abstract simplicial complex.          An abstract simplicial complex K on a finite vertex set V is a
family K ⊆ 2V such that:

   1. {v} ∈ K for every v ∈ V ,

   2. if σ ∈ K and τ ⊆ σ, then τ ∈ K.

Elements σ ∈ K are called simplices. The dimension of σ is dim(σ) = |σ| − 1, and dim(K) is the
maximum dimension of a simplex in K. A facet of K is a maximal simplex under inclusion.
   As a basic example, the full d-dimensional simplex is the complex

                                              ∆d := 2[d+1] ,

i.e., it consists of all subsets of a (d + 1)-element vertex set.
     The k-skeleton of K is the subcomplex

                                    K (k) := {σ ∈ K : dim(σ) ≤ k}.

    Let K and L be simplicial complexes on vertex sets V (K) and V (L). A map on vertices
φ : V (K) → V (L) induces a simplicial map φ : K → L if for every simplex σ ∈ K the image
φ(σ) := {φ(v) : v ∈ σ} is a simplex of L (i.e. φ(σ) ∈ L). The simplicial bijection φ is called an
isomorphism if
                                   σ ∈ K ⇐⇒ φ(σ) ∈ L.
In which case, we say that K and L are isomorphic and write K ∼   = L.
    A Z2 -action on K is a simplicial map τ : K → K such that τ 2 = id (equivalently, τ (τ (v)) = v
for all vertices v). Furthermore, the map τ is free if τ (σ) ̸= σ for any simplex σ ∈ K. A free
Z2 -action is also called an involution.
    Let (K, τK ) and (L, τL ) be simplicial complexes equipped with free Z2 -actions. A simplicial
map f : K → L is called Z2 -equivariant if it commutes with the involutions, that is,

                          f (τK (v)) = τL (f (v))   for every vertex v ∈ V (K).

Equivalently,
                                             f ◦ τK = τL ◦ f
as maps of simplicial complexes.
    Let K be a simplicial complex with an involution τ : K → K. It follows that no simplex of K
contains both v and τ (v) for any vertex v, hence K can be viewed as a subcomplex of the boundary
of the crosspolytope which is discussed next.
    Given simplicial complexes K and L on disjoint vertex sets, their join K ∗ L is defined as

                                   K ∗ L := {σ ∪ τ : σ ∈ K, τ ∈ L}.

If K and L do not necessarily have disjoint vertex sets, their deleted join is

                 K ∗∆ L := {(σ × {1}) ∪ (τ × {2}) : σ ∈ K, τ ∈ L, and σ ∩ τ = ∅}.

Connectivity. A standard method to provide a lower bound for Z2 -index of a topological space
X is to show that X is highly connected. A topological space X is r-connected if for every k ≤ r,

                                         f : Sk −→ X

carries over as well. This is particularly useful in a scenario where Y is a Z2 -space of low or unknown

Corollary 12. Let Y be a Z2 -space. Suppose that there exists a r-connected Z2 -space X admitting
Z2 -equivariant map f : X → Y . Then indZ2 (Y ) ≥ (r + 1).

in at most one vertex. Define the simplicial map f : sd(K) → G from the barycentric subdivision
of K to G by f (σ) = σ if σ is a vertex and f (σ) = F (σ) otherwise. To see that f is a simplicial
map let σ0 ⊂ · · · ⊂ σk be a chain of faces in K. If σ0 is not a vertex, then f (σi ) = F (σi ) = F (σ0 ),
so the face {σ0 , . . . , σk } of sd(K) is mapped to the vertex F (σ0 ) in G. If σ0 is a vertex and k ≥ 1
(the case k = 0 being trivial) then F (σ1 ) = · · · = F (σk ) and the image of the face {σ0 , . . . , σk } is
the edge {σ0 , F (σ1 )} of G.
    Clearly, the map f is Z2 -equivariant and G has dimension one. Thus indZ2 (K) ≤ indZ2 (G) ≤
dim(G) ≤ 1.

6.3   Index vs. coindex
For the sake of completeness, we include the following separation between coindex and index. The
odd-dimensional real projective spaces RP2N −1 is the space of 1-dimensional real subspaces of CN ,
which carries a free Z/2-action induced by multiplication with i. Stolz [Sto89] determined the
Z/2-index of RP2N −1 as N + 1, N + 2, or N + 3, depending on the remainder of N modulo 8.
The coindex of a Z2 -space X space is bounded from above by the Stiefel–Whitney height, that is,
the largest integer N such that the N th power of the Stiefel–Whitney class of the double cover
X → X/Z2 is non-zero in mod-2 cohomology. The Stiefel–Whitney height of RP2N −1 with a free
Z2 -action is one; see Singh [Sin11].
    On the other hand, Matsushita [Mat17] constructs free Z2 -spaces with coindex one and ar-
bitrarily large Stiefel–Whitney height. The Stiefel–Whitney height in turn is a lower bound for
the Z2 -index. To summarize, denoting Stiefel–Whitney height of a Z2 -space X by swh(X) all
inequalities in
                                  coindZ2 (X) ≤ swh(X) ≤ indZ2 (X)
have arbitrarily large separation.

7    Concluding remarks and open questions
Consider the chain of inequalities

                                                                        ±
                     coindZ2 (S(A)) ≤ indZ2 (S(A)) ≤ indlin
                                                        Z2 (S(A)) = rank (A) − 1.
    A downside is that all of these parameters are very hard to compute for a given input matrix
A. Indeed, it is known that checking whether sign-rank is 3 or not is complete for the existential
theory of the reals [Mnë06, BFG+ 09, BK15] which is a class including PSPACE. Also index and
coindex are known to be hard [Mat03].
    However, it is possible to insert more useful topological invariants between coindZ2 and indZ2
that are efficiently computable. For example, the Stiefel–Whitney height of a Z2 -complex K,
denoted by swh(K), also known as cohomological index satisfies
                                         coindZ2 (K) ≤ swh(K) ≤ indZ2 (K)
and we discussed in Section 6.3 that there are K with coindZ2 (K) = 1 and arbitrarily large swh(K).
It is known that swh(K) is efficiently computable in the number of faces of K (see p. 106 of [Mat03])
and therefore, it can be a useful computable lower bound on sign-rank of arbitrary matrices that
is provably stronger than analytic lower bounds, as in the case of Gap Hamming Distance.

                                                                     32
Upper bounds on Z2 -index. We showed that in the case of N by N random matrices and the
Hadamard matrix, Z2 -index of the associated complex is at most O(log N ). We leave the following
as a question, which if true, indicates that the sign complex is not able to prove a strong lower
bound on sign-rank of square matrices, and one needs to construct significantly richer complexes
than the sign complex to prove strong bounds. We phrase the question more generally for strongly
regular free Z2 -CW complexes, which are a broader class than free Z2 -simplicial complexes. In a
strongly regular CW complex the intersection of any two faces is a (possibly empty) face.

Question 37. Is it true that any finite strongly regular free Z2 –CW-complex K with N vertices
and M inclusion maximal cells, satisfies indZ2 (K) ≤ O( log N log M )?

   If true, it implies that for any N by N sign matrix A, indZ2 (S(A)) ≤ O(log2 N ) since S(A) has
2N vertices and at most 2N facets.
   Question 37 may be viewed as a topological generalization of the classical Figiel–Lindenstrauss–
Milman inequality for centrally symmetric polytopes [FLM76].

Theorem 38 (Figiel–Lindenstrauss–Milman theorem [FLM76]). There exists an absolute constant
c > 0 such that for every centrally symmetric polytope P ⊂ Rd with N vertices and M facets,

                                          d ≤ c · log N · log M

   To see the connection, let K = ∂P be the boundary complex of a centrally symmetric polytope
P ⊂ Rd , equipped with the antipodal Z2 -action. Then K is a strongly regular Z2 -CW complex
with N vertices and M facets, and

                                   coindZ2 (K) = indZ2 (K) = d − 1.

Indeed radial projection from ∂P to Sd−1 gives a Z2 -equivariant map |K| = ∂P → Sd−1 .
    Hence a positive answer to Question 37 (even with coindZ2 instead of indZ2 ) would recover The-
orem 38. However, it is significantly stronger, because boundary complexes of centrally symmetric
polytopes form a highly constrained subclass of strongly regular free Z2 -CW complexes with strong
local structure.

Equivalence of index and sign-rank for total matrices. Regardless of the answer to Ques-
tion 37, one may still hope for an equivalence between sign-rank and index. We have already
showed that for partial matrices this is impossible, since

matrix with indZ2 (S(A)) = 1 while rank (A) ≥ Ω( N ). However, we only have an exponential
separation in the case of total matrices and it is still possible that in this case, there is a qualitative
equivalence between index and sign-rank.

Question 7. Does there exist a function f : N → N such that for every total sign matrix A,

                                     rank± (A) ≤ f (indZ2 (S(A)))?

    If the question above has a positive answer, a large number of fundamental problems in sign-
rank literature can be entirely formulated in terms of Z2 -index of free Z2 -simplicial complexes.
Some of these problems include bounding closure properties of sign-rank and equivalence to semi-
algebraic complexity [Lov19, HHP+ 22], the possibility of upper bounding sign-rank based on margin
[LMSS07, HHM23], and upper bounding sign-rank based on factorization norm [HHP+ 22].
