1.1     Problem, Results, and Related Work

1.1.1    Depth-2 Linear Circuits

    The main object of study in this paper is the depth-2 linear circuit. A depth-2 linear circuit is
a linear circuit with 2 + 1 = 3 layers: an input layer, a middle layer, and an output layer. Each
middle gate computes a fixed linear combination of the input gates, and each output gate computes
a fixed linear combination of the middle gates.
   If the three layers of a depth-2 linear circuit C have n, r, and m gates, respectively, then C can
be written as an ordered pair of matrices C = (A, B), where A ∈ Matn×r and B ∈ Matr×m record
the weights from the input gates to the middle gates and from the middle gates to the output gates,
respectively.
   Interpreting the input as a row vector, an input x is transformed as x 7→ xA 7→ xAB. The
input-output behavior of the circuit is therefore exactly the linear transformation represented by
the matrix M = AB, and we say that C computes M .
    For a circuit C = (A, B), its size [JS13] is defined as
                                      S(C) := nnz(A) + nnz(B),
and its maximum input-output degree [AL25, Section 3.2], abbreviated as “degree,” is defined as
                                                                          
                        D(C) := max max nnz(A[x, ∗]), max nnz(B[∗, y]) .
                                          x                   y

Here nnz(·) denotes the number of nonzero entries of a matrix or vector; only edges with nonzero
weight need to be present in the circuit. A detailed discussion of the circuit model appears in
Section A.1.

    A direct way to obtain a depth-2 linear circuit computing Rn is to handpick depth-2 linear
circuits for constant Kronecker powers, which we call base circuits, and simply take their Kronecker
products.
    (a) For optimizing size, starting in fact with [JS13], prior works observed that there is a
construction better than directly taking Kronecker products of base circuits. In the first step,
using a base circuit for Rd , equivalently a rank-1 decomposition Rd = i Ui ViT , one rewrites Rn as
                                                                           P

Rn = i Ui ViT ⊗ Rn−d . If for every i we replace Rn−d by the same circuit, this amounts to directly
       P

taking Kronecker products of several circuits. However, for each i, one can instead choose a different
circuit for Rn−d , based on the data of Ui and Vi . This selection rule is called rebalancing. The works
[JS13, AGP23, Ser22] each proposed heuristic selection rules, while the duality theorem of [AL25]
completely solves the circuit-selection problem.
    (b) For optimizing degree, when one takes Kronecker products of several base circuits, the
degrees of most gates in the resulting product circuit concentrate, by Hoeffding’s inequality, around
the geometric mean of their degrees. However, an exponentially small fraction of gates may have
degrees exceeding this geometric mean by an exponential factor. The work [AL25] exploits the
bit-permutation symmetry of the Kronecker-power matrices themselves to compensate for this loss,
by removing the part lying far outside the concentration window; they call this procedure degree
uniformization.
    The reason the present paper can improve on (a) is that prior works did not combine the
advantages of these two routes. A circuit constructed via the selection rule in (a) may still contain,
as in (b), many gates whose degrees are far above the median. If degree uniformization can be
applied to such a circuit, it may noticeably improve both size and degree. However, analyzing the
degrees of circuits constructed by rebalancing is much harder than for plain Kronecker-product
circuits, and this degree analysis is one of the core technical contributions of our paper. Since the
duality theorem of [AL25] characterizes the infimum of the final size over circuits constructed “only
through rebalancing,” whereas our paper essentially applies degree uniformization once more to
the final constructed circuit, our construction has the potential to break the lower bound that the
duality theorem of [AL25] imposes on rebalancing constructions.

Computation trees and scripted rebalancing. Recall the first step of rebalancing from
Section 1.1.3: if a base circuit gives a rank-1 decomposition Rd = i Ui ViT , then Rn = i Ui ViT ⊗
                                                                   P                      P

Rn−d , and the circuit chosen for the remaining factor Rn−d depends on the leading term Ui ViT .
Iterating this rule expands the one-step identity into a rooted tree: at an internal node x, one
attaches a depth-2 linear circuit C(x) computing Rk ; each rank-1 term of C(x) contributes one
edge to a child; and the subtree below that child defines a circuit for the remaining Kronecker
power. The circuit defined by the whole tree is therefore the sum, over all root-to-leaf paths, of the
Kronecker products of the rank-1 terms attached to the edges along that path. This is exactly the
computation-tree formulation of [AL25, Appendix B]. The circuits CJ , CS , CR , CRT are all defined
by such computation trees with 1011 + 1 node layers: each internal node uses a circuit computing
Rk , uniformly with k = 7, and each root-to-leaf path has 1011 edges, so the resulting root circuit
computes R7×1011 .
    In [AL25, Appendix B], the choice of C(x) depends only on the accumulated tilt between the
left and right sides of the rank-1 terms along the root-to-x path, and this tilt is real-valued. In
this paper, we instead propose scripted rebalancing, which replaces the real-valued tilt with a finite
state: each node carries a state h ∈ [H], and the circuit attached to the node is I(h), a prescribed
circuit computing Rk . For a rank-1 term (U, V T ), the state offset is the nearest integer to the
base-Z logarithm of the ratio of the (1 − β, β)-Bernoulli weight of the nonzero support of V T
to the (1 − α, α)-Bernoulli weight of the nonzero support of U . The child state is obtained by
adding this offset to the current state and clamping the result to [0, H − 1]. Thus a parameter set
(k, H, I, Z, α, β) specifies a finite-state script for building a computation tree. We handpick four


                                                                7
such scripts with k = 7, named Jessica, Sonetto, Regulus, and Regulus Transpose; starting each
script from root state 0 and running it for 1011 steps produces CJ , CS , CR , CRT , respectively. For
details, see Sections 2.1 and 2.2.

Reducing mean log-degree to a Lyapunov exponent. It remains to prove that plugging
{CJ } and C8 into the respective transfer theorems indeed yields the claimed upper bounds on σ(R1 )
and δ(R1 ).
   Fix p ∈ [0, 1]. To compute fCJ (Ber(p)), recall what this quantity means: choose an input-gate
index of CJ whose 7 × 1011 bits are i.i.d. Ber(p), take the base-2 logarithm of its degree, and take
the expectation. Recall that CJ is defined by a computation tree with 1011 + 1 layers, each step
consuming a block of 7 bits. We reveal the random input-gate index one block at a time. After
revealing the first several blocks, we count the nodes in the corresponding layer that are compatible
with the revealed prefix: namely, those nodes for which the product of the rank-1 terms along the
root-to-node path has a left vector that is nonzero at the revealed prefix index. Since the subtree
below any node is determined solely by the node’s state, it suffices to maintain a vector indexed by
[H], recording, for each state, the number of compatible nodes in the current layer with that state.
    The layer-to-layer update is therefore linear: the next vector is the current vector multiplied on
the right by a matrix. Moreover, this matrix depends only on how many of the newly revealed 7 bits
equal 1. Under the i.i.d. Ber(p) input distribution, this number has distribution Bin(7, p). Hence
fCJ (Ber(p)) is obtained as follows: start from [1, 0, . . . , 0]; at each of the 1011 steps, independently
choose a matrix from A0 , . . . , A7 according to Bin(7, p) and multiply the current vector on the right
by that matrix; finally, take the expected base-2 logarithm of the ℓ1 -norm of the resulting vector
and divide by 7 × 1011 . The matrices A0 , . . . , A7 are determined by the parameter set alone and can
be computed from it. For details, see Section 3.1.

Toll-and-potential certificates and machine verification. Consider a collection of matrices
A0 , . . . , A7 and the distribution Bin(7, p) on them. For the corresponding random matrix product,
choose a large number of steps, randomly select one matrix at each step to multiply the current
vector, and take the logarithm of the resulting vector’s ℓ1 -norm divided by the number of steps. As
the number of steps tends to infinity, this quantity converges almost surely to a fixed value: the
quenched Lyapunov exponent, a quantity studied in random dynamical systems, especially in the
multiplicative ergodic theory of random matrix products.
    Because 1011 is so large, the numerical value of fCJ (Ber(p)) is extremely close to this exponent
divided by 7. We show that any pair of arrays (a, b) ∈ R(k+1)×(k+1) × R(k+1)×H satisfying certain
conditions determined by A0 , . . . , A7 , called a valid certificate, gives rise to a polynomial Φ
                                                                                                  e a (p)
that upper-bounds the Lyapunov exponent simultaneously for every distribution Bin(7, p). This
certificate theorem is derived from the Lagrangian dual of the convex program underlying the upper
bound of Gharavi and Anantharam [GA05].
    Accordingly, for each circuit family X, we choose a lattice on [0, 1], obtain optimal certificates at
the lattice points, and assemble a global upper bound ℓX : [0, 1] → R≥0 . For each X, the inequality
fCX (7m,h) (Ber(p)) ≤ ℓX (p) holds for all m ≥ 1011 , all h ∈ [H], and all p ∈ [0, 1]; moreover, ℓX is
13-Lipschitz on [0, 1]. These two facts allow us to write two computer programs whose acceptance is
a sufficient (though not necessary) condition for the desired numerical bounds: ξ({CJ }) < 1.244844
after plugging {CJ } into the corresponding formula, and ϕ(C8 ) < 0.319866 after plugging C8 into
the corresponding formula. The implementation is available at https://osf.io/yz4fr/overview?
view_only=2f77f3a2c69e44a2bb57738e01223149. For details, see Sections 3.2 and 3.3.

   In this section, we use our upper bounds on σ(R1 ), δ(R1 ), and δ(P1 ) to derive algorithms and

       Our first application is a deterministic offline algorithm for #OV.

    Let M be a finite collection of nonnegative matrices, and let µ be a probability distribution on
M. Consider a sequence of independent and identically distributed random matrices A1 , A2 , . . .
drawn from M according to µ. The associated quenched Lyapunov exponent is defined as the
almost-sure limit
                                        1

The existence of this limit is well established: it is a direct consequence of the Furstenberg–Kesten

ergodic theorem [Kin73] and Oseledets’ multiplicative ergodic theorem [Ose68]. Despite this
theoretical guarantee of existence, however, computing the exponent λ exactly remains notoriously
difficult.

A.1     Circuit Model and Cost Measures

   In this paper, a depth-2 linear circuit is an ordered pair C = (A, B) of matrices A ∈ Matn×r and
B ∈ Matr×m , and we say that C computes the n × m matrix M = AB. This definition corresponds
to depth-2 linear circuits in the following sense:
    • consider a circuit with three layers, an input layer, a middle layer, and an output layer, having
      n, r, and m gates, respectively;
    • each middle gate computes a fixed linear combination of the input gates;
    • each output gate computes a fixed linear combination of the middle gates;
    • the matrices A and B record the weights from the input gates to the middle gates and from
      the middle gates to the output gates, respectively;

      by M = AB, saying that C computes M is justified.
