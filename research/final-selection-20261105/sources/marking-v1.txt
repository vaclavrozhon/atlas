                                                                                            Abstract
                                                    We show that for every first-order logic (FO) formula ϕ, and every graph class G of bounded
                                                expansion, there exists a distributed (deterministic) algorithm that, for every n-node graph
                                                G ∈ G of diameter D, decides whether G |= ϕ in O(D + log n) rounds under the standard
                                                CONGEST model. Graphs of bounded expansion encompass many classes of sparse graphs such
                                                as planar graphs, bounded-genus graphs, bounded-treedepth graphs, bounded-treewidth graphs,
                                                bounded-degree graphs, and graphs excluding a fixed graph H as a minor or topological minor.
                                                Note that our algorithm is optimal up to a logarithmic additional term, as even a simple FO
                                                formula such as “there are two vertices of degree 3” already on trees requires Ω(D) rounds in
                                                CONGEST.
                                                    Our result extends to solving optimization problems expressed in FO (e.g., k-vertex cover
                                                of minimum weight), as well as to counting the number of solutions of a problem expressible
                                                in a fragment of FO (e.g., counting triangles), still running in O(D + log n) rounds under the
                                                CONGEST model. This exemplifies the vast contrast between sparse graphs and general graphs
                                                as far as CONGEST algorithms are concerned. For instance, Drucker, Kuhn, and Oshman [PODC
                                                2014] showed√ that the mere problem of deciding whether a general graph contains a 4-cycle
                                                requires Θ( n/ log n) rounds in CONGEST. For counting √      triangles, the best known algorithm
                                                of Chang, Pettie, and Zhang [SODA 2019] takes Õ( n) rounds.
                                                    Finally, our result extends to distributed certification. We show that, for every FO for-
                                                mula ϕ, and every graph class G of bounded expansion, there exists a certification scheme for ϕ
                                                using certificates on O(log n) bits. This significantly generalizes the recent result of Feuilloley,
                                                Bousquet, and Pierron [PODC 2022], which held solely for graphs of bounded treedepth.
                                                Keywords: Sparse graphs, bounded expansion, model checking, logic, distributed computing,
                                                CONGEST model.

1     Introduction
Distributed computing in (synchronous, failure-free) networks has to cope with two obstacles,
namely dilation and congestion. The dilation obstacle is that far-away nodes are required to ex-
change information in various network problems, e.g., constructing a Minimum Spanning Tree
(MST). In this case, the diameter D of the network is an inherent lower bound on the round
complexity for a large class of problems. The congestion obstacle is due to limited communication
bandwidth between the nodes. The standard CONGEST model [69] assumes that every communi-
cation link between adjacent nodes in the network cannot transmit more than B bits in a single
round of communication, where B is usually set to Θ(log n) in any n-node network, with the mere
objective of keeping the model simple, yet informative and meaningful1 . The tight lower bound
        √
Ω(D + n) rounds in CONGEST for constructing an MST in n-node networks of diameter D
(see [70]) is a typical illustration of the price to pay for long-distance communications over links of
                                                         √
limited bandwidth. A crucial point is that the Ω(D+ n) rounds lower bound is not specific to MST
construction but applies (up to logarithmic factors) to many problems, including approximating or
verifying connectivity, spanning connected subgraph, s-t cut, etc. (see [12]).
                        √
    While the Ω(D + n) lower bound on the number of rounds is unavoidable for many problems
even in sparse graphs [12, 18, 70], an interesting phenomenon was discovered by [40]. It appears
                                           √
that, for some problems, the Ω(D + n) barrier could be circumvented in planar graphs. For
instance, both MST and (1 + ε)-approximate min-cut can be computed in just O(D · logO(1) n)
rounds in planar graphs by combining an efficient planar embedding algorithm [39] with the low-
congestion short-cut technique [40]. The Õ(D) bounds2 on the number of rounds for MST and
min-cut were extended to graphs of bounded genus [51], and graphs excluding a fixed graph as a
                                                                                        √
minor [52, 41]. On the other hand, as it was observed in [40], the bound Ω̃(D + n) for MST
holds even when the maximum degree of a graph is at most 3.
    Besides MST and min-cut, most of the developments on the “Õ(D)-phenomenon” focus on a
very few problems like Depth-First Search [45] or approximation of maximum independent set [8],
which naturally raises the question of whether this phenomenon is unique to a few optimization
problems or if it extends to a broader class of problems on various classes of sparse graphs. In the
centralized computing framework, a successful approach to describing general tractability results
is by making use of algorithmic meta-theorems. Such theorems establish that certain families of
algorithmic problems, defined by some logical conditions, can be solved “efficiently” on some classes
of graphs. According to [48], algorithmic meta-theorems play an important role in the theory of
algorithms as they reveal deep interplays between algorithms, logic, and combinatorics.

Algorithmic Meta-Theorems. The most popular logics used in meta-theorems are first-order
(FO) and monadic second-order (MSO) logics. By adapting known constructions (we provide a
formal proof in Theorem 5), it is possible to show that deciding whether a graph of bounded degree
and logarithmic diameter is not 3-colorable requires Ω̃(n) rounds. Since 3-colorability is expressible
in MSO, this immediately excludes MSO as a suitable logic for describing problems with Õ(D)
round complexity. Nevertheless, FO remains a suitable candidate, and will be at the core of our
approach.

    Our understanding of model checking in classes of sparse graphs has been subject to enormous
progress since the turn of the century. The first fixed-parameter tractability results (parameterized
by the size of the formula) were obtained by [72] on graphs of bounded degree. [34] proved the
tractability of FO model checking on planar graphs and apex-minor-free graph classes. These
results were extended to graph classes with excluded minors by [27]. Progress in sparsity theory
by [67, 66] have significantly extended the boundaries of FO model checking tractability on sparse
graphs.
    Within the theory of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez, a crucial role is assigned to the graph
classes of bounded expansion. Intuitively, a graph class G has bounded expansion if there exists a
function f : N → N such that, for every G ∈ G, and any r ≥ 0, every minor H of G obtained by
contracting disjoint subgraphs of radius at most r is f (r)-degenerate (i.e., every subgraph of H has
a vertex of degree at most f (r)). Bounded expansion can be thought of as strengthened degeneracy,
which must persist even after performing “local minor operations”. For instance, the graph class
containing every graph obtained from a complete graph by subdividing every edge once is sparse
but not of bounded expansion. Instead, the class of planar graphs, the classes of graphs with
bounded genus, the classes of graphs of bounded degree, and the classes of minor-free graphs and
topologically minor-free graphs are all of bounded expansion. Interestingly, [14] demonstrated that
many network models (e.g., stochastic block models) exhibit bounded expansion for some ranges
of parameters, and these findings are even supported with empirical measurements on a corpus of
real-world networks. All these points make graph classes of bounded expansion quite attractive
from both conceptual and practical perspectives. And indeed, all the aforementioned results on
model checking FO formulas can be extended graphs classes of bounded expansion, as shown by
[16] who obtained an FPT algorithm for model-checking FO formulas on graph classes of bounded
expansion.
    The situation is not as advanced and mature in the distributed computing framework. Our work
is strongly motivated by the question of whether it is possible to align the knowledge of distributed
model checking with the current knowledge of centralized model checking.

Distributed Model-Checking, and Distributed Certification. The problem of the (dis-
tributed) model checking for a property Π on a labeled graph is formulated as follows. The algo-
rithm checking Π must guarantee that if the labeled graph G satisfies Π, i.e., if G |= Π, then all
nodes output accept, and if G 6|= Π then at least one node must output reject. In short, a distributed
algorithm A decides, or checks Π in a graph class G if, for every G = (V, E) ∈ G,

                           G |= Π ⇐⇒ ∀v ∈ V, A accepts G at node v.

As a typical example, [65] introduced the locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems as a formaliza-
tion of problems for which the global correctness of a solution is determined as the conjunction of
local criteria (e.g., maximal independent set, or proper coloring). LCL plays a central role in dis-
tributed network computing [69]. In bounded degree graphs, an LCL can be checked in a constant
                                                                                               √
number of rounds in CONGEST. However, simple FO properties such as C4 -freeness require Ω̃( n)
rounds to be checked in general graphs in CONGEST [15].
    As a byproduct of our techniques developed for FO model checking in CONGEST model, we
are also questioning the existence of meta-theorems susceptible to extend the known results about
distributed certification [30, 46, 57], which plays a major role in the design of fault-tolerant al-
gorithms. Distributed certification can be conceptually thought of as a non-deterministic variant

                                                  2
of distributed model checking. Roughly, a certification scheme for a graph property Π is a pair
prover-verifier. The prover is a computationally unlimited oracle that assigns a certificate c(v) to
each node v. The verifier is a distributed algorithm whose role is to check whether the certificates
assigned by the prover establish evidence that the network satisfies Π. The scheme certifying Π
must guarantee that if G |= Π then the prover can assign certificates such that the verifier accepts at
each and every node, and if G 6|= Π then, for every certificate assignment by the prover, the verifier
rejects in at least one node (i.e., the verifier cannot be fooled by the prover on illegal instances).
In short, a scheme certify Π in a graph class G if, for every G = (V, E) ∈ G,

             G |= Π ⇐⇒ ∃c : V → {0, 1}⋆ , ∀v ∈ V, verifier accepts (G, c) at node v.

Distributed certification actually provides a concrete mechanism enabling the nodes of a network
to quickly determine whether the system entered an illegal state, and to fix it (a node at which
the verifier rejects can launch a recovery procedure). Indeed, for very many problems (e.g., MST
construction), the certificates can be collectively computed by the nodes themselves, in parallel to
computing a solution, with negligible extra cost.
    In distributed certification, it is desirable that the verifier perform as quickly as possible. The
focus is actually on verifiers such that the decision (accept or reject) taken at every node v depends
only on the certificates assigned by the prover to the nodes in the closed neighborhood N [v] of
node v, that is, on verifiers performing in a single round of communication. The main complexity
measure for a certification scheme is the size |c(v)| of the certificate assigned by the prover to every
node v of legal instances. It was proved that every FO property could be distributedly certified
using certificates of O(log n) bits on graphs with bounded treedepth [22], and that every MSO
property can be certified using certificates of O(log2 n) bits on graphs with bounded treewidth [32]
and cliquewidth [31]. Note that O(log n)-bit certificates enable the verifier to perform a single
round of communication in CONGEST for exchanging the certificates between neighbors, whereas
O(log2 n)-bit certificates require Θ(log n) rounds for exchanging the certificates between neighbors.

1.1     Our Results
1.1.1    Distributed Model Checking
Our first main result states that every graph property expressible as an FO formula can be decided
in O(D + log n) rounds under the CONGEST model in n-node graphs of bounded expansion, and
diameter D. This result actually holds for labeled graphs as well. A labeled graph is a pair (G, L)
where G = (V, E) and L : V → 2Λ assigns to each node a set of labels taken from a finite set Λ.
FO formulas on graphs can be extended to FO formulas on labeled graphs by merely introducing a
unary predicate labλ (x) for every λ ∈ Λ, which holds if and only if λ is one of the labels of vertex x
in L.




                                                   3
Theorem 1. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula ϕ without free variables on Λ-labeled
graphs, and for every class of Λ-labeled graphs G of bounded expansion, there exists a distributed
algorithm which, for every n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G of diameter D, and every L : V → 2Λ ,
decides whether (G, L) |= ϕ in O(D + log n) rounds under the CONGEST model.

    The constant hidden in the big-O notation depends on the length |ϕ| of the formula ϕ, on the
set Λ, and on the function controlling the expansion of G, but not on the graph G nor on the
labeling L. Theorem 1 complements the previous work on MST and s, t-cut of [39, 40, 41, 52]
                       √
circumventing the Ω̃( n) barrier on the congestion of distributed algorithms in CONGEST in two
directions.
    First, it holds for the large class of problems definable by FO formulas. By making use of the
expressive power of FO, Theorem 1 implies that the bound O(D + log n) on the number of rounds
under the CONGEST model on graphs of bounded expansion holds for various problems, such as
deciding whether a graph contains (or excludes) a cycle of a fixed length, or, more generally, whether
it contains a subgraph isomorphic to a fixed graph of constant size, a connected dominating set of
a fixed size, or a Steiner tree of a fixed size.
    Second, Theorem 1 holds for a significantly more general class of sparse graphs than minor-free
                                                                          √
graph. Moreover, as we already mentioned, the lower bound Ω̃(D + n) holds for MST (which
is not FO-definable) when the maximum degree of a graph is at most 3. On the other hand,
graphs of bounded degree, as well as a more general class of graphs excluding some fixed graph as
a topological minor, are of bounded expansion.
    The dependence in D in Theorem 1 cannot be avoided, as witnessed by the mere FO property
“there are at least two nodes of degree 3” in binary trees. The dependence on the size n of the
                                                    √
networks is however drastically reduced from Ω( n) rounds in general graphs (e.g., for the FO
formula C4 -freeness [15]) to just O(log n) rounds in graphs of bounded expansion. Note also that
bounded degeneracy would not suffice to establish our result, as we show that the mere FO formula
                         √
C6 -freeness requires Ω̃( n) rounds to be checked in CONGEST, in graphs of degeneracy 2 and of
bounded diameter (see 7). Moreover, our result is the best that one can hope for regarding the
class of problems, in the sense that it cannot be extended to, e.g., MSO. Indeed, we show that
non-3-colorability requires Ω̃(n) rounds to be checked in bounded degree graphs with logarithmic
diameter (see Theorem 5).

1.1.2   Counting and Optimization
Our result extends beyond model checking, in two directions. Firstly, we consider the problem of
counting solutions of FO formulas with no free variables. This includes triangle counting, and more
generally counting copies of some given subgraph.

Theorem 2. Let Λ be a finite set. For every quantifier-free FO formula ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ) on Λ-labeled
graphs with k ≥ 1 free variables x1 , . . . , xk , and for every class of labeled graphs G of bounded
expansion, there exists a distributed algorithm that, for every n-node network G ∈ G of diameter D
and every labeling function L : V → 2Λ , counts the number of k-tuples of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk ) for
which G |= ϕ(v1 , . . . , vk ), in O(D + log n) rounds under the CONGEST model.

1.1.3    Distributed Certification
Last but not least, our results extend to distributed certification.

Theorem 4. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula ϕ on labeled graphs, and for every class
of Λ-labeled graphs G of bounded expansion, there exists a distributed certification scheme that, for
every n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G and every labeling L : V → 2Λ , certifies (G, L) |= ϕ using
certificates on O(log n) bits.

    Note that, with O(log n)-bit certificates, the verifier can be implemented in a single round of
communication in the CONGEST model, as desired. Note also that our certification scheme is a
so-called proof-labeling scheme [57], which is a weaker form of distributed certification than, e.g.,
locally checkable proofs [46].
    As a direct consequence of Theorem 4, certifying C4 -freeness can be done with O(log n)-bit
certificates in graphs of bounded expansion. In contrast, certifying C4 -freeness requires certificates
       √
on Ω̃( n) bits in general graphs, using the reduction from [15]. More generally, [22] show that
every MSO formula can be certified with O(log n)-bit certificates in graphs of bounded treedepth.
Theorem 4 extends this result. Indeed, while Theorem 4 is for FO, and the result of [22] is for
MSO, both logics have identical expressive power on graphs of bounded treedepth.
    Last but not least, as for our model-checking algorithm, Theorem 4 is the best that can be
achieved. Indeed, it does not extend to MSO as certifying non-3-colorability requires certificates
      √
of Ω̃( n) bits in planar graphs (see Theorem 6), and certificates of Θ̃(n) bits in bounded degree
graphs (see Theorem 5).

1.2     Our Techniques
1.2.1    Distributed Model Checking
The proof of our model checking algorithm in Theorem 1 uses tools completely different from the
low-congestion short-cut technique developed for planar graphs and graphs of bounded genus by
[39, 40]. It is also different from the ones in [28, 22, 32, 31] used for graphs of bounded treedepth,
treewidth, and cliquewidth, respectively. Our motivation is to bring in the techniques and ideas
developed for efficient centralized meta-algorithms for sparse classes of graphs to the distributed
domain. In a nutshell, we use modern tools from centralized model checking, and we show how to


                                                    5
implement them efficiently in the distributed setting, namely the CONGEST model. Our approach
is based on the quantifier elimination methods from [16], [48], and, mainly, [71].
    Before we summarize our approach, let us note that model checking FO formulas in graphs
of bounded expansion is trivial in the so-called Congested Clique model (referred to as CLIQUE
hereafter), which is essentially the CONGEST model in a complete graph Kn . In this model, every
node i ∈ {1, . . . , n} of Kn plays the role of a node v of the input graph G = (V, E) (i.e., node i
is given as input all the edges {i, j} incident to v in G). Now, in the CLIQUE model, every graph
of bounded degeneracy can be reconstructed at each node in a single round of communication
(see [2]). This holds even in the broadcast variant BCLIQUE of CLIQUE in which every node is
bounded to send a same O(log n)-bit message to all the other nodes at each round. The techniques
used for obtaining this result in CLIQUE do not extend to CONGEST as, in the latter, nodes can
only communicate through the edges of the input graph G, and thus every node may only learn
O(log n) bits of information from each of its neighbors at each round. Instead, in BCLIQUE, each
node can learn a total of Θ(n log n) bits of information at each round even if G is a graph of bounded
degree.
    We implement in the CONGEST model a quantifier elimination technique designed for the
centralized setting, so that the elimination is performed in O(D) rounds in diameter-D graphs of
bounded expansion. For this, we essentially revisit the existing approaches, and we show that they
can be implemented in the distributed setting. As said before, our argument is strongly based on
the exposition of [71], which in turn is based on the work of [48]. However, our work differs from
the previous ones in two important aspects.
    First, the distributed implementation of the techniques in [71] requires technical attention in
several places. There are indeed operations that are very simple to perform in the centralized
setting, and they are actually often even implicit when describing the sequential algorithm. We had
to carefully revisit the whole construction of Pilipczuk et al., and to “open all boxes” for ensuring
that each step of the construction can be implemented efficiently in the distributed setting.
    Second, previous results consider model checking of first-order logics over arbitrary relational
structures, and each relational structure has an associated graph (the Gaifman graph). Model
checking over graphs of bounded expansion actually refers to model checking of relational structures
whose Gaifman graphs are of bounded expansion. The techniques of Pilipczuk et al. (as well as
the other related approaches) consist of enriching the structure relative to an arbitrary first-order
formula with new symbols and new predicates for obtaining a quantifier-free formula. Enriching
the structure naturally modifies the Gaifman graphs. In contrast, in distributed computing models
such as CONGEST, nodes establish communications over a network that does not change. When
enriching the structure, one thus need to be very careful for avoiding creating connections between
nodes that cannot directly communicate in the network.

constructs a quantifier-free formula ϕ.b This formula satisfies that, for each labeled graph (G, L) ∈ G,

presence of certain symbols in the labels assigned to each node. The new binary predicates, called
least common ancestor (or lca for short) predicates check the depth of the least common ancestor
of two nodes in certain subtrees of the input graph.

1.2.2   Counting and Optimization
For establishing Theorem 2, we aim at computing the number of k-tuples of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk )
for which a quantifier-free FO formula ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ) with k free variables holds. let G = (V, E) be

                                                     7
the input graph, and L : V → 2Λ be its labeling. We stress the fact that the formula ϕ is assumed
to be quantifier-free, and, in particular, it has no other variables than (x1 , . . . , xk ). As for model
checking, we use the fact that there exists an integer function g depending only on the class G such
that, for any graph G ∈ G, and any constant p, G admits a coloring with g(p) colors such that
any subset of at most p colors induces a subgraph of treedepth at most p. Thanks to [68], such
a coloring can be computed in O(log n) rounds in CONGEST. For p = k, each k-uple of vertices
(v1 , . . . , vk ) of the labeled graph in (G, L) satisfying the formula ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ) covers some set S
of colors, with |S| ≤ p. Assuming given an O(D + log n)-round algorithm counting the solutions
of ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ) on labeled subgraphs (G[S], L) of treedepth at most k, we show how to count the
solutions on the whole labeled graph (G, L). For any two sets of colors S1 , S2 , the set of solutions

1.2.3   Distributed Certification
At a high lever, our certification scheme for an FO formula ϕ and a class of graphs G with bounded
expansion essentially consists for the prover to provide each node with the transcript of the execution
our distributed model-checking algorithm at this node. The role of the verifier is then to check (in
a distributed manner) whether this transcript encodes a correct execution of our algorithm. Note
that the certification scheme does not have to certify the bounded expansion property of G, which
is a promise. On the other hand, given coloring of the input graph G = (V, E) ∈ G, the certification
scheme must certify that any set S of k = |ϕ| colors does induce a subgraph with treedepth k.
This is done in an ad hoc manner, and not by providing each node with a transcript of the coloring
algorithm from [68]. The main difficulty is to encode the transcript of the quantifier elimination
procedure, i.e., the transformation from (ϕ, G, L) to (ϕ, b L bϕ ) with ϕb quantifier-free.
     Each lca predicate refers to a local condition, and the value of such a predicate can be certified
locally at each node, in forests of bounded depth. On the other hand, the unary predicates may
refer to non-local properties. Nevertheless, we show that verifying the correctness of the unary
predicates can be done using a spanning tree whose root is able to decide. For instance, let us
consider the unary predicate “red” stating, for every node v, whether it is red (red(v) = ⊤) or not
(red(v) = ⊥), and the formula ♯red stating whether or not there is at least one red node in the
labeled graph (G, L). Given a tree T rooted at a node r, and spanning the vertices of G, certifying
♯red can merely be done by assigning to every node v a certificate c(v) equal to the number of
red nodes in the subtree Tv of T rooted at v. The verifier at node v checks that the sum of the
certificates c(w) over all the children w of v in T is equal to xv if red(v) = ⊥, and to xv −1 otherwise
(i.e., if red(v) = ⊤). Each node checks that its value of ♯red is equal to the value of ♯red of all its
neighbors. The root r checks that

                            (♯red = ⊤ ∧ c(r) ≥ 1) ∨ (♯red = ⊥ ∧ c(r) < 1).

If any of these test fails at a node, this node rejects, otherwise it accepts. What remains is to
certify the spanning tree T , which is standard technique using O(log n)-bit certificates [57].
    Note that, at a first glance, it may seem over complicated to use quantifier elimination for
certifying a formula of the type ϕ(x̄) = ∃y ψ(x̄, y). Indeed, to certify that ϕ(x̄) = ⊤, it may seem
sufficient to certify the existence of a vertex y that yield ψ(x̄, y) = ⊤, which can be done easily
thanks to a spanning tree rooted at y, consuming O(log n)-bit certificates [57]. The situation is
however more complex than it seems, for at least two reasons. First, in the intermediate steps
ψ may refers to free variables (x1 , . . . , xℓ ) = x̄, which makes the identification of a vertex y such
that ψ(x̄, y) = ⊤ non-trivial. Second, there may be negations in the global formula, that is, one
may have to consider also the case where ϕ(x̄) = ¬∃y ψ(x̄, y), and certifying the non existence of
a vertex y is not straightforward. A typical example is the case of certifying the existence of a
4-cycle, which is trivial using O(log n)-bit certificate, in contrast to certifying the non existence of
                                                 √
a 4-cycle, which requires certificates on Ω̃( n) bits in general graphs.

1.3.1    Distributed Computing on Sparse Networks
The quest for efficient (sublinear) algorithms for solving classical graph problems in the CONGEST
model dates back to the seminal paper by [37], which presented an algorithm for constructing
a Minimum Spanning Tree (MST). Since then, a long series of problems have been addressed,
including connectivity decomposition by [7], tree embeddings by [44], k-dominating set by [60],
Steiner trees by [61], min-cut by [43, 63], max-flow by [42], and shortest paths by [53, 62], among
others. Additionally, algorithms tailored to specific classes of networks have been developed, such as
depth-first search (DFS) for planar graphs [45], MST for bounded genus graphs [50], and maximal
independent set (MIS) for networks excluding a fixed minor [8].
                             √
    Overcoming the Ω(D + n) barrier has been a significant focus in this field. Notably, the work
by [39, 40] showed a planar embedding in O(D · min log n, D), while [50] demonstrated that an em-
bedding is unnecessary for MST. Later, these results were extended to graphs of bounded genus by
[51], and graphs excluding a fixed graph as a minor by [52, 41]. Furthermore, algorithms achieving
Õ(D) complexity in the CONGEST model for bounded treewidth graphs have been developed for
various problems, including matching, the directed single-source shortest paths problem, bipartite
unweighted maximum matching, and girth [54].

1.3.2    Centralized Meta-Theorems
Meta-theorems, according to Grohe and Kreutzer [48], are the results that certain problems of
model checking for formulas of some logic are tractable (in some sense) on some class of graphs.
One of the most celebrated examples of a meta-theorem is Courcelle’s theorem, which asserts that
graph properties definable in MSO are decidable in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth
[10]. There is a long line of meta-theorems for FO logic on sparse graph classes in the centralized
model of computation. The starting point is Seese’s theorem [72] on linear time decidability of
FO on graphs of bounded degree. [34] gave linear time algorithms for planar graphs and all apex-
minor-free graph classes, as well as an O(n1+ε ) algorithms for graphs of bounded local tree-width.
[27] proved that deciding FO properties is fixed-parameter tractable on graph classes with excluded
minors. The next step to classes of graphs locally excluding a minor was done by [13].
    The Sparsity Theory of [67] opened new horizons for model checking on sparse graphs. [16]
proved that FO properties can be decided in linear time on graph classes of bounded expansion.
Further extensions of these results are known for nowhere-dense graphs [49] and graphs of bounded
twin-width [4]. See [58] and [47] for surveys.
    From the technical perspective, the most important for our work is the quantifier elimination
procedure introduced in [16] and in [48], see also [71]. Similar to these works, we apply the idea of
modifying the structure by adding new unary relations and functions without changing the Gaifman
graph.

1.3.3    Distributed Meta-Theorems
We are not aware of any meta-theorem combining logical and combinatorial constraints within the
CONGEST model, with one exception. The very recent paper by [28] establishes that every MSO
formula on graphs with bounded treedepth is decidable in a constant number of rounds within the
CONGEST model. The results of [28] are incomparable withTheorem 1. Indeed, Theorem 1 applies


                                                 10
to graph classes that are significantly broader than graphs of bounded treedepth, but the number
of rounds of the algorithm by [28] is independent of the number of vertices of the input graph. We
incorporate some of the methods developed by [28] in our paper.
    Another general result for CONGEST model is the work of [68], who gave a distributed algorithm
for computing a low treedepth decomposition of graphs of bounded expansion, running in O(log n)
rounds. We use this construction in our algorithm as a subroutine. In the same paper, Nešetřil
and Ossona de Mendez observe that their algorithm could be used to check whether a graph of
bounded expansion contains a given connected subgraph H of constant size, in O(log n) rounds.
    Distributed certification is a very active area of research, with numerous results emerging since
the survey by [23]. Recent work has focused on approximate variants of the problem, similar
to property testing [19, 24], and [17]. Notably, it has been shown that every monotone (i.e.,
closed under taking subgraphs) and summable (i.e., stable under disjoint union) property Π has a
compact approximate certification scheme in any proper minor-closed graph class [21]. Other recent
contributions have explored augmenting the power of the verifier in certification schemes. This
includes tradeoffs between the size of the certificates and the number of rounds of the verification
protocol by [26], randomized verifiers by [33], quantum verifiers by [29], and several hierarchies
of certification mechanisms. These mechanisms include games between a prover and a disprover
[1, 25], interactive protocols [11, 55, 64], zero-knowledge distributed certification [3], and distributed
quantum interactive proofs [36].
    Several meta-theorems are known for distributed certification. A significant result in the field
was achieved in [22], who demonstrated that any MSO formula can be locally certified on graphs
with bounded treedepth using a logarithmic number of bits per node, representing a gold standard
in certification. This theorem has numerous implications for certification; for more details, we refer
to [22]. Notably, the FO property of C4 -freeness and the MSO property of non-3-colorability, which
typically require polynomial-size certificates in general graphs, can be certified with just O(log n)-
bit certificates in graphs of bounded treedepth. The result by Bousquet et al. has been extended
to more comprehensive classes of graphs, including graphs excluding a small minor [6], graphs of
bounded treewidth, and graphs of bounded cliquewidth. However, this extension comes at the cost
of slightly larger certificates of O(log2 n) bits, as shown in [32] and [31], respectively.



2     Model and Definitions
2.1   The CONGEST Model
The CONGEST model is a standard model of distributed computation in networks [69]. The
network is modeled as a connected simple (i.e., no self-loops nor multiple edges) graphs G = (V, E).
The n ≥ 1 vertices of G model the processing nodes in the networks, and its edges models the
communication links. In the following, we may indistinguishably use “node” of “vertex” for referring
to the processing elements in the network. Each node is assigned an identifier, which is assumed
to be unique in the network. The identifiers may not be between 1 and n, but it is assumed that
each identifier can be stored on O(log n) bits in n-node networks. We also assume, w.l.o.g., that
the nodes know a linear upper-bound on the diameter D of the input graph [69].
    Computation proceeds in lockstep, as a sequence of synchronous rounds. All nodes start si-
multaneously, at the same round. At each round, every node v sends an O(log n)-bit message to


                                                   11
each of its neighbors, receives the messages sent by all its neighbors, and performs some individual
computation. The round-complexity of an algorithm A is the function RA (n) = max|V (G)|≤n RA (G)
where RA (G) denotes the number of rounds performed by the nodes executing A in G until all of
them terminate.

2.2     First-Order Formulas on Labeled Graphs
Again, in this paper, all graphs are supposed to be finite, simple (i.e., no self-loops nor multiple
edges), and undirected.

2.2.1    Definition
Recall that a first-order (FO) formula over graphs is a combination (conjunction, disjunction, and
negations) of Boolean predicates over variables, which may be quantified or not. Every variable
represents a vertex of a graph, and there are two binary predicates: equality x = y, and adjacency
adj(x, y), where the latter states whether or not there is an edge between the two vertices x and y.
The variables of an FO formula can be quantified with existential or universal quantifiers. Non-
quantified variables are called free variables. Given an FO formula ϕ with free variables x1 , . . . , xk ,
we make explicit that the latter are free by denoting ϕ = ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ). In the following, FO
denotes the set of all first order formulas over undirected graphs.
     Throughout the paper, a tuple (x1 , . . . , xk ) is abbreviated in x. The length k of a tuple
(x1 , . . . , xk ) is then denoted by |x|. Expressions such as ∃x or ∀x are shorthand for ∃x1 . . . ∃xk
and ∀x1 . . . ∀xk , respectively. We may also abuse notation in context where no confusions may
occur by letting x denote the set {x1 , . . . , xk }.
     Given an FO formula ϕ and a graph G, G |= ϕ denotes that G satisfies ϕ (i.e., G is a model
for ϕ). The negation of G |= ϕ is denoted by G 6|= ϕ. Given an FO formula ϕ(x) with free variables,
a graph G = (V, E), and a set of vertices v ∈ V k where k = |x|, we say that G satisfies ϕ(x) for
vertices v, denoted by G |= ϕ(v), if the formula is satisfied by G whenever assigning v to the free
variables of ϕ(x).
     A graph property is called a first-order property if it can be formulated as the satisfiability of an
FO formula without free variables. For instance, existence of a triangle is an FO property, which
holds whenever the graph contains a triangle (i.e., a set of three pairwise adjacent nodes). Indeed,
this property can be merely formulated as the formula:

                        ϕ = ∃x1 ∃x2 ∃x3 , adj(x1 , x2 ) ∧ adj(x2 , x3 ) ∧ adj(x1 , x3 ).

A formula with free variables expresses properties over graphs and tuples of vertices. As an example,
the following formula ψ(x) holds whenever x is a node in a triangle, formulated as

                          ψ(x) = ∃y1 ∃y2 , adj(x, y1 ) ∧ adj(x, y2 ) ∧ adj(y1 , y2 ).

The graph G with vertex set V = {a, b, c, d}, and edge set E = {{a, b}, {a, c}, {b, c}, {c, d}} satisfies
G |= ϕ as the vertices {a, b, c} form a triangle. Similarly, G |= ψ(a), but G 6|= ψ(d) as d is not in
any triangle.

2.2.2    FO Formulas for Labeled Graphs
The formalism of FO properties can be extended to labeled graphs. Let Λ be a finite set, whose
elements are called labels. A Λ-labeled graph is a pair (G = (V, E), L) where G is an undirected
graph, and L : V → 2Λ is an assignment of a set of labels to each vertex of G. An FO formula
over Λ-labeled graphs is a first order formula ϕ over graphs, extended with predicates of the form
labλ (x) for every λ ∈ Λ. The predicate labλ (x) is true if vertex x contains λ as one of its labels.
Such predicates are called label predicates. Whenever labλ (x) holds, we say that x is labeled with λ.
Note that since L assigns a set of labels to each vertex, it may be the case that labλ (x) ∧ labλ′ (x)
holds for two different labels λ and λ′ . It may be convenient to also consider the predicates labS for
all sets S ⊆ Λ. In this case, labS (x) holds if and only if L(x) = S. In particular, labS (x) ∧ labS ′ (x)
is necessarily false whenever S 6= S ′ . The set of all first-order logic formulas over Λ-labeled graphs
is denoted FO[Λ].
    Given a Λ-labeled graph (G, L), and a subgraph G′ of G, we abuse notation and denote (G′ , L)
the Λ-labeled graph defined by the restriction of L to the nodes in G′ . For instance, let Λ = {0, 1},
and let us consider the following graph property on labeled graphs: “existence of a triangle with a
node labeled 1”. This property can be expressed by the formula:

                    ϕ = ∃x1 ∃x2 ∃x3 , adj(x1 , x2 ) ∧ adj(x2 , x3 ) ∧ adj(x1 , x3 ) ∧ lab1 (x1 ).

We may also consider formula ψ(x) with free variables, such as

                       ψ(x) = ∃y1 ∃y2 , adj(x, y1 ) ∧ adj(x, y2 ) ∧ adj(y1 , y2 ) ∧ lab1 (x).

The graph G with vertex set V = {a, b, c, d} and edge set E = {{a, b}, {a, c}, {b, c}, {c, d}} with
labels L(a) = {0}, L(b) = {0}, L(c) = {1}, and L(d) = {1} satisfies (G, L) |= ϕ as the nodes {a, b, c}
form a triangle where c is labeled 1. For the same reason, we have that (G, L) |= ψ(c). However,
(G, L) 6|= ψ(a) as a is not labeled 1 (even if it is in a triangle), and (G, L) 6|= ψ(d) as node d is not
in a triangle (even if it is labeled 1).
    For ϕ(x1 , . . . , xk ) ∈ FO[Λ], and a Λ-labeled graph (G, L), we denote by true(ϕ, G, L) the set of
all tuples of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk ) satisfying that (G, L) |= ϕ(v1 , . . . , vk ). In particular, if ϕ has no
free variables then true(ϕ, G, L) is equal to the truth value of (G, L) |= ϕ. Two FO[Λ] formulas
ϕ1 and ϕ2 are equivalent if true(ϕ1 , G, L) = true(ϕ2 , G, L) for every Λ-labeled graph (G, L). The
following basic definitions play a central role in this paper.
    • A formula ϕ(x) ∈ FO[Λ] is called quantifier-free if it has no quantifiers, i.e., it can be expressed
      as a boolean combination of predicates involving only the free variables in x.

    • A formula ϕ(x) ∈ FO[Λ] is existential if it can be written as ϕ(x) = ∃y, ψ(x, y), where ψ(x, y)
      is quantifier free formula in FO[Λ].
    Furthermore, a formula ϕ(x) is in prenex normal form if it can be written in the form

                                        ϕ(x) = Q1 y1 . . . Qt yt ζ(x, y),

where ζ(x, y) ∈ FO[Λ], and Q1 , . . . , Qt are existential or universal quantifiers. The value of t is the
quantifier depth of ϕ. Every FO formula admits an equivalent formula in prenex normal form (see,
e.g., [20]). This fact can be easily established by induction on the length of the formula, and it can
be extended to FO[Λ], i.e., first order formulas for labeled graphs, in a straightforward manner.

Recall that a graph G = (V, E) has treedepth at most d if there exists a forest F of rooted trees
with depth at most d satisfying that there exists a one-to-one mapping f : V → V (F ) such that,
for every edge {u, v} ∈ E, f (u) is an ancestor of f (v) in a tree of F , or f (v) is an ancestor of f (u)
in a tree of F . The treedepth of a graph is the smallest d such that such a mapping f exists.
    It has been recently shown in [28] that decision, counting and optimization of FO formulas can
be performed in O(log n) CONGEST rounds for connected labeled graphs of bounded treedepth.

    Graphs of bounded expansion [67] can be defined in term of local minors and degeneracy as
pointed out in the Introduction. It is more convenient for us to use the following characterization.
Specifically, let N denote the set of positive integers. A class G of graphs has bounded expansion if
there exists a function f : N → N such that, for every G ∈ G, and for every k ∈ N, there exists a
coloring of the vertices of G with colors in [f (k)] = {1, . . . , f (k)} such that, for every set S ⊆ [f (k)]
of cardinality k the subgraph of G induced by the nodes colored by a color in S has treedepth at
most |S|. In particular, each subset of at most k colors induces a subgraph of treedepth at most k.
    For every positive integers p and k, a (p, k)-treedepth coloring of a graph G is a k-coloring of
G satisfying that each set S ⊆ [k] of at most p colors induces a graph of tree-depth at most |S|.
Given a function f : N → N, a class of graphs G has expansion f if, for every p ∈ N>0 , every G ∈ G
has a (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring. A class of graphs has bounded expansion if it has expansion
f for some function f . A class of graphs has effective bounded expansion if it has expansion f
for some Turing-computable function f . In the following, we always assume that function f is
Turing-computable functions. Interestingly, treedepth colorings can be efficiently computed in the
CONGEST model, as stated below.

Proposition 2 ([68]). Let G be a class of graphs of bounded expansion. There exists a function
f : N → N such that, for any graph G ∈ G, and any constant positive integer p, a (p, f (p))-treedepth
coloring of G can be computed in O(log n) rounds in the CONGEST model.

3.1     Rooted Forests of Constant Depth
In this section we explain how to implement in CONGEST the quantifier elimination procedure from
[16, 48] for rooted forests of constant depth. That is, every tree of the forest at hand is rooted,
and of depth at most d, for some d ≥ 1. The basic idea for eliminating the quantifiers is to encode
information about the satisfiability of the formula in labels at the nodes. This increases the size
of the labeling. However, by taking advantage of the simple structure of FO formulas on forests of
bounded depth, it is possible to bound the number of labels by a constant depending solely on the
size of the formula, and on the depth of the forests. In the following, for each positive integer d,
we denote by Fd the set of all rooted forests of depth at most d. For a set of labels Λ, we denote
by Fd [Λ] the set of all Λ-labeled graphs (G, L) such that G ∈ Fd .

3.2   Graphs of Bounded Treedepth
Given a graph G = (V, E), a decomposition forest F of G is a rooted spanning forest of G satisfying
that for every edge e ∈ E, one of its endpoints is an ancestor of the other in F . Observe that if G
is connected then any decomposition forest of G is actually a tree, and every DFS tree of G is a
decomposition tree of G. For every t > 0, any induced subgraph H of G with treedepth at most t
is called a treedepth-t induced subgraph.
    We stress the fact that a decomposition forest F of G must be a subgraph of G, which is very
convenient for distributed algorithms because this enables us to communicate along the edges of F .
There exist graphs of treedepth t, in which any decomposition forest is of depth Ω(2t ), for example
the paths on 2t − 1 vertices. Nevertheless, graphs of treedepth at most t have paths of at most 2t − 1
vertices. In particular, for any class of graphs of bounded treedepth, all possible decomposition
forests are also of constant (although exponentially larger) depth.

Proposition 3 ([67]). Any decomposition forest of a graph of tree-depth at most t belongs to F2t .

    Recall that the tree decomposition of a graph H = (V, E) is a pair (T = (I, F ), {Bi }i∈I ) where
T is a tree and each Bi is a subset of vertices of V , called bag of node i, such that

   • each vertex of H is contained in at least one bag,

   • for each edge of H, at least one bag contains both endpoints,

   • for each vertex v of H, the nodes of T whose bags contain v form a connected subgraph of T .

The width of the decomposition is the maximum size of a bag, minus one. The treewidth of H is
the minimum width over all its tree decompositions.
   Graphs of treedepth at most p have treewidth at most 2p [67]. The proof of Proposition 1 is
based on three ingredients.

   1. The first ingredient is a dynamic programming sequential algorithm for decision, optimization
      and counting solutions of a given formula ϕ(x) ∈ F O(Λ), over labeled graphs (H, L) of

                                                     34
     bounded treewidth. Here we will assume that a tree decomposition of constant width is part
     of the input, and moreover the tree is rooted. The algorithm used in [28] is due to Borie,
     Parker and Tovey [5]. The nodes of the tree decomposition (T = (I, F ), {Bi }i∈I ) are processed
     bottom-up, from the leaves to the root of T . When processing node i, let j1 , . . . , jq be its
     children in T . The essential observation due to [5] is that the dynamic programming tables
     at node i are of constant size for decision, and of O(log n) size for counting and optimization.
     Moreover, for computing the information (encoding partial solutions) at node i, one only
     needs to know the subgraphs H[Bi ] and H[Bj1 ], . . . , H[Bjq ] induced by the bags at node i
     and its children, together with the information already computed at the children nodes. In
     particular, when i is a leaf, H[Bi ] suffices for computing the dynamic programming table at
     node i.
  2. The second ingredient is an implementation of the above sequential algorithm in the CONGEST
     model, in a number of rounds equal to the depth of the tree decomposition T , under the con-
     dition that one can associate to each node i of T a vertex ℓ(i) of H, such that vertices ℓ(i)
     satisfy several properties in order to “simulate the behavior” of node i in the dynamic pro-
     gramming. Vertex ℓ(i) must know the depth of tree T and the depth of node i in T , the
     bag Bi and the subgraph H[Bi ], and moreover ℓ(i) knows the node ℓ(pT (i)) associated to
     the parent pT (i) of i in T , and vertices ℓ(i), ℓ(pT (i)) are equal or adjacent in H. Informally,
     the CONGEST algorithm implements the sequential one by making vertex ℓ(i) to compute at
     the right moment (i.e., at the round corresponding to the depth of T minus the depth of i
     in T , plus one) the dynamic programming table at node i, and transmitting it to ℓ(pT (i)).
     Observe that ℓ(i) has collected all the information from the children j1 , . . . , jq of i, thanks
     to the fact that ℓ(j1 ), . . . , ℓ(jq ) have already computed and communicated it at the previous
     round. (To be complete, in [28], we actually have ℓ(i) = i and T is a subgraph of H, but
     our generalization is a simple observation.) This explains that the number of rounds of the
     CONGEST algorithm is the depth of the tree decomposition T .
  3. The third ingredient computes in a constant number of rounds the desired tree decomposition
     (T = (I, F ), {Bi }i∈I ) of graph H of treedepth at most p, together with the information
     requested by the previous item. The width of the tree-decomposition is at most p, and the
     depth of T is at most 2p .

5.1   The Case of Trees with Bounded Depth
We begin with a basic primitive, that we use to count values, to identify connected components,
and for several other applications.
Proposition 4 ([57]). Let us suppose that each node u in a graph G = (V, E) is given as an input a
triple (root(u), parent(u), depth(u)), where root(u) and parent(u) are node identifiers, and depth(u)
is a positive integer. Let us consider the algorithm where each node u checks:
   • For each v ∈ N (u), root(v) = root(u) = ρ.

   • If id(u) = ρ then parent(u) = id(u) and depth(u) = 0.

   • If id(u) 6= ρ, then there exists v ∈ N (u) s.t. id(v) = parent(u) and depth(v) = depth(u) − 1.
If all checks are passed at every node, then there exists a spanning tree T of G rooted at ρ such that
the function parent defines the parent relation in T , and depth(u) is the depth of u in T .
   In the above proposition, if all checks are passed at every node, then the set

                               {(root(u), parent(u), depth(u)) | u ∈ V }

is called the encoding of the tree T . Given a graph G and a forest F ⊆ G with components

Proof. On legal instances (f, g) for network G = (V, E), the prover provides each vertex u ∈ V

its neighbors on input f (u) during the i-th round of A.
    The verification algorithm proceeds as follows. Every node collects the certificates of its neigh-
bors, and simulates the R(n) rounds of A. That is, u checks first whether m1 (u) is indeed the
message that it would send in the first round of A. For every i ∈ {2, . . . , R(n)}, node u assumes

whether mi (u) is indeed the message that it would broadcast at round i. If some of these tests are
not passed then u rejects. If all tests are passed, then u finally checks whether g(u) is the output
that u would produce by executing A, and accepts or rejects accordingly.
    The completeness and soundness of the algorithm follow by construction.

5.2   Certifying Treedepth Colorings and Skeletons
[22] have shown that, for every MSO property P, there is a certification scheme using O(log n)-bit
certificates for certifying P on graphs of bounded treedepth. Since the mere property “treedepth
at most k” is expressible in MSO, we obtain the following.

Proposition 5 ([22]). For every integer p ≥ 1, there exists a certification scheme using O(log n)-bit
certificates for certifying “treedepth at most p”.

extend this certification scheme to an encoding of a p-skeleton. Given a (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring

of G[U ], for all U ∈ Col(p). More precisely, in the encoding of τp , for every U ∈ Col(p), every node

the decomposition forest F U of G[U ].

6     Lower Bounds and Impossibility Results
In this section we give evidence that our results somehow represent the limit of tractabilty of dis-
tributed model checking and distributed certification of properties on graphs of bounded expansion.
    All our reductions are based on classical results in communication complexity. Let f : X × Y →
{0, 1} be a function. The deterministic communication complexity of f D(f ) corresponds to the
minimum number of bits that must be exchanged in any deterministic protocol between a player
Alice holding an input x ∈ X and another player Bob y ∈ Y in order to output the value f (x, y).
The non-deterministic communication complexity of f , N (f ) is defined similarly, but for non-
deterministic communication protocols. We refer to the book of Kushilevitz and Nissan for more
detailed definitions [59].



                                                   43
    In important problem in communication complexity is set disjointedness. It is defined by func-
tion Disjn . For each n the inputs of Disjn are subsets of [n]. Given A, B ⊆ [n] we have that
Disj(A, B) = 1 if and only if A ∩ B = ∅.

Proposition 6 ([59]). D(Disjn ) = Ω(n) and N (Disjn ) = Ω(n).

6.1   No Extension to Monadic Second-Order Logic
We first show that it is not possible to extend our results to the whole set of MSO properties on
graphs of bounded expansion. We illustrate this fact by considering the property of being non-three-
colorable. We define Non-3-Coloring the set of graphs that do not admit a proper three-coloring
(their chromatic number is at least 4). The membership in Non-3-Coloring can be represented by
the following MSO formula:
                                                                                                   
    ∀C1 , C2 , C3 , ∃x, y, adj(x, y) ∧ (x ∈ C1 ∧ y ∈ C1 ) ∨ (x ∈ C2 ∧ y ∈ C2 ) ∨ (x ∈ C3 ∧ y ∈ C3 )

   In the following theorem, we show that there are no efficient CONGEST algorithms for checking
nor compact certification scheme for certifying the membership in Non-3-Coloring, even under the
promise that the input graph belongs to a class of graphs of bounded expansion and of logarithmic
diameter.

Theorem 5. Let G the class of graphs of maximum degree 5 and diameter O(log n). Every
CONGEST algorithm deciding the membership in Non-3-Coloring of a n-node input graph in G
requires Ω(n/ log2 n) rounds of communication.
    Every certification scheme that certifies membership to Non-3-Coloring of a n-node input graph
in G requires certificates of size Ω(n/ log n).

Proof. [46] show that every locally checkable proof, and therefore every proof-labeling scheme
for Non-3-Coloring requires certificates of size Ω(n2 / log n). The same proof directly implies the
same lowerbound for the number of rounds of a CONGEST algorithm deciding the membership in
Non-3-Coloring. We give an sketch of the proof of Göös and Suomella and then we show how to
adapt it to the case when the input graph has maximum degree 4.
    The proof of [46] is consists in a reduction to Disjn2 . In the reduction each instance (A, B) of
Disjn2 is associated with a O(n)-node graph GA,B . The set of vertices of GA,B is divided in two
sets of vertices, denoted VA and VB , and satisfies the following conditions:

(C1) The graph induced by VA only depends on A.

(C2) The graph induced by VB only depends on B.

(C3) The set of edges EA,B with one endpoint in VA and the other in VB contains at most O(log n)
     edges, and does not depend on A and B, only on n. The nodes with endpoints in EA,B are
     denoted VA,B .

(C4) A ∩ B = ∅ if and only if GA,B ∈ Non-3-Coloring.

Then, given a certification scheme P for Non-3-Coloring with certificates of size c(n) on n-node
graphs, we build the following non-deterministic protocol for Disjn2 . First, Alice and Bob build
graph GA,B , where Alice builds the subgraph depending on A, i.e., VA and all edges incident to VA ,


                                                 44
and Bob the part depending on B. Then, non-deterministically each player generate the certificates
of all the nodes of GA,B , and communicate to the other player the certificates generated for the
nodes in the cut. Then, the players run the verification algorithm of P and accept if all the nodes
on their side accept. We have that both players accept if and only if GA,B ∈ Non-3-Coloring, which
holds if and only if A ∩ B = ∅. The total communication is O(log n · c(n)). Using Proposition 6 we
obtain that O(log n · c(n)) = N (Disjn2 ) = Ω(n2 ). Therefore, c(n) = Ω(n2 / log n).
    The construction is also useful for a lowerbound in the CONGEST model. Let A be an algorithm

as above, and simulate the R(n) rounds of A on it, communicating on each round to other player

accept. On each communication round the players send O(log n) bits per edge of EA,B . Hence, the
nodes interchange O(R(n) · |EA,B | · log n) = O(R(n) · log2 n) bits in total. From Proposition 6 we
obtain that O(R(n) · log2 n) = D(Disjn2 ) = Ω(n2 ). Therefore, R(n) = Ω(n2 / log2 n).

7    Conclusion
In this paper, we have established that, for every FO formula ϕ, and every graph class G of bounded
expansion, there is a distributed model checking algorithm deciding whether G |= ϕ in O(D + log n)
rounds in the CONGEST model, for every graph G ∈ G. In the distributed framework, this result
can be viewed as an analog of the FPT algorithms for model-checking FO formulas on classes of
bounded expansion derived in [16] for centralized computing. A natural question is whether our
result could be extended to a more general class of sparse graphs. In the centralized model checking,

                                                 48
the fixed-parameter tractability of FO was extended from graphs of bounded expansion to a more
general, namely nowhere dense, class of graphs in [49]. Note that nowhere dense graphs represent the
ultimate limit of the program in designing fixed-parameter tractable algorithms for model checking
FO formulas. Indeed, as proven in [16], for any graph class G that is not nowhere dense (i.e., G is
somewhere dense), and is closed under taking subgraphs, model checking FO formulas on G is not
fixed-parameter tractable (unless FPT = W[1]). Where the tractability border for CONGEST is
unknown. The question is therefore twofold: (1) Is it possible to design model checking algorithms
for FO formulas running in Õ(D) rounds in CONGEST for nowhere dense graphs of diameter D?
(2) Is the class of nowhere dense graphs the ultimate limit of this program under the CONGEST
model?
     There are several difficulties in extending our approach to nowhere dense graphs. First, it is
unclear how to implement the combinatorial arguments from [49], particularly the constructions
of sparse neighborhood covers, in distributed model. Second, the proof of [49] builds on a weak
variant of Gaifman local form and not a quantifier elimination procedure. While this is sufficient
for fixed-parameter tractability of FO model-checking, we do not know whether it could be adapted
in the distributed setting.
     As far as our specific results are concerned, two questions arise. First, could we remove the
additional logarithmic term from the complexity of our model checking algorithm? This term
reflects the coloring algorithm’s complexity in [68] for graph classes of bounded expansion. So, the
question is whether it is possible to compute such a coloring in O(D) rounds, or even in a constant
number of rounds. Another intriguing question is already asked in [68], which we report here.
The question is whether it is possible, for every graph class of bounded expansion, to design an
O(log n)-round algorithm in CONGEST for identifying all nodes satisfying a “local” FO formula ϕ,
i.e., to mark all nodes v such that ϕ(v) holds, where ϕ(x) refers only to nodes at constant distance
from x.
