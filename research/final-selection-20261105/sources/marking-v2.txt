                                                                                            Abstract
                                                    The sparsity theory of Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez provides generic combinatorial and
                                                algorithmic methods suitable for many classes of sparse graphs. As such, it has already driven
                                                major advances in model checking, parameterized complexity, and approximation algorithms.
                                                We show that this theory is equally well suited to the design of distributed algorithms, providing
                                                the first generic algorithmic results for distributed first-order (FO) model checking on graphs
                                                of bounded expansion. The latter form a rich graph family which subsumes planar, bounded-
                                                genus, bounded-treedepth, bounded-treewidth, and bounded-degree graphs, as well as graphs
                                                excluding a fixed minor or topological minor, sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs (a.a.s.), and several
                                                network models such as stochastic block models for suitable parameter ranges.
                                                    Our main results are the following algorithmic meta-theorems for distributed algorithms in
                                                the standard CONGEST model on every graph class G of bounded expansion.
                                                   • First, we resolve an open problem originally posed by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez
                                                     (Distributed Computing 2016), and reiterated by Pilipczuk, Siebertz, and Toruńczyk
                                                     (LICS 2018). Recall that a formula φ(x) is local if the satisfaction of φ(x) depends only
                                                     on the r-neighbourhood of its free variable x, for some fixed r. For instance, the formula
                                                     “x belongs to a triangle” is local. We show that, for every local FO formula φ(x), there
                                                     exists a deterministic algorithm that, for every n-vertex graph G ∈ G, identifies all vertices
                                                     v ∈ V (G) such that G |= φ(v), in O(log n) rounds.
                                                   • Second, we show that, for every FO formula φ, there is a deterministic algorithm that, for
                                                     every n-vertex graph G ∈ G, decides whether G |= φ, in O(D + log n) rounds, where D is
                                                     the diameter of G. Note that the dependence on D is unavoidable, as even the simple FO
                                                     formula “there exist two vertices of degree 3” needs Ω(D) rounds in CONGEST, even on
                                                     trees.
                                                The techniques behind these meta-theorems extend to distributed counting, optimization, and
                                                certification problems on graphs of bounded expansion.

1     Introduction
1.1     Context and Objective
What is a sparse graph? The answer to this question depends on the perspective. For instance, the
graph class in which every graph is obtained from a complete graph by subdividing every edge once
is “combinatorially sparse” (it has a number of edges linear in the number of vertices), but is not
necessarily “effectively sparse” from an algorithmic perspective. The sparsity theory by Nešetřil
and Ossona de Mendez [53, 54] provided us with an approach of sparsity that can be effectively
used algorithmically.

1.1.1    Graphs of Bounded Expansion
Within the sparsity theory, a crucial role is assigned to the graph classes of bounded expansion.
Intuitively, a graph class G has bounded expansion if there exists a function f : N → N such that,
for every G ∈ G, and any r ≥ 0, every minor H of G obtained by contracting disjoint subgraphs of
radius at most r is f (r)-degenerate (i.e., every subgraph of H has a vertex of degree at most f (r)).
Bounded expansion can be thought of as strengthened degeneracy, which must persist even after
performing “local minor operations”. For instance, the aforementioned graph class containing every
graph obtained from a complete graph by subdividing every edge once is not of bounded expansion.
    Graph classes with bounded expansion form a rich graph family (see Figure 1). For instance,
the class of planar graphs, the class of graphs with bounded genus, the class of graphs of bounded
degree, and the class of minor-free graphs and topologically minor-free graphs are all of bounded
expansion, as well as sparse Erdős–Rényi graphs (a.a.s.). Moreover, [15] demonstrated that many
network models (e.g., stochastic block models) exhibit bounded expansion for some ranges of pa-
rameters, and these findings are even supported with empirical measurements on a corpus of real-
world networks. All these points make graph classes of bounded expansion quite attractive from
both conceptual and practical perspectives. In particular, it has already driven major advances
in model checking [18], parameterized complexity [53], kernelization [16, 20] and approximation
algorithms [43].

1.1.2    Distributed Computing in Graphs of Bounded Expansion
Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez also showed that bounded expansion can also be approached from
a distributed computing perspective (see [55]). Their finding is based on the following characteriza-
tion [54]. A class G of graphs has bounded expansion if and only if there exists a function g : N → N
such that, for every G ∈ G, and for every k ∈ N, there exists a coloring of the vertices of G with
colors in [g(k)] = {1, . . . , g(k)} such that, for every set U ⊆ [g(k)] of cardinality at most k, the
subgraph of G induced by the nodes colored with the colors in U has treedepth at most |U |. (Recall
that a graph G = (V, E) has treedepth at most d if there exists a forest F = (V, E ′ ) of rooted trees,
on the same set of vertices as G, such that (1) every tree in F has depth at most d, and (2) for
every edge in E, one of its end points is an ancestor of the other in some tree of F .)
    Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez [55] established that for every graph class G of bounded expan-
sion f , there exists a distributed algorithm which, for every positive integer k, and for every G ∈ G,
computes a g(k)-coloring of the nodes of G satisfying the desired treedepth property, in O(log n)

1.1.3    Deciding Local First-Order Formulas in CONGEST
The fact that a coloring satisfying the requirement of bounded expansion can be computed in a
distributed manner yields the question of whether this ability can be used as a building block for
solving “hard problems” efficiently in CONGEST, in graph classes of bounded expansion. In the
context of CONGEST, “hard problems” are usually thought as problems requiring at least poly(n)
rounds to be solved, whereas “easy problems” are problems solvable in poly log(n) rounds. As a
concrete illustration, it is known [17] that deciding C4 -freeness is hard in CONGEST, as it requires
   √
Ω̃( n) rounds — in the distributed setting, deciding a property asks all nodes to accept if the
property is satisfied, and at least one node to reject if the property is violated. Is it possible to
                   √
overcome the Ω̃( n) lower bound for deciding C4 -freeness in graphs of bounded expansion?
    Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez [55] actually answered positively to this question, by highlighting
a general approach for deciding H-freeness, in O(log n) rounds, for any fixed (connected) graph H.
In fact, this approach can even be used to mark all nodes v such that v belongs to some subgraph
of G isomorphic to H. However, they asked a broader and more challenging question, reiterated
by Pilipczuk, Siebertz, and Toruńczyk [59]. Recall that a formula φ(x) is local if the satisfaction
of φ(x) depends only on the r-neighborhood of its free variable x, for some fixed r. For instance,
in graphs, the formula “node x belongs to a 4-cycle” is indeed local. The question in [55, 59] goes
way beyond deciding H-freeness. It can be formulated as follows:

1.2     Our Results
Our first main result is a positive answer to the open problem in [55, 59]. That is, we show that,
for every local FO formula φ(x), and for every graph class G of bounded expansion, there exists
a deterministic algorithm that, for every n-vertex graph G ∈ G, marks all vertices v ∈ V (G) such
that G |= φ(v), in O(log n) rounds in the CONGEST model. In fact, this result also holds for FO
formulas on labeled graphs, i.e., graphs in which every node is provided with labels drawn from a
finite set Λ. That is, the formulas may also include unary predicates on node labels. Let us call a
Λ-labeled graph any graph whose nodes are label by labels in Λ. A Λ-labeled graph is thus a pair
(G, ℓ) where G = (V, E) is a graph, and ℓ : V → 2Λ is a label-assignment to the nodes of G. Our
first main theorem is the following:

Theorem 1.1. Let Λ be a finite set. For every local FO formula φ(x) on Λ-labeled graphs, and
for every class of graphs G of bounded expansion, there exists a deterministic algorithm that, for
every n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G, and every ℓ : V → 2Λ , marks all vertices v ∈ V such that
(G, ℓ) |= φ(v), in O(log n) rounds under the CONGEST model.

   Before summarizing our techniques used to solve the open problem in [55, 59], it is worth
mentioning that we extend this result in several directions.

1.2.1    Extension 1: Deciding FO formulas
An immediate corollary of our result on local FO formulas is that, for every formula φ such as
φ = ∀x ψ(x) where ψ(x) is a local FO formula (e.g., H-freeness), and for every graph class G
of bounded expansion, deciding whether G |= φ can be done in O(log n) rounds in the CONGEST
model (by merely applying our algorithm to ¬ψ(x), and letting every marked node reject). However,
Gaifman’s theorem [29] tells us that not all FO formulas are as simple as that. For instance, even
the simple FO formula “there exist two vertices of degree 3” needs Ω(D) rounds to be checked in
CONGEST, where D denotes the diameter of the graph — this lower bound holds even for trees.
In other words, a dependence on D is unavoidable, even for trees. Nevertheless, our second main
result states that, for every FO formula φ, and for every graph class G of bounded expansion,
there exists a deterministic algorithm that, for every n-vertex graph G ∈ G of diameter D, decides
whether G |= φ, in O(D + log n) rounds. Again, our result holds for labeled graphs too, and is
formally stated as follows:

Theorem 1.2. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula φ on Λ-labeled graphs, and for every
class of graphs G of bounded expansion, there exists a distributed algorithm that, for every n-node
network G = (V, E) ∈ G of diameter D, and every ℓ : V → 2Λ , decides whether (G, ℓ) |= φ in
O(D + log n) rounds under the CONGEST model.

counting solutions of FO formulas with free variables. We actually consider two forms of counting:
   • Local counting: Given a local formula φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) with k ≥ 1 free variables, such as
     adj(x1 , x2 )∧adj(x2 , x3 )∧adj(x3 , x1 ) (i.e., x1 , x2 , x3 form a triangle), each node v of the consid-

   • Global counting: Given a general formula φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) with k ≥ 1 free variables, all nodes
     output a same value equal to the number of tuples v1 , . . . , vk for which (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ).
     E.g., counting the k-tuples of nodes of degree 3.

Theorem 1.3. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) on Λ-labeled graphs
with k ≥ 1 free variables x1 , . . . , xk , and for every class of graphs G of bounded expansion, there
exists a distributed algorithm that, for every n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G of diameter D and
every ℓ : V → 2Λ , performs global counting in O(D + log n) rounds in CONGEST. If the for-
mula φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) is local then there exists a distributed algorithm that performs local counting in
O(log n) rounds in CONGEST.
    Note that, in contrast to our Theorem 1.3, the best known algorithm for (locally) counting
triangles in general graphs takes Õ(n1/3 ) rounds [9].
    Some optimization problems can also be expressed as finding optimal solutions for FO formulas
with free variables, over weighted graphs, e.g., finding a maximum-weight triangle. (If no triangles
exist in the graph, then all nodes reject.) Note that, as usual in the literature on CONGEST [57],
the weights assigned to the nodes or edges are assumed to be polynomial in the network size so
that any weight can be transmitted between neighbors in a single round of communication. We
prove the following theorem.
Theorem 1.4. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) on Λ-labeled graphs
with k ≥ 1 free variables x1 , . . . , xk , and for every class graphs G of bounded expansion, there
exists a distributed algorithm that, for every n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G of diameter D, every
ℓ : V → 2Λ , and every weight function        P ω : V → N, computes a k-tuple of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk )
such that (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ) and ki=1 ω(vi ) is maximum, in O(D + log n) rounds under the
CONGEST model. (The same holds if replacing maximum by minimum.) If no tuples (v1 , . . . , vk )
exist such that (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ), then all nodes reject.
   Theorem 1.4 allows to find, for example, a monochromatic triangle of minimum weight, or a
multi-colored k-independent set of maximum weight, in a class G of c-colored graphs of bounded
expansion.

                                                      4
    Last but not least, our results extend to distributed certification. Roughly, a distributed certifi-
cation scheme [36, 48] for a graph property φ is a pair prover-verifier. The prover is a centralized,
computationally unlimited oracle that assigns certificates to the nodes. The verifier is a 1-round
distributed algorithm whose role is to check whether the certificates assigned to the nodes form a
proof that the graph satisfies the property. A distributed certification scheme is correct if it satisfies
completeness (i.e., if (G, ℓ) |= φ, then the prover can assign certificates such that the verifier accepts
at all nodes), and soundness (i.e., if (G, ℓ) ̸|= φ, then, no matter the certificates assigned to the
nodes, the verifier rejects in at least one node). The main complexity measure is the size of the
certificates assigned by the prover on legal instances.
Theorem 1.5. Let Λ be a finite set. For every FO formula φ on Λ-labeled graphs, and for every
class of graphs G of bounded expansion, there exists a distributed certification scheme that, for every
n-node network G = (V, E) ∈ G and every ℓ : V → 2Λ , certifies (G, ℓ) |= φ using certificates on
O(log n) bits.
    Note that, with O(log n)-bit certificates, the verifier can be implemented in a single round of
communication in the CONGEST model, as desired. Note also that our certification scheme is
actually a proof-labeling scheme [48], which is a more demanding form of distributed certification
than, e.g., locally checkable proofs [36]. As a direct consequence of Theorem 1.5, certifying C4 -
freeness can be done with O(log n)-bit certificates in graphs of bounded expansion. In contrast,
                                                     √
certifying C4 -freeness requires certificates on Ω̃( n) bits in general graphs, using the reduction
from [17]. More generally, [22] shows that every MSO formula can be certified with O(log n)-
bit certificates in graphs of bounded treedepth. Theorem 1.5 extends this result. Indeed, while
Theorem 1.5 is for FO, and the result of [22] is for MSO, both logics have identical expressive
power on graphs of bounded treedepth. Last but not least, as for our model-checking algorithm,
Theorem 1.5 is the best that can be achieved. Indeed, we show that it does not extend to MSO, as
                                                          √
certifying non-3-colorability requires certificates of Ω̃( n) bits in planar graphs (see Theorem 7.3),
and even certificates of Θ̃(n) bits in bounded degree graphs (see Theorem 7.2).

Remark. The open problem by Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez was actually stated for the
BROADCAST CONGEST model, i.e., a weaker version of the CONGEST model in which, at ev-
ery round, each node v must send the same O(log n)-bit message to all its neighbors (see, e.g.,
[17, 47]). However, (1) the problem is not necessarily easier to solve in the general version of the
CONGEST model, and (2) we solve it anyway by the affirmative using an algorithm designed for the
BROADCAST CONGEST model, and the complexity of our algorithm is expressed under the latter.
For the sake of simplifying the notations, we simply refer to CONGEST in the statements of our the-
orems, but all our upper bound results (but Theorem 1.4) hold under the BROADCAST CONGEST
model too2 .

guaranteed that at least one set U of k colors captures k nodes for which ψ holds whenever such
nodes exist. For instance, for C4 freeness, for any coloring of the nodes with at least 4 colors, there
exists a set U of at most 4 colors such that, whenever there is a 4-cycle in the graph, this 4-cycle
appears in the subgraph induced by the vertices colored with some color in U . Assuming given
an appropriate g(k)-coloring, the quantifier elimination can proceed by considering all sets U of k
colors, and eliminating the existential quantifier in graphs of bounded treedepth.
    The second stage involves eventually eliminating the existential quantifier by transforming a
formula on labeled graphs of bounded treedepth with an existential quantifier into a formula on
labeled forests of bounded depth without existential quantifiers. This is achieved by encoding
the existential quantifier into a disjunction of quantifier-free formulas, each referring to a specific
finite subgraph pattern. To encode the subgraph pattern, new unary and binary predicates are
introduced. These unary predicates are called label predicates. Their aim is to specify the presence
of certain symbols in the labels assigned to the nodes. The new binary predicates are called least
common ancestor predicates, or simply lca predicate for short. Their aim is to specify the depth of
the least common ancestor of two nodes in certain trees of the forest. The subgraph patterns are
encoded as Boolean combinations of label and lca predicates.

1.3.2    Locality-Preserving Quantifier Elimination
A first novelty of this paper is to adapt the approach of [59] such that each step preserves the
locality property of the considered formula. To that end, we extend the notion of local formula to
more that one free variables: Roughly, a formula ψ(x, ȳ) is local if (1) (G, ℓ) |= φ(x, ȳ) implies that
ȳ ⊆ BG (x, r), i.e., each yi is at distance at most r from x, and (2) for every two graphs G and G′ ,
and every two vertices x and x′ of G and G′ , respectively, if there is a (label preserving) isomorphism
h : BG (x, r) → BG′ (x′ , r) satisfying h(x) = x′ , then (G, ℓ) |= φ(x, ȳ) ⇐⇒ (G′ , ℓ) |= φ(h(x), h(ȳ)).
This “semantic” definition is difficult to use in practice, which is why we introduce a “syntactic”
notion of local form for local formulas. For this purpose, we introduce new predicates, called
distance predicates. These predicates are of the form ball(y, x, r), interpreted as “y is in the ball
of radius r around x in the graph”. The distance predicates allow us to syntactically enforce
the locality property of a formula. For instance, in a formula ball(y, x, r) ∧ φ(x, y), the distance
predicate enforces the fact that x and y are at bounded distance. At each step of the quantifier
elimination process, we make sure that both the original formula φ, and the transformed formula
φb explicitly contain distance predicates that guarantee their locality.
    The presence of negations in the formula, potentially induced by universal quantifiers treated as
negated existential quantifiers, must be treated with care. Indeed, the locality property is not stable
by negation. For instance, the negation of ball(y, x, r) ∧ φ(x, y) is ¬ball(y, x, r) ∨ ¬φ(x, y), which
is not local because ¬ball(y, x, r) is satisfied for nodes y that are far away from x. To overcome

                                                       7
this issue, we introduce redundancy in the syntax of local formulas. That is, instead of adding
distance predicates once in the formula (for instance, immediately after the innermost quantifier),
we duplicate the distance predicates wherever they are needed.
    For instance, let us consider a formula φ(x, ȳ) = ∀z ψ(x, ȳ, z) that is assumed to be local. As said
before, quantifier elimination proceeds by eliminating existential quantifiers, and thus this formula
is rewritten as φ(x, ȳ) = ¬∃z ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z). Now, we make the locality of z explicit by rewriting φ as
                                                                          
                               φ(x, ȳ) = ¬∃z ball(z, x, r) ∧ ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z) .

Moreover, we use redundancy, and reinforce the locality of ȳ by introducing another distance
predicate:                                                                     
                   φ(x, ȳ) = ball(ȳ, x, r) ∧ ¬∃z ball(z, x, r) ∧ ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z) .
By distributing the distance predicate on ȳ, we get
                                                                                             
                φ(x, ȳ) = ball(ȳ, x, r) ∧ ¬∃z ball(ȳ, x, r) ∧ ball(z, x, r) ∧ ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z) ,

which can be rewritten as
                                                                                       
                     φ(x, ȳ) = ball(ȳ, x, r) ∧ ¬∃z ball((ȳ, z), x, r) ∧ ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z) .

As a result, the potential loss of locality resulting from the negation of ψ(x, ȳ, z) is recovered thanks
to the (redundant) distance predicate, as ball((ȳ, z), x, r) ∧ ¬ψ(x, ȳ, z) is local. Eliminating the
existential quantifier in this formula yields a (local) quantifier-free formula. Negation is, however,
applied a second time, which may result in losing locality. Yet, locality is again recovered using the
other (redundant) distance predicate ball(ȳ, x, r).

1.3.3   Quantifier Elimination in CONGEST
The second novelty of this paper is the implementation of quantifier elimination, including the new
constraint of preserving locality, in CONGEST, specifically in BROADCAST CONGEST. We had to
tackle two main difficulties.
     First, the distributed implementation of the techniques in [59] requires technical attention in
several places. There are indeed operations that are very simple to perform in the centralized
setting (they are even often implicit when described in the centralized algorithm), but it is not
necessarily clear whether all these operations are local (i.e., involving nodes that are close in the
graph), and frugal in term of bandwidth (i.e., requiring neighboring nodes to exchange a limited
amount of information). As a consequence, we had to carefully revisit the entire construction and
’open all boxes’ to ensure that each step of the construction can be implemented efficiently in
(BROADCAST) CONGEST.
     Second, model checking over graphs of bounded expansion actually refers to model checking
of relational structures whose Gaifman graphs are of bounded expansion. The techniques from
[59] (as well as other related approaches) involve enriching the structure relative to an arbitrary
first-order formula with new symbols and predicates to obtain a quantifier-free formula. Enriching
the structure naturally modifies Gaifman graphs. In contrast, in distributed computing models
such as CONGEST, nodes establish communications over a network that does not change. When
enriching the structure, one must therefore be very careful to avoid creating connections between
nodes that cannot directly communicate in the network.

performs dynamic programming over this tree decomposition. If the input graph of small treedepth

from the leaves, upward to the root of the tree decomposition by communicating along the edges
of G. Altogether, the whole process for counting and optimization for formula φ on the pair (G, ℓ)
costs O(D + log n) rounds in CONGEST.

reduce the study to quantifier-free formulas, using the (locality preserving) quantifier elimination
method. Similarly to the case of global counting, this reduction introduces lca-predicates, which
need to be eliminated in a locality-preserving fashion. It turns out that the method applied to the
case of global counting happens to preserve locality, although the proof is somewhat technical. For
quantifier-free formulas, we essentially apply the same idea as for global counting, based on the
inclusion-exclusion principle. However, we also use the fact that the formula is local, by considering
some set of colors U , and a tuple of vertices v such that (G[U ], ℓ) satisfies φ(v). Then, since φ is
local, all vertices in v belong to the same connected component of G[U ], which implies that we only
need to count the solutions to φ in the connected components of G[U ]. The latter are connected
graphs of bounded treedepth, and thus counting is achieved in O(1) rounds using the algorithm
in [25].

1.4     Related Work
The results of our paper lie at the intersection of two research directions. The first is the study
of distributed computing and distributed certification schemes on sparse networks, such as planar
graphs, or graphs excluding a fixed minor, for fundamental graph optimization problems. The
second is the development of meta-theorems for sparse graph classes in centralized computation.

1.4.1    Distributed Computing on Sparse Networks
One of the popular areas of research in distributed computing concerns the study of classical com-
binatorial problems on sparse networks, including planar graphs, bounded-degree graphs, graphs of
bounded treewidth, and graphs excluding a fixed minor. In such settings, a central goal is to develop
distributed algorithms that exploit structural sparsity to achieve improved efficiency—compared
to general networks—particularly in terms of communication rounds. Typical problems studied in
this context include coloring, matching, dominating sets, cuts, spanning trees, and network decom-
positions. Research directions focus on understanding how sparsity affects the complexity of these
problems in standard distributed models such as LOCAL and CONGEST, and on designing algo-
rithms with optimal or near-optimal round complexity. This line of work often builds on structural
properties of sparse networks, revealing deep and fruitful connections between structural graph
theory and distributed computation.
     Examples of the work in this direction include distributed algorithms for maximal matchings,
maximal independent sets (MIS), and colourings in graphs of bounded vertex degrees [56], depth-
first search (DFS) for planar graphs [34], minimum spanning tree (MST) and min-cut for planar,


                                                   11
bounded genus, and H-minor-free graphs [31, 32, 40, 41, 42, 33], minimum dominating set on planar
and H-minor-free graphs [1, 4, 12, 13, 52], and MIS for graphs excluding a fixed minor [8].
    The round complexity Õ(D) achieved in Theorem 1.2, is considered a “gold standard” for
algorithms on sparse graphs in the CONGEST model. It has been obtained, for instance, for the
Minimum Spanning Tree (MST) problem on planar graphs and, more generally, on H-minor-free
graphs [31, 32, 40, 41, 42, 33]. Algorithms with Õ(D) complexity in the CONGEST model have
also been developed for bounded-treewidth graphs for a variety of problems, including matching,
the directed single-source shortest paths problem, bipartite unweighted maximum matching, and
girth [46].
    We are not aware of any meta-theorem that combines logical and combinatorial constraints
within the CONGEST model, with one exception. The paper [25] establishes that every MSO for-
mula on graphs of bounded treedepth is decidable in a constant number of rounds in the CONGEST
model. The results of [25] are incomparable with Theorems 1.1 and 1.2. Indeed, Theorems 1.1
and 1.2 apply to graph classes that are significantly broader than graphs of bounded treedepth,
whereas the number of rounds in the algorithm of [25] is independent of the number of vertices in
the input graph. We incorporate some of the methods developed in [25] in our work.
    Regarding distributed algorithms on graphs of bounded expansion, the foundational work in
this area is due to Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez [55]. In the CONGEST model, they presented a
distributed algorithm for computing a low treedepth decomposition of graphs of bounded expansion
in O(log n) rounds. We use this construction as a subroutine in our algorithms. In the same paper,
Nešetřil and Ossona de Mendez also used this decomposition to design an algorithm for checking
whether a graph of bounded expansion contains a given connected subgraph H of constant size,
also in O(log n) rounds. Theorem 1.1 significantly extends this result.
    Approximation algorithms for minimum dominating set on graphs of bounded expansion in
LOCAL model were developed in [1, 44, 50]. In [62], Siebertz and Vigny discuss the complexity of the
model-checking problem for first-order logic on graphs of bounded expansion, in the CONGESTED
CLIQUE model.
    Distributed certification is a very active area of research, see [23] for a survey. Several meta-
theorems are known for distributed certification. A significant result in the field was achieved in
[22], who demonstrated that any MSO formula can be locally certified on graphs with bounded
treedepth using a logarithmic number of bits per node, representing a gold standard in certification.
This theorem has numerous implications for certification; for more details, we refer to [22]. Notably,
the FO property of C4 -freeness and the MSO property of non-3-colorability, which typically require
polynomial-size certificates in general graphs, can be certified with just O(log n)-bit certificates in
graphs of bounded treedepth. The result by Bousquet et al. has been extended to more compre-
hensive classes of graphs, including graphs excluding a small minor [7], graphs of bounded treewidth
[2, 10, 27], and graphs of bounded cliquewidth [26].

1.4.2   Centralized Meta-Theorems
Meta-theorems, according to Grohe and Kreutzer [38], are the results that certain problems of
model checking for formulas of some logic are tractable (in some sense) on some class of graphs.
One of the most celebrated examples of a meta-theorem is Courcelle’s theorem, which asserts that
graph properties definable in MSO are decidable in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth
[11]. There is a long line of meta-theorems for FO logic on sparse graph classes in the centralized


                                                  12
model of computation. The starting point is Seese’s theorem [61] on linear time decidability of
FO on graphs of bounded degree. [28] gave linear time algorithms for planar graphs and all apex-
minor-free graph classes, as well as an O(n1+ε ) algorithms for graphs of bounded local tree-width.
[24] proved that deciding FO properties is fixed-parameter tractable on graph classes with excluded
minors. The next step to classes of graphs locally excluding a minor was done by [14].
    The Sparsity Theory of [54] opened new horizons for model checking on sparse graphs. [18]
proved that FO properties can be decided in linear time on graph classes of bounded expansion.
Further extensions of these results are known for nowhere-dense graphs [39] and graphs of bounded
twin-width [5]. See [49] and [37] for surveys.
    From a technical perspective, the most important aspect for our work is the quantifier elimina-
tion procedure introduced in [18] and [38], as well as in [59]. Similar to these works, we apply the
idea of modifying the structure by adding new unary relations and functions without changing the
Gaifman graph.


2     Model and Definitions
In this section, we recall basic concepts of distributed computing, logic, and graphs. Readers
familiar with these frameworks may skip the first two sub-sections, but Section 2.3, which introduces
and extends the notion of local formulas, and Section 2.4, which recalls properties of graphs with
bounded expansion, may require special attention.

2.1   The CONGEST Model
The CONGEST model is a standard model of distributed computation in networks [57]. The
network is modeled as a connected simple (i.e., no self-loops nor multiple edges) graph G = (V, E).
The n ≥ 1 vertices of G model the processing nodes in the networks, and its edges models the
communication links. In the following, we may indistinguishably use “node” of “vertex” for referring
to the processing elements in the network. Each node is assigned an identifier, which is assumed to
be unique in the network. The identifiers may not be between 1 and n, but the CONGEST model
assumes that each identifier can be stored on O(log n) bits in n-node networks. In fact, it is usually
assumed that all the nodes of a graph G are given a polynomial upper bound N = poly(n) on the
number n of vertices in G, with all identifiers in {1, . . . , N }.
    Computation proceeds in lockstep, as a sequence of synchronous rounds. All nodes start si-
multaneously, at the same round. At each round, every node v sends an O(log n)-bit message to
each of its neighbors, receives the messages sent by all its neighbors, and performs some individual
computation. The round-complexity of an algorithm A is the function RA (n) = max|V (G)|≤n RA (G)
where RA (G) denotes the number of rounds performed by the nodes executing A in G until all of
them terminate.
    As said before, it is worth mentioning the BROADCAST CONGEST model, that is, the weaker
variant of the CONGEST model in which, at every round, each node must send the same O(log n)-
bit message to all its neighbors. For the sake of simplifying the notations, we solely refer to the
CONGEST model in the statements of our results. Yet, all our upper bound results also holds in
the weaker BROADCAST CONGEST model, apart from Theorem 1.4.

2.2     First-Order Formulas on Labeled Graphs
2.2.1    Definition
Recall that a first-order (FO) formula over graphs is a combination (conjunction, disjunction, and
negations) of Boolean predicates over variables, which may be quantified or not. Every variable
represents a vertex of a graph, and there are two binary predicates: equality x = y, and adjacency
adj(x, y), where the latter states whether or not there is an edge between the two vertices x and y.
The variables of an FO formula can be quantified with existential or universal quantifiers. Non-
quantified variables are called free variables. Given an FO formula φ with free variables x1 , . . . , xk ,
we make explicit that the latter are free by denoting φ = φ(x1 , . . . , xk ). In the following, FO
denotes the set of all first order formulas over undirected graphs.
     Throughout the paper, a tuple (x1 , . . . , xk ) is abbreviated in x. The length k of a tuple
(x1 , . . . , xk ) is then denoted by |x|. Expressions such as ∃x or ∀x are shorthand for ∃x1 . . . ∃xk
and ∀x1 . . . ∀xk , respectively. We may also abuse notation in context where no confusions may
occur by letting x denote the set {x1 , . . . , xk }.
     Given an FO formula φ and a graph G, G |= φ denotes that G satisfies φ (i.e., G is a model
for φ). The negation of G |= φ is denoted by G ̸|= φ. Given an FO formula φ(x) with free variables,
a graph G = (V, E), and a set of vertices v ∈ V k where k = |x|, we say that G satisfies φ(x) for
vertices v, denoted by G |= φ(v), if the formula is satisfied by G whenever assigning v to the free
variables of φ(x).
     A graph property is called a first-order (FO) property if it can be expressed by a first-order
formula with no free variables. For example, the property “the graph contains a triangle” is clearly
an FO property: it holds whenever there is a set of three pairwise adjacent vertices in the graph.
Indeed, it can be written as the formula
                                                                                       
                          φ = ∃x1 ∃x2 ∃x3 adj(x1 , x2 ) ∧ adj(x2 , x3 ) ∧ adj(x1 , x3 ) .

More generally, the property of containing a fixed graph H as a subgraph (sometimes called “sub-
graph isomorphism”) is an FO property. Its negation—namely, the property that a graph does not
contain H as a subgraph (often called “H-freeness”)—is also an FO property, since first-order logic
is closed under negation.
    A formula with free variables expresses properties over graphs and tuples of vertices. As an
example, the following formula ψ(x) holds whenever x is a node in a triangle, formulated as

                          ψ(x) = ∃y1 ∃y2 , adj(x, y1 ) ∧ adj(x, y2 ) ∧ adj(y1 , y2 ).

The graph G with vertex set V = {a, b, c, d}, and edge set E = {{a, b}, {a, c}, {b, c}, {c, d}} satisfies
G |= φ as the vertices {a, b, c} form a triangle. Similarly, G |= ψ(a), but G ̸|= ψ(d) as d is not in
any triangle.


                                                      14
2.2.2    FO Formulas for Labeled Graphs
The formalism of FO properties can be extended to labeled graphs. Let Λ be a finite set, whose
elements are called labels. A Λ-labeled graph is a pair (G, ℓ) where G = (V, E) is a graph, and
ℓ : V → 2Λ is an assignment of a set of labels to each vertex of G. An FO formula over Λ-labeled
graphs is a first order formula φ over graphs, extended with predicates of the form labλ (x) for every
λ ∈ Λ. The predicate labλ (x) is true if vertex x contains λ as one of its labels. Such predicates are
called label predicates. Whenever labλ (x) holds, we say that x is labeled with λ. Note that since
ℓ assigns a set of labels to each vertex, it may be the case that labλ (x) ∧ labλ′ (x) holds for two
different labels λ and λ′ . It may thus be convenient to define the predicates labS for sets S ⊆ Λ,
as follows. Given S ∈ Λ, labS (x) holds if and only if ℓ(x) = S. In particular, labS (x) ∧ labS ′ (x) is
necessarily false whenever S ̸= S ′ . The set of all first-order logic formulas over Λ-labeled graphs is
denoted FO[Λ].
    Given a Λ-labeled graph (G, ℓ), and a subgraph G′ of G, we abuse notation and denote (G′ , ℓ)
the Λ-labeled graph defined by the restriction of ℓ to the nodes in G′ . For instance, let Λ = {0, 1},
and let us consider the following graph property on labeled graphs: “existence of a triangle with a
node labeled 1”. This property can be expressed by the formula:

                    φ = ∃x1 ∃x2 ∃x3 , adj(x1 , x2 ) ∧ adj(x2 , x3 ) ∧ adj(x1 , x3 ) ∧ lab1 (x1 ).

We may also consider formula with free variables, such as

                       ψ(x) = ∃y1 ∃y2 , adj(x, y1 ) ∧ adj(x, y2 ) ∧ adj(y1 , y2 ) ∧ lab1 (x).

The graph G with vertex set V = {a, b, c, d} and edge set E = {{a, b}, {a, c}, {b, c}, {c, d}} with
labels ℓ(a) = {0}, ℓ(b) = {0}, ℓ(c) = {1}, and ℓ(d) = {1} satisfies (G, ℓ) |= φ as the nodes {a, b, c}
form a triangle where c is labeled 1. For the same reason, we have that (G, ℓ) |= ψ(c). However,
(G, ℓ) ̸|= ψ(a) as a is not labeled 1 (even if it is in a triangle), and (G, ℓ) ̸|= ψ(d) as node d is not
in a triangle (even if it is labeled 1).
    For φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) ∈ FO[Λ], and a Λ-labeled graph (G, ℓ), we denote by true(φ, G, ℓ) the set of
all tuples of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk ) satisfying that (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ). If φ has no free variables
then true(φ, G, ℓ) is equal to the truth value of (G, ℓ) |= φ. Two FO[Λ] formulas φ1 and φ2 are
equivalent if true(φ1 , G, ℓ) = true(φ2 , G, ℓ) for every Λ-labeled graph (G, ℓ). The following basic
definitions play a central role in this paper.
    • A formula φ(x) ∈ FO[Λ] is called quantifier-free if it has no quantifiers, i.e., it can be expressed
      as a boolean combination of predicates involving only the free variables in x.

    • A formula φ(x) ∈ FO[Λ] is existential if it can be written as φ(x) = ∃y ψ(x, y), where ψ(x, y)
      is quantifier-free formula in FO[Λ].
Furthermore, a formula φ(x) is in prenex normal form if it can be written in the form

                                        φ(x) = Q1 y1 . . . Qt yt ψ(x, y),

where ψ(x, y) ∈ FO[Λ], and Q1 , . . . , Qt are existential or universal quantifiers. The value of t is the
quantifier depth of φ. Every FO formula admits an equivalent formula in prenex normal form (see,
e.g., [21]). This fact can be easily established by induction on the length of the formula, and it can
be extended to FO[Λ], i.e., first order formulas for labeled graphs, in a straightforward manner.

Λ-labeled graphs (G, ℓ) with G = (V, E) ∈ G, and ℓ : V → 2Λ .

2.3    Local Formulas
Local formulas can be defined as follows. For a graph G = (V, E) and a vertex v ∈ V , let BG (v, r)
denote the r-neighborhood of v in G, that is, the subgraph of G induced by vertices at distance at
most r from v. By extension, when considering a labeled graph (G, ℓ), BG (v, r) is also a labeled
graph: each node u ∈ BG (v, r) is labeled by the same set of labels ℓ(u).
Definition 2.1. Let Λ be a finite set of labels, and let r ≥ 0 be an integer. A formula φ(x) ∈ F O[Λ]
with one free variable is r-local if, for any two Λ-labeled graphs (G, ℓ) and (G′ , ℓ′ ), and for any two
vertices v ∈ V (G) and v ′ ∈ V (G′ ), we have
                                                                                     
                 BG (v, r) = BG′ (v ′ , r) =⇒ (G, ℓ) |= φ(v) ⇐⇒ (G′ , ℓ′ ) |= φ(v ′ ) .

where BG (v, r) = BG′ (v ′ , r) means that there exists a label-preserving isomorphism h : BG (v, r) →
BG′ (v ′ , r) such that h(v) = v ′ .
   We say φ(x) is local if there exists r ≥ 0 such that φ(x) is r-local.
    For technical reasons that will appear clear later, we need to extend the notion of local formulas
to formulas φ(x, y) with more than a single free variable. Our notion of locality is defined with
respect to the first free variable x of the formula: intuitively, we require that each variable is in a
ball centered on x. This is different from the notion of Gaifman-locality [29], in which quantified
variables are required to be in a ball centered on any of the free variables.
Definition 2.2. Let Λ be a finite set of labels, and let r ≥ 0 be an integer. A formula φ(x, y) ∈
F O[Λ] is r-local if, for any Λ-labeled graph (G, ℓ) with G = (V, E), the following two properties are
satisfied:
   • for every pair (v, u) ∈ true(φ, G, ℓ), it holds that u ⊆ BG (v, r);
   • for every Λ-labeled graph (G′ , ℓ′ ) with G′ = (V ′ , E ′ ), for every vertices v ∈ V, v ′ ∈ V ′ , u ⊆
     BG (v, r) and u′ ⊆ BG′ (v ′ , r), if there exists a label-preserving isomorphism h : BG (v, r) →
     BG′ (v ′ , r) such that h(v) = h(v ′ ) and h(u) = h(u′ ), then:

                                    (G, ℓ) |= φ(v, u) ⇐⇒ (G′ , ℓ′ ) |= φ(v ′ , u′ )

    We shall often write φ(x) instead of φ(x, y), in which case it is x1 that takes the particular role
of x in the definition above.

formation on the form of the considered formula. We therefore define the notion of local form in
Definition 2.3. To that end, let us introduce the new predicate ball(y, x, r) defined as

                                    G |= ball(u, v, r) ⇐⇒ u ∈ BG (v, r).

redundancy is to guarantee that every “subformula” of φ remains local. Thanks to this redun-
dancy, all the formulas below are local, despite the fact that negation does not preserve locality for
formulas with several free variables. In particular, the following two formulas are equivalent, where
the universal quantifier is merely replaced by a negated existential quantifier:

2.4    Graphs of Bounded Expansion
Recall that a graph G = (V, E) has treedepth at most d if there exists a forest F of rooted trees
with depth at most d satisfying that there exists a one-to-one mapping f : V → V (F ) such that,
for every edge {u, v} ∈ E, f (u) is an ancestor of f (v) in a tree of F , or f (v) is an ancestor of f (u)
in a tree of F . The treedepth of a graph is the smallest d such that such a mapping f exists.
    It has been recently shown in [25] that decision, counting and optimization of FO formulas can
be performed in O(1) rounds in CONGEST, for connected labeled graphs of bounded treedepth.
Proposition 2.7 ([25]). Let G[Λ] be a class of connected labeled graphs of bounded treedepth, and
let φ(x1 , . . . , xk ) ∈ F O[Λ]. Then, for any and any (G, ℓ) ∈ G[Λ], the following problems can be
solved by a distributed algorithm running in O(1) rounds in CONGEST:

                                                          19
- Deciding φ: deciding whether         it   exists   a    k-uple   of   vertices   (v1 , . . . , vk )   such   that
     (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ).

- Counting the solutions of φ: computing the number of k-uples (v1 , . . . , vk ) such that
     (G, ℓ) |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ).

- Optimizing φ: assuming n-node graphs G = (V, E) are provided with a weight function
     ω : V → N satisfying ω(v) = O(nc )Pfor some c ≥ 0, computing a k-tuple of vertices (v1 , . . . , vk )
     such that G |= φ(v1 , . . . , vk ), and ki=1 ω(vi ) is maximum (or minimum).

   Graphs of bounded expansion [54] can be defined in term of local minors, and degeneracy,
as pointed out in the Introduction. It is however more convenient for us to use the following
characterization. A class G of graphs has bounded expansion if there exists a function f : N → N
such that, for every G ∈ G, and for every k ∈ N, there exists a coloring of the vertices of G
with colors in [f (k)] = {1, . . . , f (k)} such that, for every set U ⊆ [f (k)] of cardinality at most k
the subgraph of G induced by the nodes colored by a color in U has treedepth at most |U |. In
particular, each subset of at most k colors induces a subgraph of treedepth at most k. For every
positive integers p and k, a (p, k)-treedepth coloring of a graph G is a k-coloring of G satisfying
that each set U ⊆ [k] of at most p colors induces a graph of treedepth at most |U |.

Definition 2.8. Given a function f : N → N, a class of graphs G has expansion f if, for every
p ∈ N>0 , every G ∈ G has a (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring. A class of graphs has bounded expansion
if it has expansion f for some function f : N → N.

   A class of graphs has effective bounded expansion if it has expansion f for some Turing-
computable function f . In the following, we always assume that the considered functions f are
Turing-computable. Interestingly, treedepth colorings can be efficiently computed in the CONGEST
model.

Proposition 2.9 ([55]). Let G be a class of graphs of bounded expansion f . There exists a function
g : N → N such that, for any graph G ∈ G, and any constant positive integer p, a (p, g(p))-treedepth
coloring of G can be computed in O(log n) rounds in the CONGEST model.

    The algorithm of [55] takes as input the function f , and an integer p, and outputs a (p, g(p))-
treedepth coloring of the actual graph G in a distributed manner. The function g may however be
different from the given bound f on the expansion of the class G, which may itself be different from
the “best” expansion function for the class G.

3.1     Rooted Forests of Bounded Depth
In this section we adapt the quantifier elimination procedure from [59] for rooted forests of constant
depth (i.e., every tree in the forest is rooted and has constant depth) to the case of local formulas,
and show how to implement this procedure in CONGEST. The basic idea for eliminating the
quantifiers is to encode information about the satisfiability of the formula in node-labels. This
approach does increase the size of the label set. Nevertheless, by taking advantage of the simple
structure of FO formulas on forests of constant depth, it is possible to bound the number of labels
by a constant. This constant depends solely on the size of the formula, and on the depth of the
trees.
    As the locality property focuses on distances between nodes, the diameter of each tree plays
an important role. Therefore, instead of considering forests with depth at most d for some d, we
consider forests in which each tree has diameter at most d − 1 for some d. For every d ∈ N, let
us denote by Fd the set of forests in which each tree has diameter at most d − 1. Note that, in
particular, all forests of Fd have depth at most d. For a set of labels Λ, let us denote by Fd [Λ] the
set of all Λ-labeled graphs (G, ℓ) such that G ∈ Fd .

3.1.1    First-Order Formulas on Forests of Bounded Depth
Let Λ be a (finite) set of labels, and let d be a positive integer. It is convenient to consider a
specific way of encoding formulas, suitable for Fd [Λ]. For each i ∈ {−1, 0, 1, . . . , d − 1}, we define
the predicate lcai (x, y) (for least common ancestor) as the predicate with free variables x and y
that holds if the path Px from x to the root of the tree containing x, and the path Py from y to
the root of the tree containing y share exactly i + 1 nodes. Moreover, we set lca−1 (x, y) to be true
whenever x and y are in different trees. Note that lcai (x, x) holds if and only if x is a node at
depth i in its tree. In particular, lca0 (x, x) holds if and only if x is a root. The predicates lcai are
called lca-predicates. As opposed to the label predicates, for which it may be the case that labλ (x)
and labλ′ (x) both hold for two different labels λ ̸= λ′ , we have that, for every x and y, lcai (x, y)

3.2   Quantifier Elimination on Graphs of Bounded Treedepth
Given a graph G = (V, E), a decomposition forest F of G is a rooted spanning forest of G satisfying
that for every edge e ∈ E, one of its endpoints is an ancestor of the other in F . Observe that if G
is connected then any decomposition forest of G is actually a tree, and every DFS tree of G is a
decomposition tree of G. For every t > 0, any induced subgraph H of G with treedepth at most t
is called a treedepth-t induced subgraph.
    We stress the fact that a decomposition forest F of G must be a subgraph of G, which is very
convenient for distributed algorithms because this enables communication along the edges of F .
Note that there exist graphs of treedepth t in which any decomposition forest is of diameter Ω(2t ).
This is for example the case of paths on 2t − 1 vertices. Nevertheless, graphs of treedepth at most t
have paths of at most 2t − 1 vertices. In particular, for any class of graphs of bounded treedepth, all
possible decomposition forests also have trees of constant (although exponentially larger) diameter.

Proposition 3.10 ([54]). Any decomposition forest of a graph of treedepth at most t belongs to F2t .

3.3     Graphs of Bounded Expansion
Let us fix a graph class G of expansion f . Given a set of labels Λ, let G[Λ] be the set of Λ-labeled
graphs (G, ℓ) such that G ∈ G. Let (G, ℓ) ∈ G[Λ] be the input instance. For every integer p, let

3.3.1    Skeletons
We introduce a central notion for our proof of Theorem 1.1.

Definition 3.13. Let p ∈ N, and let G be a graph of expansion f . A p-skeleton of G is a triple
S = (G, c, δ) where c is (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring of G, and δ is a function mapping every U ∈
Col(p) to a decomposition forest F U of the subgraph G[U ] induced by nodes with colors in U . Labeled
p-skeletons are pairs (S, ℓ), where S = (G, c, δ) is a p-skeleton, and ℓ is a labeling of G.

    Note that, by definition of a (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring c, the graph G[U ] is of treedepth at
most p for every U ∈ Col(p). Hence, by Proposition 3.10, we have F U ∈ F2p . The following lemma
states that a p-skeleton can be computed in O(log n) rounds in the CONGEST model, where the
big-O notation hides constants depending only of p, and of the expansion of the class.

Lemma 3.14. For every p ∈ N, there exists an algorithm that constructs a p-skeleton of G in
O(log n) rounds in CONGEST. Specifically, at the end of the algorithm, each node knows its own
color, and, for every set U ∈ Col(p) containing its color, its parent and depth in the forest F U
corresponding to the skeleton.

Remark. In the remaining of the paper, when an algorithm takes a skeleton as input, or outputs
a skeleton, it is assumed that the skeleton is represented in a distributed manner, in the way the
algorithm in Lemma 3.14 computes it.

Proof. The algorithm of Proposition 2.9 enables to compute a (p, f (p))-treedepth coloring c of G,

4    Distributed Model Checking of General Formulas
In this section, we establish Theorem 1.2. We thus consider an arbitrary FO formulas — not
necessarily local. Actually, we show a result stronger than Theorem 1.2. Given a formula φ(x)
with one free variable, we provide a CONGEST algorithm that marks all nodes of n-node graphs
with bounded expansion that satisfy the formula, in O(D + log n) rounds, where D is the diameter



                                                       40
of the input graph3 . To prove the result, we need to revisit the proof of Theorem 1.1, which involves
quantifier elimination of existential formulas first over forests of bounded-depth, then over graphs
of bounded tree-depth, and finally over graphs with bounded expansion. Fortunately, once we
have resolved quantifier elimination over forests bounded-depth, the extensions to the other two
cases will follow from the arguments already developed in the previous section for local formulas.
Therefore, it will be sufficient to revisit quantifier elimination for existential formulas on forests of
bounded-depth, and then proceed by induction to cover the general case.

4.1       Rooted Forests of Bounded Depth
As established in the proof of Lemma 3.7, an existential formula over bounded-depth forests cor-

is to be eliminated. Depending on the position of this existentially quantified variable y, we distin-

needed to handle the connected cases. However, for general formulas, we must also address the
remaining case. This is for this “disconnected case” that our algorithm requires O(D) additional
rounds.

6     Distributed Certification
In this section we prove that every graph property expressible in FO can be certified with certificates
on O(log n) bits in graphs of bounded expansion. For establishing Theorem 1.5, we revisit the logical
structures introduced and analyzed in the previous section, by mainly focusing on the points where
theses structures must be certified instead of being computed.

6.1    Certifying Reduction of Trees with Bounded Depth
We begin with a basic primitive, that we use to count values, to identify connected components,
and for several other applications.
Proposition 6.1 ([48]). Let us suppose that each node u in a graph G = (V, E) is given as an
input a triple (root(u), parent(u), depth(u)), where root(u) and parent(u) are node identifiers, and
depth(u) is a positive integer. Let us consider the algorithm where each node u checks:
    • For each v ∈ N (u), root(v) = root(u) = ρ.

    • If id(u) = ρ then parent(u) = id(u) and depth(u) = 0.

    • If id(u) ̸= ρ, then there exists v ∈ N (u) s.t. id(v) = parent(u) and depth(v) = depth(u) − 1.

                                                        55
If all checks are passed at every node, then there exists a spanning tree T of G rooted at ρ such that
the function parent defines the parent relation in T , and depth(u) is the depth of u in T .

   In the above proposition, if all checks are passed at every node, then the set

                               {(root(u), parent(u), depth(u)) | u ∈ V }

is called the encoding of the tree T . Given a graph G and a forest F ⊆ G with components
T1 , . . . , Tk , we say that the nodes of G are given the encoding of F if, for every i ∈ [k], each node
u ∈ Ti is given the encoding of Ti .

Lemma 6.2. Let A be an algorithm such that, in every n-node network G = (V, E), every node u ∈
V is given an O(log n)-bit input In(u), and must produce an O(log n)-bit output Out(u). Let R(n)
be the round-complexity of A in the BROADCAST CONGEST model. Then, there is a certification
scheme using certificates on O(R(n) log n) bits that certifies that a pair of functions f : V → {0, 1}∗
and g : V → {0, 1}∗ form an input-output pair for A (i.e., if, for every node u ∈ V , In(u) = f (u),
then, for every node u ∈ V , Out(u) = g(u)).

Proof. On legal instances (f, g) for network G = (V, E), the prover provides each vertex u ∈ V
with the sequence M (u) = (m1 (u), . . . , mR(n) (u)) where mi (u) is the message that u broadcasts to
its neighbors on input f (u) during the i-th round of A.
    The verification algorithm proceeds as follows. Every node collects the certificates of its neigh-
bors, and simulates the R(n) rounds of A. That is, u checks first whether m1 (u) is indeed the
message that it would send in the first round of A. For every i ∈ {2, . . . , R(n)}, node u assumes
that the messages m1 (v), . . . , mi−1 (v) of each of its neighbors v ∈ N (u) were correct, and checks
whether mi (u) is indeed the message that it would broadcast at round i. If some of these tests are
not passed then u rejects. If all tests are passed, then u finally checks whether g(u) is the output
that u would produce by executing A, and accepts or rejects accordingly.
    The completeness and soundness of the algorithm follow by construction.

7     Lower Bounds and Impossibility Results
In this section we give evidence that our results somehow represent the limit of tractability of dis-
tributed model checking and distributed certification of properties on graphs of bounded expansion.
    All our reductions are based on classical results in communication complexity. Let f : X × Y →
{0, 1} be a function. The deterministic communication complexity of f D(f ) corresponds to the
minimum number of bits that must be exchanged in any deterministic protocol between a player
Alice holding an input x ∈ X and another player Bob y ∈ Y in order to output the value f (x, y).
The non-deterministic communication complexity of f , N (f ) is defined similarly, but for non-
deterministic communication protocols. We refer to the book of Kushilevitz and Nissan for more
detailed definitions [51].
    In important problem in communication complexity is set disjointedness. It is defined by func-
tion Disjn . For each n the inputs of Disjn are subsets of [n]. Given A, B ⊆ [n] we have that
Disj(A, B) = 1 if and only if A ∩ B = ∅.

Proposition 7.1 ([51]). D(Disjn ) = Ω(n) and N (Disjn ) = Ω(n).

8    Conclusion
In centralized model checking, the fixed-parameter tractability of FO was extended from graphs of
bounded expansion to the more general class of nowhere dense graphs in [39]. Notably, nowhere
dense graphs represent the ultimate frontier in the program of designing fixed-parameter tractable
algorithms for model checking FO formulas. Indeed, as shown in [18], for any graph class G that
is not nowhere dense (i.e., G is somewhere dense) and closed under taking subgraphs, FO model
checking on G is not fixed-parameter tractable (unless FPT = W[1]). The tractability frontier
for FO model checking in the CONGEST model remains unknown. Moreover, there is no reason
to expect that the tractability landscape in the distributed setting should mirror the one of the
centralized setting. This raises several concrete and intriguing questions, discussed hereafter.
    Is it possible to design model-checking algorithms for FO formulas running in Õ(D) rounds in
CONGEST on nowhere dense graphs of diameter D? From a theoretical perspective, a negative
answer to this question would be even more insightful than a positive one. More broadly, what is
the precise boundary for Õ(D)-round FO model checking in the CONGEST model? Does it coincide
with the class of bounded expansion graphs, extend to nowhere dense graphs, or even include a
larger family of graphs? There are several difficulties in extending our approach to nowhere dense
graphs. First, it is unclear how to implement the combinatorial arguments from [39], particularly
the constructions of sparse neighborhood covers, in a distributed model. Second, the proof of [39]
builds on a weak variant of Gaifman’s local form, and not a quantifier elimination procedure. While
this is sufficient for fixed-parameter tractability of FO model-checking, we do not know whether it
could be adapted in the distributed setting.
    Another question is whether a meta-theorem with Õ(D) round complexity can be obtained
for some extensions of FO logic on planar graphs. Theorem 7.2 shows that we cannot hope to
extend Theorem 1.2 to MSO, even on planar graphs. On the other hand, in centralized computing,
the fixed-parameter tractability of logics lying strictly between FO and MSO is an active area of
research. For example, the extension of FO known as separator logic, denoted FOL+conn, was intro-
duced independently by Schirrmacher, Siebertz, and Vigny [60], and by Bojańczyk and Pilipczuk [3].
This logic extends FO, for every k ≥ 1, with the general predicate connk (x, y, z1 , . . . , zk ), which
evaluates to true on a graph G if the nodes corresponding to x and y are connected by a path that
avoids the nodes corresponding to {z1 , . . . , zk }. Another example is disjoint paths logic, denoted
FOL+DP, which extends FO with the atomic predicate dpk (x1 , y1 , . . . , xk , yk ), expressing the exis-
tence of k pairwise internally vertex-disjoint paths between xi and yi for i ∈ {1, . . . , k}. The main


                                                   67
obstacle to developing distributed meta-theorems for these logics lies in the absence of distributed
counterparts of the core algorithmic tools used in the centralized setting—such as tree decomposi-
tions which underlies tractability results for FOL+conn [58], or the irrelevant vertex technique used
for FOL+DP [35].
