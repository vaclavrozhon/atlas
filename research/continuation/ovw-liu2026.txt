                                                                              Abstract
                                                   In this article, we study the reverse mathematics of variable word
                                               theorems used in the proof of the Dual Ramsey theorem. We prove that
                                               the Ordered Variable Word theorem does not imply Ramsey’s theorem for
                                               pairs and that every computable instance admits a solution of low2 degree.
                                               This is used to prove the Carlson-Simpson Lemma and the Open Dual
                                               Ramsey theorem over ACA0 , thereby answering some 40-years old open
                                               questions. These results have consequences in the reverse mathematics of
                                               structural Ramsey theory.


                                          1    Introduction
                                          Celebrated Ramsey’s theorem [43] (RTn ) states the existence, for every k-
                                          coloring of [N]n , of an infinite set H ⊆ N on which [H]n is monochromatic,
                                          for k, n ≥ 1. Here, we write [X]n for the collection of all n-subsets of X. In
                                          1984, Carlson and Simpson [6] proved a dual version of Ramsey’s theorem, in
                                          which k-colorings of N are colored instead of [N]n . Given α ≤ ω, let (N)α de-
                                          notes the class of partitions of N into exactly α non-empty parts, and given
                                          p ∈ (N)ω , let (p)α denotes the class of coarsenings of p into exactly α many
                                          parts, that is, given q ∈ (p)α and x, y ∈ N, if p(x) = p(y), then q(x) = q(y).
                                          Theorem 1.1 (Dual Ramsey Theorem [6]). For all finite k, ℓ ≥ 1, for every
                                          Borel ℓ-coloring of (N)k , there exists some p ∈ (N)ω such that (p)k is monochro-
                                          matic.
                                              We write DRTk and ODRTk for the Dual Ramsey theorem restricted to Borel
                                          and open colorings of (N)k , respectively. The core combinatorial content of the
                                          Open Dual Ramsey theorem is known as the Carlson-Simpson Lemma and can
                                          be formulated in terms of variable word theorems.
                                              A finite or infinite word over a finite alphabet A is a map w : α → A for
                                          some α ≤ ω. We write |w| for its length α. Given β ≤ ω, a β-variable word
                                          over A is a word w over the alphabet A ⊔ {xi : i < β} where each xi appears at
                                          least once, and the first occurrence of xi appears before the first occurrence of
                                          xi+1 . We write Aα,β and A<α,β for the sets of all β-variable word of length α,

and a γ-variable word u of length |u| ≤ β, we write w[u] for the γ-variable word
over A where each occurrence of xj is replaced with u(j), and cut before the
first occurrence of x|u| . If |u| = β, then |w[u]| = |w|.
Lemma 1.2 (Carlson-Simpson Lemma [6]). Fix a finite alphabet A and some
n, ℓ ∈ N. For every ℓ-coloring of A<ω,n , there exists an ω-variable word W
over A such that {W [u] : u ∈ A<ω,n } is monochromatic.
    We write CSLn for the Carlson-Simpson Lemma restricted to ℓ-colorings
of A<ω,n for any finite alphabet A and any ℓ ≥ 1. Since then, the Carlson-
Simpson Lemma found other applications in proving the existence of finite big
Ramsey degree in structural Ramsey theory [29], such as reproving a result by
Dobrinen [11] for the triangle-free Henson graph. The Carlson-Simpson Lemma
admits an inductive proof along n which is similar to the proof of Ramsey’s
theorem. For the base case, Carlson and Simpson [6, Theorem 6.3] proved a
stronger statement in terms of ordered variable words. Given β ≤ ω, an ordered
β-variable word over A is a word w over the alphabet A⊔{xi : i < β} where each
xi appears at least once, and the last occurrence of xi appears before the first
occurrence of xi+1 . Note that contrary to the unordered version, each variable
kind appears only finitely often in an ordered ω-variable word.
Theorem 1.3 (Ordered Variable Word theorem [6]). Fix a finite alphabet A and
some ℓ ∈ N. For every ℓ-coloring of A<ω,0 , there exists an ordered ω-variable
word W over A such that {W [u] : u ∈ A<ω,0 } is monochromatic.
    We write OVW0 for the Ordered Variable Word theorem restricted to ℓ-
colorings of A<ω,0 for any finite alphabet A and any ℓ ≥ 1. There exists actually
an ordered counterpart of the Carlson-Simpson Lemma proven by Carlson [5]
but for which no elementary proof is known.
    We are interested in the meta-mathematics of the Dual Ramsey theorem
and its core lemmas from the viewpoint of computability theory and reverse
mathematics.

1.1    Reverse mathematics
Reverse mathematics is a foundational program started by Harvey Friedman,
whose goal is to find optimal axioms to prove ordinary theorems. It uses the
framework of subsystems of second-order arithmetic, with a base theory, RCA0 ,
capturing computable mathematics. More precisely, RCA0 consists of the axioms
of a discrete ordered commutative semiring, together with the Σ01 -induction
scheme and the ∆01 -comprehension scheme. See Simpson [50] or Dzhafarov and
Mummert [18] for a reference in reverse mathematics. Overall this article, we
shall be interested in the following subsystems, which are listed in increasing
axiomatic strength:
   • WKL0 , standing for Weak König’s lemma, is RCA0 augmented with the
     statement “Every infinite binary tree admits an infinite path”. It can be
     thought of as capturing compactness arguments.

We shall mostly study Π12 -problems, which are Π12 -sentences of the form
                         P = ∀X[Φ(X) → ∃Y Ψ(X, Y )]
where Φ and Ψ are arithmetic formulas. A P-instance is any set X such that
Φ(X) holds, and a P-solution to X is a set Y such that Ψ(X, Y ) holds. For
example, an OVW0 -instance is a coloring f : A<ω,0 → ℓ for a finite alphabet A
and some ℓ ≥ 1. An OVW0 -solution to f is an ordered ω-variable word W such
that {W [u] : u ∈ A<ω,0 } is monochromatic.
    In their article, Carlson and Simpson [6] asked about the proof-theoretic
and computability-theoretic strength of the Dual Ramsey theorem and its com-
binatorial lemmas. In an unpublished paper, Slaman [51] noticed that DRTkℓ
holds over Π11 −CA0 . Miller and Solomon [37] proved that ODRTk+1 implies RTk
over RCA0 , hence that ODRT4 implies ACA0 over RCA0 . They also proved that
OVW0 is not provable over WKL0 , even for binary alphabets and 2-colorings.
Erhard [20] proved that OVW0 is not provable by Ramsey’s theorem for pairs re-
stricted to stable colorings over RCA0 . Dzhafarov, Flood, Solomon and Westrick
[16] studied extensively the Dual Ramsey theorem and its variable word lemmas.
They showed in particular that the full statement of the Dual Ramsey theorem
is sensitive to representation, in terms of Borel or Baire codes. Liu, Monin
and Patey [35] proved OVW0 over ACA0 for binary alphabets and constructed a
computable 2-coloring of {0, 1}<ω,0 with no ∆02 OVW0 -solution. More recently,
Anglès d’Auriac, Mignoty, Liu and Patey [1] proved two versions of OVW0 :
a piecewise syndetic and a density version, the latter being based on Dodos,
Kanellopoulos and Tyros [14]. They deduced that the Carlson-Simpson Lemma
holds over ACA+  0.

1.2    Main contributions
In this article, we give a new proof of OVW0 in the style of Towsner [53], and
derive from its combinatorics a new parametric notion of effective forcing for
building solutions to OVW0 with a good first-jump control. Thanks to this
notion of forcing, we prove the following theorem:
Main Theorem 1.4. OVW0 implies neither RT22 nor ACA0 over RCA0 .
    Moreover, such a separation is witnessed by an ω-model, that is, a structure
M = (M, S, 0, 1, +, · · · , <) in which the first-order part consists of the standard
integers ω, equipped with the natural operations and order. This answers par-
tially [38, Question 11] and [37, Problem 2] and answers negatively [37, Problem
1] and [35, Question 4.9].
    Second, we prove that every computable instance of OVW0 admits a solution
of low2 degree by constructing a ∅′ -computable 1-generic filter for this notion
of forcing. Formalizing the construction over ACA0 , we use this as the base
case of an inductive proof CSLn over ACA0 . We therefore obtain an exact
characterization of the strength of the Carlson-Simpson Lemma and the Open
Dual Ramsey Theorem.
Main Theorem 1.5. For every n ∈ ω, ACA0 ⊢ CSLn and ACA0 ⊢ ODRTn+2 .
Moreover, a reversal holds for every n ≥ 2.

    This therefore gives a partial answer to the original question of Carlson and
Simpson [6] and a complete answer to [37, Problem 3]. Based on the work of
Hubička [29], formalized in reverse mathematics by Anglès d’Auriac et al. [1],
Main Theorem 1.5 shows over ACA0 that the triangle-free Henson graph has
finite big Ramsey degree. Note that this fact was recently proven over ACA0 by
Cholak, Dobrinen, and Towsner [8] for every Henson graph, using a Milliken-
style proof for coding trees, yielding exact bounds for finite big Ramsey degree
contrary to the approach of Hubička [29].
    Last, we complete the picture by showing that CSL1 and ODRT3 are logically
weak, with the following theorem:
Main Theorem 1.6. Neither CSL1 , nor ODRT3 implies ACA0 over RCA0 .

    Main Theorem 1.6 is by far the most complicated part of the article, in-
volving three non-trivial steps: we first prove that an infinite variant of the
Hales-Jewett theorem admits so-called strong preservation of hyperimmunity,
then use it to prove that the Ordered Variable Word theorem admits the same
property, and last, we prove that a cohesive version of CSL1 admits preservation
of hyperimmunity.
    Main Theorem 1.6 shows that the statement of the indivisibility of the
triangle-free Henson graph does not imply ACA0 , answering a question of Gill [22]
and Cholak, Dobrinen and McCoy [7, Question 48].

2.2    Hales-Jewett theorem
The following theorem is known as the Hales-Jewett theorem [24]. The original
proof used double induction and yielded a bound HJ growing as fast as the
Ackermann function. Shelah [48] gave a new proof yielding primitive recursive
bounds:


                                         6
Theorem 2.1 (Hales-Jewett, RCA0 ). There exists a primitive recursive func-
tion HJ(k, ℓ) such that for every n ≥ HJ(k, ℓ), every alphabet A of size k and
every coloring f : An → ℓ, there exists some 1-variable word v over A of length n
such that WSub= (v) = {v[a] : a ∈ A} is f -homogeneous.
   For simplicity, given a finite alphabet A of size k and some ℓ ≥ 1, we might
simply write HJ(A, ℓ) for HJ(card A, ℓ).

3     Weakly low basis theorem for OVW
The levels of the arithmetic hierarchy are not closed under relativization, in the
sense that for n ≥ 2, the relation“X is ∆0n (Y )” is not transitive. It is therefore
useful to work with notions of lowness. A set X is of lown degree over Y if
(X ⊕ Y )(n) ≤T Y (n) . In particular, the relation “X is of lown degree over Y ” is
transitive. When n = 1, we simply say low for low1 .
Definition 3.1. A Π12 -problem P admits a lown basis if for every set Z and every
Z-computable P-instance X, there is a P-solution Y to X such that (Y ⊕Z)(n) ≤
Z (n) .


                                           11
   For instance, Jockusch and Soare [32] proved that WKL admits a low basis.
Some Π12 -problems are “close” to admit a low basis, in that any PA degree
over ∅′ can decide the jump of a solution. We then say that it admits a weakly
low basis.
Definition 3.2. A Π12 -problem P admits a weakly lown basis if for every set Z,

3.2    Effective forcing
It is common in effective forcing to work with a restricted set of P-conditions with
computability-theoretic constraints. In our situation, we shall restrict ourselves
to P-preconditions (I, W ) such that W belongs to a non-empty class of sets C
satisfying the following closure properties:

 (1) C is downward-closed under Turing reducibility: ∀X ∈ C∀Y ≤T X, Y ∈ C;
 (2) C is closed under PA degrees: ∀X ∈ C∃Y ∈ C, Y is of PA degree over X.
Any such class is called a Scott class. If one furthermore requires that C is
closed under the effective join, then we obtain the well-known notion of Scott
ideal. Any basis theorem for Π01 classes induces a Scott class. For instance, by
the computably dominated and the low basis theorems [32], the classes of all
sets of computably dominated degree or of all low degree form Scott classes,
respectively. None of the above examples form Scott ideals.
Definition 3.13. Given a Scott class C, a PC -(pre)condition is a P-(pre)condition
(I, W ) such that W ∈ C.

in the class LOW of all sets of low degree. Any PLOW -precondition can be
represented as a finite object as follows:

Given a notion of forcing, there exists a general forcing relation defined as
follows: p forces a property φ(G) if φ(GF ) holds for every sufficiently generic
filter F containing p, where GF is the set induced by the filter F . Although
this forcing relation also admits a simple inductive and syntactical definition,
the complexity of the forcing relation often remains too high with respect to the
complexity of the forced formula.
     For our purpose, it will be sufficient to force Σ01 and Π01 formulas. At this level
of the arithmetic hierarchy, there is often a stronger forcing relation which does
not only hold for any sufficiently generic filter, but for every filter containing p.
We now give a formal definition of a forcing relation for Σ01 and Π01 -formulas.
     By Kleene’s normal form theorem, every Σ01 -formula φ(G) can be written of
the form ∃xψ(G ↾x ), where ψ(σ) is a monotonous ∆00 -formula, that is, if ψ(σ)
holds and σ ⪯ τ , then ψ(τ ) also holds. We will always assume that a Σ01 -formula
is in normal form.
Definition 3.15 (Forcing relation). Let (v, W ) be a P-branch and φ(G) :=

4    Preservation of hyperimmunities
The halting set being Σ01 -definable, every ω-model of ACA0 contains ∅′ . It follows
that to prove that a Π12 -problem P does not imply ACA0 over RCA0 , it suffices
to show the existence of an ω-model of RCA0 + P which does not contain ∅′ .
Actually, most statements P such that RCA0 + P ⊬ ACA0 are shown to satisfy a
stronger property, called cone avoidance.
Definition 4.1. A Π12 -problem P admits cone avoidance if for every set Z, every
set C ̸≤T Z and every Z-computable P-instance X, there is a P-solution Y such
that C ̸≤T Y ⊕ Z.
    If a Π12 -problem P admits cone avoidance, then for every non-computable
set C, there is an ω-model of RCA0 +P which does not contain C. This technique
was used, for instance, by Seetapun and Slaman [47] to prove that Ramsey’s
theorem for pairs is strictly weaker than ACA0 .
    Later, the author [41] introduced the notion of preservation of hyperim-
munities, as a refinement of cone avoidance to prove separations from other
statements such as RT22 , where RTnk is the restriction of Ramsey’s theorem for
k-colorings of the n-tuples. A function h : N → N is Z-hyperimmune if it is
not dominated by any Z-computable function, where g dominates h means that
∀xg(x) ≥ h(x).
Definition 4.2. Let k ≤ ω. A Π12 -problem P admits preservation of k hyper-
immunities if for every set Z, every sequence ⟨hi : i < k⟩ of Z-hyperimmune
functions and every Z-computable P-instance X, there is a P-solution Y such
that for every i < k, hi is Y ⊕ Z-hyperimmune.
    Both notions are related: Downey et al [15] proved that cone avoidance and
preservation of 1 hyperimmunity coincide. On the other hand, for every k, there
is a Π12 -problem which admits preservation of k, but not k + 1 hyperimmunities.
In particular, RT22 admits preservation of 1 hyperimmunity, but the following
classical argument shows that the bound is optimal:
Lemma 4.3 ([31]). RT22 does not admit preservation of 2 hyperimmunities.
Proof. The principal function pX of an infinite set X = {x0 < x1 . . . } is the
map n 7→ xn . By [30, Theorem 5.2], there exists a ∆02 set A such that pA and
pA are both hyperimmune. By Shoenfield’s limit lemma [49, Theorem 2], there
exists a computable coloring f : [N]2 → 2 such that for every x, limy f (x, y)
exists and equals A(x). Seeing f as a computable instance of RT22 , every infinite
f -homogeneous set H is either a subset of A or of A, hence pH dominates either
pA or pA .
    Preservation of k hyperimmunities is a particular case of a general notion
of preservation of a weakness property. One can show that if a Π12 -problem P
preserves some weakness property, but another Π12 -problem Q does not, then
RCA0 + P ⊬ Q, as witnessed by an ω-model (see [40, Section 3.4]). We reprove
the separation in the case of preservation of hyperimmunities for the sake of
containment:

5     Strong weakness of ∃∞ -IHJ
Preservation of 1 hyperimmunity shows the inability, for a Π12 -problem P, to
encode in its computable instances sufficiently fast-growing functions that dom-
inate some fixed hyperimmune function. There are some Π12 -problems that are
so weak that even arbitrary instances have this inability. This yields the notion
of strong preservation of 1 hyperimmunity.
Definition 5.1. Let k ≤ ω. A Π12 -problem P admits strong preservation of k hy-
perimmunities if for every set Z, every sequence ⟨hi : i < k⟩ of Z-hyperimmune
functions and every arbitrary P-instance X, there is a P-solution Y such that
for every i < k, hi is Y ⊕ Z-hyperimmune.



                                          25
    The equivalence from Downey et al [15] between preservation of 1 hyper-
immunity and cone avoidance extends to their strong counterparts. The most
famous example of Π12 -problem admitting strong cone avoidance is Ramsey’s
theorem for singletons (RT1 ), also known as the infinite pigeonhole principle
(see Dzhafarov and Jockusch [17]). For many hierarchies of partition theorems
(Pn )n∈N , there exists a deep link between strong preservation of properties for
Pn and preservation of properties for Pn+1 . This will be exemplified in Section 8
to show that CSL1 admits preservation of 1 hyperimmunity, and therefore does
not imply ACA0 over RCA0 .

that an infinite version of the Hales-Jewett theorem admits strong preservation
of ω hyperimmunities. Then, in Section 6, this fact will be used to prove that
OVW0 admits strong preservation of 1 hyperimmunity.

6    Strong weakness of OVW0
The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 6.1. OVW0 admits strong preservation of 1 hyperimmunity.
   Actually, given a coloring f : A<ω → ℓ, we shall construct an ordered
ω-variable word G with slightly weaker properties: each level of G will be f -
monochromatic, but the color of monochromaticity will depend on the level:
Definition 6.2. An ordered variable word v is level-homogeneous for a coloring
f : A<ω → ℓ iff for every u0 , u1 ∈ WSub⋆ (v) such that |u0 | = |u1 |, we have
f (u0 ) = f (u1 ).
   This weaker notion of monochromaticity yields the following statement, that
we shall refer to as Level Ordered Variable Word theorem:

Definition 6.3. Let LOVW0 be the Π12 -problem whose instances are colorings
f : A<ω → ℓ for a finite alphabet A and some number of colors ℓ ≥ 1. A LOVW0 -
solution to f is an ordered ω-variable word W which is level-homogeneous for f .
    LOVW0 can be thought of as OVW0 without one last application of the
infinite pigeonhole principle (RT1 ). Because of this, it satisfies a stronger notion
of preservation:
Theorem 6.4. LOVW0 admits strong preservation of k hyperimmunities for
every k ∈ N.
    Note that OVW0 does not admit strong preservation of 2 hyperimmunities.
Indeed, RT1 is strongly computably reducible to OVW0 , and it is known that
RT1 even for 2-colorings does not admit strong preservation of 2 hyperimmuni-
ties (by [30, Theorem 5.2], there exists a coloring f : N → 2 such that f −1 (0)
and f −1 (1) are both hyperimmune). Before proving Theorem 6.4, let us assume
it holds, and deduce Theorem 6.1.
Proof of Theorem 6.1. As usual, we prove it in its unrelativized form. Let f :
A<ω → ℓ be an arbitrary coloring for some finite alphabet A and ℓ ≥ 1. Let
h : N → N be a hyperimmune function. By Theorem 6.4, there is an ordered

ω-variable word W which is level-homogeneous for f , and such that h is W -
hyperimmune. Let X = {t0 < t1 < . . . } be such that tn is the position of
the first occurrence of xn in W . Let g : N → ℓ be such that g(n) is the color

    Through this section, fix a coloring f : A<ω → ℓ and a family ⃗h = (hi )i<k

following notion of forcing:
Definition 6.5. A Q-condition is a pair (v, W ) such that

 (1) v is a finite ordered variable word and W an ordered ω-variable word;
 (2) v is level-homogeneous for f ;

 (3) each h ∈ ⃗h is W -hyperimmune.
    In other words, a Q-condition corresponds with a P-branch with some color
constraints on v and some computability-theoretic weakness on W . Note that
this notion of forcing is parameterized by both the coloring f , and the family
of hyperimmune function ⃗h. The denotation [v, W ] of a Q-condition is exactly
the same as for a P-branch. In particular, do not impose any G ∈ [v, W ] to be
level-homogeneous for f . The notion of Q-extension is also the same as P-branch
extension. We state it again for the sake of completeness:
Definition 6.6 (Q-condition extension). A Q-condition (v̂, Ŵ ) extends a Q-
condition (v, W ) (written (v̂, Ŵ ) ≤ (v, W )) iff: v̂ ⪰ v and v̂ · Ŵ ∈ OSubω (v · W ).

6.1    Requirement and progress
For a Turing functional Ψ and some hyperimmune function h ∈ ⃗h, let RhΨ be
the requirement:
      RhΨ : Either ΨG is non-total or ΨG does not dominate h.

We now define what it means for a Q-condition to satisfy a requirement RhΨ .
Instead of asking RhΨ to hold for every G ∈ [v, W ], we ask for a stronger property:
Definition 6.7. A Q-condition (v, W ) satisfies RhΨ iff:
   • either for every G ∈ [v, W ], ΨG is non total

   • or Ψv (n) ↓< h(n) for some n.




                                           30
    This notion of satisfaction is more uniform, in the sense that every G ∈ [v, W ]
satisfies RhΨ by the same disjunct. This stronger definition will be used in the
proof of Theorem 6.1. In general, even for some fixed h ∈ ⃗h and Ψ, the set of
Q-conditions satisfying an RhΨ -requirement is not dense.
Definition 6.8 (Non-progressable). A Q-condition (v, W ) is non-progressable
for h iff for some Turing functional Ψ, there is no extension (v̂, Ŵ ) of (v, W )
such that (v̂, Ŵ ) satisfies RhΨ . Otherwise, we say (v, W ) is progressable for h
(i.e., it can be extended to satisfy RhΨ for every Ψ).
   The whole difficulty of the proof will be to show the existence of a Q-
condition (ϵ, W ∗ ) below which every Q-extension is progressable for each h ∈ ⃗h.
In other words, the set of Q-conditions satisfying any RhΨ -requirement is dense
below (ϵ, W ∗ ).

Besides the proof of the Dual Ramsey theorem, the main application of infinite
variable word theorems comes from Structural Ramsey Theory, which studies
partitiontheorems of mathematical structures. Given two structures F, G, we
write G                                                               ∼

is isomorphic to G. Given integers k, ℓ ∈ ω, structures A, B and C we write
C −→ (A)B  k,ℓ for the following statement:

                               (N, <) → (N, <)n

for every n ∈ N, where n is the ordered set ({0, . . . , n − 1}, <).
Definition 9.2. An infinite structure A has the Ramsey property if for every
finite substructure B, the relation A → (A)B
                                           <ω,1 holds.

    All the infinite structures A that we shall consider in this section  have a
unique sub-structure 1 of size 1, in which case any coloring of A
                                                                   

to a coloring of the elements of A. The Ramsey property of A restricted to 1
is called indivisibility of A.
Definition 9.3. An infinite structure A is indivisible if A → (A)1<ω,1 holds,
where 1 is its unique sub-structure of size 1.

   Many infinite structures do not admit the Ramsey property, but still enjoy
a weaker partition theorem:
Definition 9.4. Let A be an infinite structure. A finite sub-structure B has big
Ramsey degree ℓ ∈ N if ℓ is the least number such that the relation A → (A)B
                                                                           <ω,ℓ
holds, if it exists. We say that A has finite big Ramsey degree if every finite
sub-structure admits a big Ramsey degree.
   Structural Ramsey theory gives a particular focus on Fraissé limits, such as
the dense linear order with no endpoints (Q, <), the Rado (or random) graph R,
and in our case, the universal k-clique-free Henson graph Hk . Each of the pre-
vious examples are known to admit a finite big Ramsey degree. Such partition
theorems are usually proven by coding the mathematical structures into trees,
and applying a tree partition theorem. For instance, Devlin [10] and Sauer [45]
used Milliken’s tree partition theorem to prove that (Q, <) and R admit finite
Big Ramsey degree, respectively. The exact big Ramsey degrees for the Rado
graph were then characterized by Laflamme, Sauer and Vuksanovic [34]

9.1    Big Ramsey degree of the triangle-free Henson graph
The big Ramsey degree of the Henson graphs were a longstanding open ques-
tion. In 1986, Komjáth and Rödl [33] proved that H3 is indivisible and in 1989,
El-Zahar and Sauer [19] generalized it to all Henson graphs. In 1998, Sauer [46]
proved that the edges of H3 have big Ramsey degree 2. In 2017, Dobrinen [11]
proved that H3 admits finite big Ramsey degree using the method of strong
coding trees. She then generalized it to all Henson graphs [13] with an elabora-
tion of the technique, and characterized [12, 3] the exact big Ramsey degrees of
H3 . Hubička [29] gave an arguably simpler proof of the finite big Ramsey de-
gree of H3 using the Carlson-Simpson lemma. However, his proof is less precise
than the coding tree method, in that it only yields upper bounds to the Ram-
sey degrees. More recently, in a collective work, Balko, Chodounský, Dobrinen,
Hubička, Konečný, Vena and Zucker [2] characterized the big Ramsey degrees
of free amalgamation classes in finite binary languages defined by finitely many
forbidden irreducible substructures.
    From a computability-theoretic and reverse-mathematical viewpoint, Anglès
d’Auriac et al. [1] formalized the proof of Hubička [29] and noticed that for
                              n
every n ∈ N, RCA0 + CSL2 +n−1 (1) proves that every sub-structure of H3 of
size n admits finite big Ramsey degree, and deduced that over ACA+          0 , H3 has
finite big Ramsey degree for H3 . Gill [22] proved that the indivisibility of Hn is
not computably true for n ≥ 3. More recently, Cholak, Dobrinen and McCoy [7]
proved that the indivisibility of H3 holds over ACA0 and that the existence of big
Ramsey degrees for sub-structures of H3 of size 2 implies ACA0 . Finally, Cholak,
Dobrinen, and Towsner [8] proved that for every standard n, m, the existence
of big Ramsey degrees for sub-structures of Hm of size n holds over ACA0 using
a Milliken-style proof for coding trees. Note that their proof over ACA0 yields
exact bounds for finite big Ramsey degree.
    We now combine the new bounds on the Carlson-Simpson lemma with the
analysis of Anglès d’Auriac et al. [1] of the proof of Hubička [29] to obtain similar
bounds as Cholak, Dobrinen, and Towsner.
