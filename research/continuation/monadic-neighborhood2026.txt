                                         1        Introduction
                                         In the first-order model checking problem, the input is a graph (or other structure) and a sentence φ of
                                         first-order logic, and the task is to decide whether φ is true in G. This fundamental problem captures
                                         many concrete problems of interest, including k-Clique, k-Dominating Set, and k-Independent Set,
                                         and has been the focus of decades of research aimed at understanding which structural restrictions
                                         on the input graph render the problem tractable.
                                              A landmark result of Grohe, Kreutzer, and Siebertz [22] established that first-order model
                                         checking is fixed-parameter tractable on every nowhere dense graph class; the running time of the
                                         algorithm is of the form f (|φ|) · n1+o(1) , where f is a function depending on the class and the o(1)
                                         term also depends on the class. For monotone (subgraph-closed) graph classes, nowhere denseness is
                                         precisely the dividing line between (fixed-parameter) tractability and intractability [19, 22]. However,
                                         there are natural tractable classes — such as classes of bounded clique-width [11] or twin-width [9] —
                                         that are not monotone, contain dense graphs, and hence lie beyond the scope of this classification.

    The search for the exact tractability boundary among all hereditary (induced-subgraph-closed)
graph classes has converged on a notion from Shelah’s classification theory: monadic dependence [4].
A graph class C is monadically dependent if one cannot interpret all graphs in vertex-colored
graphs from C using a fixed first-order formula. Monadic dependence precisely captures the known
tractability boundaries in all settings where a complete classification exists: it is equivalent to
nowhere denseness for monotone classes [2], to monadic stability for orderless classes [27], and to
bounded twin-width for classes of ordered graphs [8]. This has led to the following conjecture, which
is now the central open problem in the area.

Conjecture 1 (e.g., [1, 8, 14, 21]). Let C be a hereditary class of graphs. Then the first-order model
checking problem is fixed-parameter tractable on C if and only if C is monadically dependent.

    The hardness direction of Conjecture 1 was recently confirmed: first-order model checking
is AW[∗]-hard on every hereditary graph class that is not monadically dependent [15]. (Here,
AW[∗] is a parameterized complexity class that can be understood as the parameterized analogue
of PSPACE. In particular, it is strongly believed that AW[∗]̸=FPT.) Tractability has been established
for increasingly general subclasses: nowhere dense classes [22], structurally nowhere dense classes [14],
and monadically stable classes [12]. Yet the full tractability direction for all monadically dependent
classes remains open.
    Very recently, Dreier and Toruńczyk [16] suggested a possible line of attack towards Conjecture 1.
Inspired by twin-width [9] and flip-width [33], they introduced a family of graph parameters called
merge-width, indexed by a radius parameter r ∈ N. They conjectured that every monadically
dependent class has almost bounded merge-width (i.e., for every fixed r, the radius-r merge-width of
n-vertex graphs in the class is no(1) ), and showed that first-order model checking is fixed-parameter
tractable on graphs of bounded merge-width, provided a suitable decomposition called a construction
sequence is supplied on input. A possible route towards Conjecture 1 is then as follows:
   • For a sufficiently high radius r depending on the given sentence φ, compute a construction
     sequence of the input graph G whose radius-r merge-width is |V (G)|o(1) .
   • Then, use the obtained construction sequence to solve the model checking problem by an
     extension of the method from [16].
In this work, we advance this line of work by the following contributions:
Result I: We prove that monadically dependent graph classes have almost linear neighborhood
    complexity, as defined below.
Result II: We leverage the bound on the neighborhood complexity to prove that monadically
    dependent classes have almost bounded radius-1 merge-width. This provides a first step
    towards the conjecture of Dreier and Toruńczyk. Importantly, our proof yields a polynomial-
    time algorithm that computes a construction sequence of radius-1 width no(1) .
We now describe these results and their relevance in more detail.

Neighborhood complexity is a fundamental quantitative notion used in the study of well-
structured graph classes. For a graph G and vertex set A ⊆ V (G), the neighborhood complexity
of A is the number of distinct sets of the form N (v) ∩ A for v ∈ V (G), where N (v) denotes the
neighborhood of v. We say that a graph class C has almost linear neighborhood complexity if for
every G ∈ C and every A ⊆ V (G),

                                 {N (v) ∩ A : v ∈ V (G)} ⩽ |A|1+o(1) .

We remark that the o(1) term hides a dependence on the graph class C .
    In the language of set systems, this says that the set system of neighborhoods in graphs from
C has an almost linear shatter function, or VC density 1. Neighborhood complexity has played
a central role in the algorithmic theory of sparse and dense graph classes alike. It is known to be
almost linear for nowhere dense classes [20], and more generally, all monadically stable classes [12],
and all classes of almost bounded merge-width [5]. All these classes are monadically dependent.
    Almost linear neighborhood complexity serves as a powerful entry point for establishing a range of
quantitative structural properties. For example, a classic result of Welzl [34] shows that every graph
class with almost linear neighborhood complexity admits vertex orderings with crossing number
no(1) . The key observation made in [12] was that Welzl orderings with crossing number no(1) can
be used to efficiently construct sparse neighborhood covers with overlap no(1) . These covers serve
as a key ingredient in the model checking algorithms for nowhere dense and monadically stable
classes [22, 14, 12].
    Whether monadically dependent classes also have almost linear neighborhood complexity was
posed as an open problem in [12]. Our first main result answers this question affirmatively.

Theorem 2. Let C be a monadically dependent graph class. Then for every G ∈ C and A ⊆ V (G),

                                    {N (v) ∩ A : v ∈ V (G)} ⩽ |A|1+o(1) .

As usual, the o(1) term hides a dependence on the graph class C . Equivalently, we prove that for
every ε > 0 there is some c = c(ε, C ) so that the cardinality above is bounded by c|A|1+ε . This result
is essentially tight as already nowhere dense classes can have superlinear neighborhood complexity1 .
    The proof of the corresponding statement for monadically stable classes in [12] relies on a
reduction to the nowhere dense case using tools from stability theory (Shelah’s 2-rank/branching
index). Our proof of Theorem 2 has a similar global structure, but uses induction on the VC-
dimension instead of the branching index. This introduces technical difficulties which we overcome
using ideas concerning set systems of bounded VC-dimension, originating in the work of Sauer and
Shelah [31, 32], and Haussler, Littlestone, Warmuth [23, 24] in computational learning theory, such
as the unit-distance graph (also called the Hamming graph).
    Combined with known results for graphs of almost linear neighborhood complexity, Theorem 2
has several immediate applications.

Corollary 3. For every monadically dependent graph class C , every n-vertex graph G ∈ C admits:
       • a vertex order (also called a spanning path) with crossing number no(1) [34],
         which can be computed in time (n + m) · no(1) [13],
       • a neighborhood cover with overlap no(1) and strong radius 2 [12],
       • a spanner with stretch 4 and n1+o(1) edges2 ,
       • an no(1) -bit adjacency labeling scheme/implicit representation [3, 6],
       • an algorithm solving All-Pairs Shortest Path in time n2+o(1) ,
         by combining [7, Thm. 6.2] and [17, Lem. 13] (see also [35, Thm. 11]).

    While neighborhood complexity has rich structural and algorithmic consequences, the overarching
goal — fixed-parameter tractable first-order model checking on monadically dependent classes —
demands more. Monadic dependence is defined by forbidding certain patterns to be interpretable in
graphs from the class, a characterization that is inherently non-constructive. On the other hand,
algorithms require decompositions. For nowhere dense classes, this role is played by generalized
coloring numbers, which underlie most algorithmic applications. As discussed above, the working
hypothesis is that the right analogue for monadically dependent classes is provided by merge-width.

Merge-width is a family of graph parameters introduced by Dreier and Toruńczyk [16] that unifies
several central structural measures, including treewidth, degeneracy, twin-width, clique-width, and
generalized coloring numbers. The underlying notion of a decomposition is called a construction
sequence: starting from the partition into singletons, it builds the graph by repeatedly either merging
two parts, or resolving all currently unresolved vertex pairs between two parts as edges or as
non-edges. The radius-r merge-width measures how many parts any vertex can reach via a path
consisting of at most r resolved vertex pairs at any point during the construction.
    Graph classes of bounded merge-width — where the radius-r merge-width is bounded by a constant
for each fixed r ∈ N — include all classes of bounded expansion and all classes of bounded twin-width,
unifying the two frameworks. The model checking problem for first-order logic is fixed-parameter
tractable on graph classes of bounded merge-width, given a witnessing construction sequence [16].
This unifies the model checking results for bounded expansion [19] and bounded twin-width [9].
    Relaxing this condition, a class C has almost bounded merge-width if for each fixed r ∈ N, the
n-vertex graphs in C have radius-r merge-width at most no(1) (where the o(1) term depends on both
the radius r and the class C ). This parallels the characterization of nowhere dense classes via almost
bounded generalized coloring numbers. It is conjectured [16] that almost bounded merge-width
coincides with monadic dependence for hereditary classes. One direction holds: almost bounded
merge-width implies monadic dependence [15]. The other direction is open, however our second
main result settles it for r = 1:

Theorem 4. Let C be a monadically dependent graph class. Then every n-vertex graph G ∈ C has
radius-1 merge-width at most no(1) .

   Our techniques are algorithmic, not just existential, and they apply to all graphs, not just
graphs from monadically dependent classes. Our algorithm returns a construction sequence of small
radius-1 width for any graph with polynomial neighborhood complexity. More precisely, we prove
the following theorem. We note that the value of the constant k in our theorem primarily comes
from the constant in Haussler’s Packing Lemma [24].

Theorem 5. There is an algorithm that, given an n-vertex graph G, computes in time O(n5 ) a
construction sequence for G with the following guarantee: For all real numbers c and d ⩾ 1, there
exists an integer k = k(c, d) so that if G is an n-vertex graph such that for every nonempty A ⊆ V (G),

                                  {N (v) ∩ A : v ∈ V (G)} ⩽ c|A|d ,

then the construction sequence has radius-1 merge-width at most k · n1−1/d log n.

    If G is drawn from a monadically dependent class C , then Theorem 2 guarantees almost linear
neighborhood complexity, and the algorithm computes a construction sequence with radius-1 merge-
width no(1) , implying Theorem 4. Similarly, if G belongs to a class C of bounded merge-width, then
G has linear neighborhood complexity by [5], and so the algorithm returns a construction sequence
of radius-1 merge-width O(log n) (hiding a constant depending on the class). Notably, the algorithm
is oblivious to the values of d and c and its running time is always just O(n5 ). Theorem 5 itself
is obtained by a greedy procedure with an iterative reweighing technique similar to the one used
by Welzl [34].
    We did not optimize the running time O(n5 ) of the algorithm. A construction sequence of radius-1
merge-width O(log n) gives rise to a very particular signed tree model with O(n log n) transversal
pairs. For graphs of bounded twin-width, signed tree models with O(n log n) transversal pairs can
also be constructed in randomized time O(m + n) log n [35, Thm. 4]. We leave for future research
the question of whether construction sequences of width O(log n) can be obtained in a similar time
for all graphs of linear neighborhood complexity.


2    Neighborhood Complexity
In this section we give a high-level overview of the proof of Theorem 2. The complete proof can be
found in Section A. We begin by introducing the relevant notions.

Transductions and monadic dependence A transduction T is specified by a number k and
a first-order formula φ(x, y) in the signature consisting of a binary adjacency symbol and k unary
relation symbols. Given a graph G, define T (G) as the set of graphs H which can be obtained as
follows: first expand G by interpreting the k unary predicates arbitrarily; then construct a graph on
vertex set V (G) and edges uv with u = ̸ v such that φ(u, v) ∨ φ(v, u) holds in the expanded structure;
finally, take an arbitrary induced subgraph H of the resulting graph.
     For example, the two formulas φ1 (x, y) := ¬E(x, y) and φ2 (x, y) := E(x, y)∨∃z (E(x, z)∧E(z, y))
specify (with k = 0) transductions producing S  the complement and the square of a graph, respectively.
     For a graph class C , we define T (C ) := {T (G) | G ∈ C }. If C and D are graph classes, then
C transduces D if D ⊆ T (C ) for some transduction T . A graph class is monadically dependent
if it does not transduce the class of all graphs. (This is the transduction formulation of monadic
dependence; the original definition is model-theoretic, and the equivalence follows from the work of
Baldwin and Shelah [4].) Since transducibility is transitive (by composing the defining first-order
formulas and unary expansions), if C is monadically dependent and C transduces D, then also D is
monadically dependent.
     It is well-known that if C is a monadically dependent graph class that does not contain Kt,t as a
subgraph, for some t, then C is nowhere dense. This follows e.g. by combining the results of [2]
and [18]; see [30, Lemma 35] or [25, Lemma 13.7] for a direct argument. The definition of nowhere
denseness will not be relevant here, only the fact that such classes are known to have almost linear
neighborhood complexity [20]. Combining these results, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 6. Fix t ∈ N and a monadically dependent class C of graphs. Then for every G ∈ C that
does not contain Kt,t as a subgraph and every A ⊆ V (G),

                                {N (v) ∩ A : v ∈ V (G)} ⩽ |A|1+o(1) ,

where the o(1) term depends on both t and C .

Set systems and VC-dimension A set system on a set D is a set F of subsets of D. If F is a
set system on D and X ⊆ D, then by F⇂X we denote the set system {X ∩ F | F ∈ F} on X. The
VC-dimension of F is the maximal size of a set X such that F⇂X = 2X , and is −∞ if F = ∅. For a
graph G, we write VCdim(G) to denote the VC-dimension of the set system {N (v) | v ∈ V (G)} on
V (G). A graph class C has bounded VC-dimension if there exists d ∈ N such that VCdim(G) ⩽ d for
all G ∈ C . It is easy to see that every monadically dependent graph class has bounded VC-dimension.
    Let F be a set system on V , and let v ∈ V . We say that A and B are a v-pair if A, B ∈ F and
A△B = {v}, that is, if v is the unique element on which A and B differ. If A ∈ F is in some v-pair
then A is v-positive if v ∈ A and is v-negative otherwise. The following observation underlies the
inductive proof of the fundamental Sauer-Shelah-Perles lemma (see for instance [26, Lemma 5.9]). We
leverage this lemma in our proof of Theorem 2. (Claims marked with ♣ are proved in the appendix.)

Lemma 7 (♣). For every nonempty set system F on a set V and every v ∈ V , the following two
set systems on V have VC-dimension strictly smaller than the VC-dimension of F :

                    {F ∈ F | F is v-positive}   and   {F ∈ F | F is v-negative}.

    The Hamming graph of a set system F, denoted as Hamming(F), is the graph with vertex set F,
where two sets A, B ∈ F are adjacent if and only if |A△B| = 1. We use a lemma due to Haussler,
Littlestone, and Warmuth [23, Lemma 2.4] (see also [24, Lemma 2]).

Lemma 8. The Hamming graph of a set system F of VC-dimension d has at most d|F| edges.

Corollary 9. Let F be a set system of VC-dimension d, whose Hamming graph H has m edges.
Then H has at least m/d non-isolated vertices.

Proof. Let G ⊆ F = V (H) be the set of non-isolated vertices of H. Then G is a set system of
VC-dimension at most d with m = |E(H)| Hamming edges, so m ⩽ d|G| by Lemma 8.

Bipartite Graphs as Set Systems In the proof of Theorem 2, it will be enough to consider
bipartite graphs G = (A, B, E). Every bipartite graph G = (A, B, E) induces a set system
{N (b) | b ∈ B} on A, and we denote this set system by B/A. Notice that |B/A| = |B| if B does not
contain any pair of twins, that is, two vertices with equal neighborhoods. More generally, for A′ ⊆ A
and B ′ ⊆ B, we view B ′ /A′ as the set system {N (b) ∩ A′ | b ∈ B ′ } on A′ .
    We define two auxiliary bipartite graphs from G = (A, B, E) – the positive merge graph and the
negative merge graph – as follows. Both graphs have parts A and B/A. We include edges {a, F }
with a ∈ A and F ∈ B/A such that F is a-positive or a-negative, respectively, in the set system
B/A on A. For instance, we include {a, F } as an edge of the positive merge graph if there exists a
set F ′ ∈ B/A such that F ∆F ′ = {a} and a ∈ F . In this case F ′ = F − {a}, and we also include
{a, F ′ } in the negative merge graph. The intuition is that removing a from the domain causes F and
F ′ to merge, i.e., F − {a} = F ′ . Note that a set F ∈ B/A is non-isolated in the Hamming graph of
B/A if and only if it is non-isolated in at least one of the merge graphs of B/A. The advantage of
merge graphs over the Hamming graph is that they allow us to describe subsets of B using vertices
of A. Moreover, Lemma 7 yields the following.

Lemma 10. Let G = (A, B, E) be a bipartite graph and let v ∈ A. Then the neighborhood of v
in both the positive merge graph and the negative merge graph is a set system of strictly smaller
VC-dimension than B/A.

3    Radius-1 Merge-Width
This section is dedicated to proving Theorem 5, which shows how to efficiently compute a construction
sequence with small radius-1 merge-width. We begin by defining the relevant terms.

Merge-Width Consider a vertex set V . A construction sequence is a sequence of steps, maintaining
a partition P of V and a partition of V2 into three sets: edges E, non-edges N , and unresolved
pairs U . Initially, P partitions V into singletons, and every pair in V2 is unresolved. In each step,
                                                                         

one of three operations is performed:
    • merge two parts A, B ∈ P, A ̸= B, replacing the two parts by their union A ∪ B,
    • resolve positively a pair of parts A, B ∈ P (possibly A = B), declaring all the unresolved pairs
      {a, b} ∈ U with a ∈ A, b ∈ B as edges, that is, moving them from U to E, or
    • resolve negatively a pair of parts A, B ∈ P (possibly A = B), declaring the corresponding
      unresolved pairs as non-edges, that is, moving them from U to N .
In the end, we require that P has one part, and that every pair from V2 is resolved as either an
                                                                             

edge or a non-edge. We thus say this is a construction sequence of the graph G = (V, E), where E is
the final edge set of the process.
    The radius-r width of a construction sequence is the smallest integer k such that at every step in
the sequence, for every vertex v ∈ V , at most k parts of the current partition P can be reached from
v by a path of length ⩽ r in the graph (V, E ∪ N ). Finally, the radius-r merge-width of a graph G is
the minimum radius-r width over all construction sequences of G.
    Observe that for any construction sequence of a graph G, at any given step, for any two parts
A, B of the partition P, the unresolved vertex pairs ab with a ∈ A and b ∈ B are either all edges
of G, or are all non-edges in G. See Figure 1 for an example illustrating radius-1 merge-width.

Fractional Twins Our algorithm is built on techniques from computational geometry and the
combinatorics of set systems related to VC-dimension and δ-separated set systems. In particular,
our algorithm uses the multiplicative weight update technique of Welzl [34, 10]. In order to apply
this technique, we now describe how graphs with small neighborhood complexity contain weighted
near twins, i.e. pairs of vertices with almost the same neighborhood.
    For a graph G with distinct vertices u and v, we write ∆G (u, v) := (N (u)△N (v)) ∪ {u, v} for
the set of all vertices that are either equal to u or v or adjacent to exactly one of u and v. We omit
the subscript whenever the graph is clear from context.
    For a positive real number p, we say that a graph G has p-fractional twins if for every weight
function w : V (G) → Z+ , there exist distinct vertices u, v ∈ V (G) such that

Lemma 14 (♣). Let p > 0, let G be a graph with p-fractional twins, and let w1 , w2 : V (G) → Z+ .
