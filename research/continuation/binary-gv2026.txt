     Error-correcting codes play central role in ensuring reliable data transmission and
storage in the presence of noise. One of the foundational results in coding theory is
the Gilbert-Varshamov bound (GV bound), which establishes the existence of codes with
asymptotically trade-offs between rate and minimum distance.

decades, many researchers have devoted considerable effort to constructing families of codes
that meet the Gilbert–Varshamov bound and have obtained a wealth of results [10, 11, 12].
     Improving upon the Gilbert-Varshamov bound is a notoriously difficult task [4], [13].
Over the decades, researchers have sought to improve, generalize, or tighten the GV bound
in various directions. The breakthrough work of Tsfasman [14] led to an asymptotic im-

      Quantum error-correcting codes play a central role in protecting quantum states
against decoherence and noise, and are widely regarded as indispensable for reliable quan-
tum information processing and communication [20, 21, 22, 23]. In the past few years, the
theory of quantum error-correcting codes has been developed rapidly, attracting substan-
tial attention from the coding theory community [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Various constructions
are given through classical coding. However, it is still a great challenge to construct good
quantum codes. Shor [20] gave the first construction of quantum codes. Subsequently,
Steane [29] proposed that quantum codes can be constructed from classical self-orthogonal
linear codes. Following these results, constructions of quantum codes through classical
codes with certain self-orthogonality have been extensively studied and investigated. For
instance, quantum codes can be obtained from Euclidean, Hermitian self-orthogonal, or
Symplectic self-orthogonal codes [30, 31, 32, 33].
      While the classical Gilbert-Varshamov bound serves as a fundamental benchmark for
classical error-correcting codes, the pursuit of analogous results in the quantum setting
has attracted considerable attention. Unlike the classical case, quantum codes must satisfy
additional self-orthogonality constraints, making the direct extension of the GV bound
to the quantum domain a highly nontrivial task. Over the past few years, the theory
of quantum error-correcting codes has developed rapidly, and researchers have devoted
substantial effort to constructing good quantum codes, in which the GV bound continues
to play a crucial role [34, 35, 36, 37].
      The first quantum GV bound was established by Calderbank and Shor as well as
Steane [38] for the binary case, marking the quantum analogue of the classical GV bound.
Following this pioneering result, Ashikhmin and Knill [39] generalized the binary quantum
GV bound to the q-ary case by employing the framework of stabilizer codes. Later, Feng
and Ma [40] derived a finite version of the GV bound for classical Hermitian self-orthogonal
codes and subsequently applied it to quantum codes, leading to a tighter finite-length
quantum GV bound. In 2011, Jin and Xing [41] established a GV-type bound for symplectic
self-orthogonal codes and, based on this construction, obtained an Gilbert-Varshamov type
bound for quantum codes.
      Despite these advancements, the quantum GV bound remains far from being tight.
One interesting question is that whether we can improve this bound like the Hamming
metric case. Notably, the approach of [16] fails to be generalized to quantum code as the


                                             3
quantum code is mostly obtained from linear codes in Hamming metric. Moreover, if we
construct quantum code from classic Hamming code, we require that this Hamming code
should be an orthogonal code. This also makes the approach of [18] inapplicable. We also
note that the approach in [19] does not extend naturally to the quantum setting. This
is because their method crucially relies on treating codewords of a random linear code as
essentially independent, thereby approximating the minimum (Hamming) weight by the
minimum of i.i.d. binomial random variables. In contrast, quantum codes are subject to
additional orthogonality constraints, which induce inherent dependencies among the corre-
sponding vectors and preclude such an independence-based approximation. Moreover, the
minimum distance of quantum code is exactly the dual distance of corresponding orthogonal
code which makes the argument more involved. Therefore, further improvement requires
new probabilistic or algebraic approaches that can handle the inherent self-orthogonality
constraints of quantum codes.

2     Preliminaries
     Let q be a positive power and Fq be the corresponding finite field. Given positive
integers n, k, a [n, k]q linear code C is a subspace with dimension k of Fnq . For a (not
necessarily linear) code of length n over Fq with M codewords, the rate of code R is
defined to be logq (M )/n, which is k/n for linear codes. The Hamming distance between
any two codewords ⃗x = (x1 , . . . , xn ) and ⃗y = (y1 , . . . , yn ) in Fnq is defined as

                                    d(⃗x, ⃗y ) := |{1 ≤ i ≤ n, xi ̸= yi }|,

and the Hamming distance of a code C is d(C) = min⃗x,⃗y∈C d(⃗x, ⃗y ). The Hamming weight

of codeword ⃗x be defined as wt(⃗x) = d(⃗x, ⃗0). For linear codes C, d(C) is equal to the
minimal weight over all nonzero codewords min⃗0̸=⃗x∈C wt(⃗x). A linear code C is said to
be a [n, k, d]-linear code if C has code length n, dimension k and minimum distance d.
Let Bq (n, d) = {⃗x ∈ Fnq : wt(⃗x) ≤ d} denote as the Hamming ball of radius d in Fnq and
Volq (n, d) denote the volume of the ball. It is well-known that a code with a minimum
distance d can correct up to ⌊ d−1        2 ⌋ errors. The relative minimum distance δ is defined as
the ratio d/n. In coding theory, the trade-off between the code rate R and error-correcting
ability δ is a central research topic.
