                                                          Exploring the entropic region
                                                                          Laszlo Csirmaz
arXiv:2509.12439v2 [cs.IT] 28 Sep 2025




                                                                              Abstract

                                                   The paper explores three known methods, their variants and limita-
                                               tions, that can be used to obtain new entropy inequalities. The Copy
                                               Lemma was distilled from the original Zhang–Yeung construction which
                                               produced the first non-Shannon inequality. Its iterated version, effects
                                               of symmetrizations, and connections with polyhedral vertex enumeration
                                               are discussed. Another method, derived from the principle of maximum
                                               entropy, has the Copy Lemma as a special case. Nevertheless, none of the
                                               two presented variants is known to generate more inequalities than the
                                               iterated Copy Lemma. Finally, the Ahlswede-Körner method is shown to
                                               employ a hidden application of the Copy Lemma – the underlying lemma
                                               alone cannot generate new inequalities –, which makes this method strictly
                                               weaker than the Copy Lemma. The paper is written in a tutorial style
                                               and concludes with a list of open questions and research problems.
                                               Keywords entropy; information inequality; polymatroid; representabil-
                                               ity; copy lemma; maximum entropy principle.
                                               AMS Numbers 06B35, 26A12, 52B12, 90C29


                                         1    Introduction
                                         Questions about probabilistic representation of structures and structure classes
                                         can often be reduced to the following one: Does there exist a collection of (fi-
                                         nite) random variables with specified entropies? Examples include, but are not
                                         limited to, channel coding [17] and network coding in particular [52], estimating
                                         the efficiency of secret sharing schemes [3, 4, 15, 20, 26], questions about matroid
                                         representability [2], guessing games [47], and finding conditional independence
                                         inference rules [49]. The N -variable entropy region is the collection of the en-
                                         tropies of the non-empty subsets (i.e., the marginals) of every possible jointly
                                         distributed N discrete random variables indexed by the non-empty subsets of N .
                                         Shannon’s basic information inequalities [51] provide linear inequalities among
                                         these entropies, thus yielding a polyhedral bound of the entropy region. The
                                         long-standing open question of whether this Shannon bound is tight has been
                                         settled by Zhang and Yeung [53, 54] by exhibiting a linear inequality that holds
                                         for entropies but is not a consequence of the basic inequalities. Their discovery
                                         initiated intensive research to further investigate the shape of the entropy region.



                                                                                   1
The phrase Copy Lemma was coined by Dougherty et al. [19] to describe the gen-
eral method distilled from the original Zhang–Yeung construction. It has been
applied successfully to generate several hundred sporadic and a couple infinite
families of non-Shannon entropy inequalities [16, 19, 38]. A different method
using an information-theoretic lemma attributed to Ahlswede and Körner [1]
was proposed in [33]; later it was shown to be equivalent to a special case of
the Copy Lemma [30]. Structures satisfying all instances of the Copy Lemma
were named self-adhesive by F. Matúš who characterized self-adhesive struc-
tures for the case when N has at most four elements [39]. Self-adhesivity was
an important ingredient in investigating different representations of conditional
independence models [6, 7].
    The Copy Lemma, and its variants, are especially amenable to numerical
computations [2, 4, 12, 26]. Although the Copy Lemma can utilize previously
generated non-Shannon inequalities, the same effect can be achieved by apply-
ing the Lemma iteratively. The influential paper of Dougherty et al. [19] also
introduces such iterative variants. Additional tricks, such as exploiting the in-
herent symmetry and special structure of the entropy region, can significantly
reduce the otherwise prohibitively huge computational complexity [12].
    The Zhang-Yeung method can be recovered by an ingenious application of
principle of maximum entropy. The principle, which was conceived in its modern
form by E. T. Jaynes [29], is easy to formulate: “If a probability distribution is
specified only partially, take the one with the largest entropy,” see [25]. In this
case the partial specification requires certain marginals (those at the “copied
instance” positions) to be isomorphic to a given, fixed distribution. Since this
requirement imposes linear constraints on the distribution probabilities, the
maximum entropy distribution always exists [8], is unique, and satisfies numer-
ous independence statements, including those provided by the Copy Lemma.
    While it is clear that the Maximum Entropy Method is more general than
the Copy Lemma, it is not known whether it is actually stronger and could
provide additional entropy inequalities. Finally, it is also unknown whether any
of these methods captures the complete entropy region, but there are indications
that this is not the case [6].
    The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls some
basic notions used later, such as probability distribution, Shannon entropy, en-
tropy region, and polymatroids. Basic facts about the entropy region are proved
in Section 3, while Section 4 is a quick introduction to polymatroids and oper-
ations on them. Linear polymatroids and the Ingleton expression are treated
in Section 5. The Copy Lemma and its refinements are discussed in Section
6. Section 7 describes the Maximum Entropy Method and its limitations. The
method based on the Ahlswede-Körner lemma is discussed briefly in Section
8. Section 9 summarizes the results and collects some open questions and re-
search problems. Proofs of two information-theoretic reductions, thinning and
compressing a distribution, are provided in the Appendix.

In this paper all sets are finite. Capital letters, such as A, J, K, N , denote
(finite) sets; elements of these sets are denoted by lowercase letters such as a, i,
j, etc. The union sign between sets is frequently omitted, as well as the curly
brackets around singletons. Thus, N ij denotes the set N ∪{i, j}. The difference
of two sets is written as A−B, or A−b if the second set is a singleton. The star
in A ∪∗ B emphasizes that the arguments A and B are disjoint sets. A partition
of N is a collection of non-empty disjoint subsets of N whose union equals N .
    A discrete random variable ξ, taking values from the finite set X called the

X } specifying that ξ takes the value x ∈ X with P     probability Prξ (x). Being

The support of ξ is the set of elements of X which ξ can take with positive
probability. ξ is uniform if it takes every element of its alphabet with equal
probability.                                             Q

Here A, B, C are arbitrary subsets of N . Shannon information inequalities
(S1) and (S2) below express, in an equivalent form, that these information
measures are non-negative (which, incidentally, follows from the concavity of
the log function, see [51]).
(S1) If A ⊆ B then 0 ≤ H(A) ≤ H(B) (monotonicity),
(S2) H(A) + H(B) ≥ H(A ∩ B) + H(A ∪ B) (submodularity).

Monotonicity in (S1) is equivalent to the non-negativity of the conditional en-
tropy H(B |A) ≥ 0, while submodularity in (S2) is the non-negativity of the
conditional mutual information I(A, B |A ∩ B) ≥ 0 in a different form. Non-
negativity of mutual information is the special case of (S2) when A ∩ B = ∅.
Non-negativity of I(A, B |C) for arbitrary subsets A, B, C also follows from (S1)
and (S2).
    Any joint distribution ξ on base N gives rise to the marginal entropies Hξ (A)
arranged into a vector indexed by the non-empty subsets of N . The collection
of these (2|N | −1)-dimensional real vectors forms the entropy region, which is
denoted by ΓN∗ [51]. Elements of ΓN∗ are considered interchangeably as vectors,
as points in this high-dimensional Euclidean space, and as functions assigning
non-negative real numbers to non-empty subsets of the base set N . A (2|N | −1)-
dimensional point (or vector, or a real function on the non-empty subsets of N )
is entropic if it is in ΓN∗ , and it is almost entropic, or aent, if it is in ΓN∗ , the
closure of ΓN∗ in the usual Euclidean topology. In other words, x is aent, if for
each positive ε there is an entropic y ∈ ΓN∗ such that ∥x − y∥ < ε in the usual
Euclidean norm. The entropy profile of the distribution ξ is the vector formed
from the marginal entropies of ξ, and the vector x ∈ ΓN∗ (or point, or function)
is represented by the distribution ξ if x is the entropy profile of ξ.
    Notions of conditional entropy, mutual information and conditional mutual
information are formally extended to the functional form of entropic points.
If f is a function on subsets of N then the following notations are used as

    Shannon inequalities (S1) and (S2) impose linear constraints on the entropy
region ΓN∗ . The collection of vectors satisfying all Shannon inequalities is de-
noted by ΓN , clearly ΓN∗ ⊆ ΓN . ΓN is a pointed polyhedral cone [55] as it is
bounded by finitely many hyperplanes and each bounding hyperplane contains
the origin. The collection of the facets of ΓN is the (unique) minimal subset
of Shannon-inequalities – the so-called basic inequalities, see [51] – which imply
all other Shannon inequalities. This minimal set, written in functional form,
consists of the following inequalities:
(B1) f (N −i) ≤ f (N )   for all i ∈ N , and
(B2) f (aK)+f (bK) ≥ f (K)+f (abK) for all K ⊆ N and different a, b ∈ N −K
     (including K = ∅ with f (∅) = 0).
It is worth pointing out that the non-negativity of f follows from (B1) and (B2)
and needs not be specified separately.
    Polymatroids are the elements of ΓN , written as functions that assign non-
negative real numbers to non-empty subsets of N . In this sense polymatroids are
axiomatized by the (basic) Shannon inequalities (B1) and (B2). Polymatroids
are discussed in more detail in Section 4.
    A non-Shannon (linear) entropy inequality is a half-space in the (2|N | −1)-
dimensional Euclidean space (the space of the entropy region ΓN∗ ), which goes
through the origin (so this half-space can be specified by the coefficients of its
normal), contains the complete entropy region ΓN∗ on its non-negative side, and
cuts into the polymatroidal region ΓN (implying that it is not a consequence of
the Shannon inequalities). The intersection of all valid linear entropy inequali-
ties is the closure of the conic hull of ΓN∗ .
    A natural strategy to separate ΓN and ΓN∗ by such an inequality is the follow-
ing. Find some linear operation on the real vectors that maps entropic vectors
into entropic vectors but maps some polymatroid outside the polymatroid re-
gion. If such an operation is found, the entropic region sits inside the pullback
of the polymatroid region along the inverse of the operation. Since the op-
eration is linear, the pullback of one of the bounding hyperplanes provides a
linear inequality that separates ΓN and ΓN∗ . In fact, this strategy works with
the additional twist that ΓN∗ is mapped to ΓM  ∗

3     The entropy region
This section explores various properties of the entropy region ΓN∗ . Many of the
results and proofs in this section are from the paper [37] by F. Matúš. For
additional material on Information Theory and on the entropy region, consult
one of the books [11] or [17] or [51].
     For each real number α ≥ 0, there is a random variable ξ such that H(ξ) = α.
In fact, the range the entropy of a random variable defined on the alphabet X can
take is the closed interval [0, log2 |X |], and ξ takes the upper bound only when
it is uniform on X . From this it follows immediately that Γ1∗ , the 1-dimensional
entropy region for a single variable, is the non-negative real half-line.

3.1    Independence
Two random variables ξ and η jointly distributed on X × Y are independent if
for every x ∈ X and every y ∈ Y,

                    Pr(ξ = x, η = y) = Pr(ξ = x) · Pr(η = y);                     (1)

this occurs if and only if H(ξη) = H(ξ) + H(η). If ξ is defined on X and η is
defined on Y, then joining them independently yields the distribution ξ × η on
X × Y that has independent marginals identical to ξ and η. The probability
mass of this distribution is determined uniquely by the formula (1). If both ξ
and η are jointly distributed on the same base set N , then ξ × η can also be
considered jointly distributed on the same base set. In this case the marginal of
ξ × η on A ⊆ N is isomorphic to the independent join of the marginals ξA and
ηA , therefore,

                  Hξ×η (A) = Hξ (A) + Hη (A)        for all A ⊆ N.

This observation proves the first part of the following claim.
Claim 1. Both the entropy region ΓN∗ and the almost entropy region ΓN∗ are
closed under addition.

clearly in ΓN∗ , this claim can be expressed equivalently as ΓN∗ + ΓN∗ = ΓN∗ , and
ΓN∗ + ΓN∗ = ΓN∗ .
Proof. The claim for ΓN∗ was proved above. The other part follows from this
part and the continuity of vector addition.

3.2    Restricting, factoring, duplicating
Restricting a distribution to a subset of variables means discarding the rest and
replacing the probabilities with their marginal values. Factoring is the process
when a group of variables is taken as (or grouped into) a single variable. Finally
duplicating means adding an identical copy of one of the variables so that the
duplicate always takes the same value as the original.
    These operations on distributions have their corresponding operations on
the entropy region since the entropy of the result depends only on the entropy
of the operands and not on the particular distribution. If f is the entropy profile
of a distribution ξ on N and the distribution is restricted to the smaller base
M ⊆ N , then the entropy profile of the restricted distribution will be denoted
by f ↾M . Clearly, (f ↾M )(A) = f (A) for all A ⊆ M , and this value does not
depend on the particular distribution ξ. Using f ↾M is a slight abuse of notation
as f is restricted not to M but to the (non-empty) subsets of M . The restriction
operation is also known as deleting the complement of M , and the corresponding
notation is f \ (N −M ).
    Factoring requires an equivalence relation ∼ on N ; this relation defines the
groups of variables that are to be merged. When lifted to the entropy profile

do on their union. Again, the result clearly depends on the profile only and not

Claim 7. If λ is not of the form log2 n for some integer n ≥ 2, then λu is not

Proof. Take a distribution on N = {a, b, c} with entropy profile λu. Then any
two of the variables a, b, c are independent and any two determine the third
one. Denote the alphabets of the variables by A, B, and C, respectively, keeping
only those elements that occur with positive probability. Create a matrix M
with rows indexed by A ∪ {∗} and columns indexed by B ∪ {∗}. The matrix
entry M[a, b] in the row marked by a and in the column marked by b is the list

                                ⟨Pr(a, b, c) : c ∈ C⟩,

where ∗ is interpreted as taking the marginal. For example, M[∗, ∗] is the
marginal distribution of c. Since b and c are independent, M[∗, b] equals M[∗, ∗]
multiplied by Pr(b), and M[a, ∗] is the multiple of M[∗, ∗] by Pr(a). In addition,
the sum of the probabilities in the list M[a, b] is Pr(a, b), which is not zero since
a and b are independent.
   By assumption, a and b together determine the value of c. This means that
the list at M[a, b] contains exactly one non-zero probability. Furthermore, since
a and c together determine the value of b, the same non-zero position c cannot
occur twice in the row indexed by a. The sum of lists in row a is the list at
M[a, ∗] with all non-zero probabilities, thus in row a all non-zero positions occur
exactly once. By symmetry, the same holds for each column b.
   Suppose that the only non-zero entry in M[a, b] is labeled by c. It is the same
value that appears in M[a, ∗] at position c; thus it is Pr(a) Pr(c). Similarly, the
same number occurs at position c in M[∗, b], thus it also equals Pr(b) Pr(c).
Therefore Pr(a) = Pr(b) for any a and b, which means that all these marginal
probabilities are the same, that is, a takes every value with the same probability.
Thus H(a) = log2 |A|, which is a logarithm of an integer number.

Theorem 8. ΓN∗ , the closure of ΓN∗ , is a full-dimensional convex cone. Fur-
thermore, internal points of ΓN∗ are entropic.
Proof. Since non-negative multiples of the vector rA are entropic, their conic
(that is, non-negative linear) combination is also entropic. The vectors rA span

Let {ξi : i ∈ N } be a quasi-uniform distribution where each alphabet Xi is
“large enough”. Fix the set Z ⊂ N of the variables; the subset A ⊆ N , however,
will vary. Let Z be the set of elements from XZ that Z can take with positive
probability, and define A similarly. Since the distribution is quasi-uniform, each
element of Z has probability 1/|Z|, and each element of A has probability 1/|A|.
Consequently, H(Z) = log2 |Z|, and H(A) = log2 |A|, or after exponentiation,
|Z| = 2H(Z) and |A| = 2H(A) .
    Consider the matrix M with columns labeled by Z and rows labeled by
A, where the entry at M[a, z] is the probability Pr(ξA = a, ξZ = z). Quasi-
uniformity implies that each matrix entry is either zero or 2−H(AZ) (to account
for the total entropy of AZ). The probabilities in a row add up to 1/|A| =
2−H(A) (the probability of that row), and in a column they add up to 2−H(Z) .
Therefore, of the |Z| = 2H(Z) many elements in a row exactly

many columns from M and then renormalizing the probabilities. Consider the
row labeled with a ∈ A. When choosing a column randomly, the probability

4    Polymatroids
Polymatroids are non-negative real functions defined on the (non-empty) subsets
of some finite base N that satisfy the Shannon inequalities (S1) and (S2). As the
basic inequalities imply all Shannon inequalities, polymatroids are axiomatized
by the inequalities in (B1) and (B2).
    Polymatroids, as their name suggests, emerged as generalizations of ma-
troids, which, in turn, were designed to capture fundamental properties of de-
pendencies and independencies in matrices and linear spaces [44]. Many polyma-
troid terms originate from matroids. The intimate connection between polyma-
troids and Shannon information was recognized by Fujishige [21]. This connec-
tion was used extensively, either implicitly or explicitly, to investigate numerous
optimization problems related to entropies. For a comprehensive overview, see
[43]. This section recalls some terminology and results for polymatroids and
investigates how these are related to the notions discussed in Section 3.
    A polymatroid is usually defined by specifying both the function, called the
rank function, and the base set N , as the tuple (f, N ). When convenient, the
rank of the empty set is assumed to be zero. When it is clear from the context,
the base of the polymatroid is omitted and the rank function f alone defines the
polymatroid. A polymatroid is identified with the (2|N | −1)-dimensional vector
(or point) consisting of the rank values. f is a polymatroid if and only if this
vector, also written as f , is an element of the cone ΓN . The polymatroid is
entropic if its rank function determines an entropic point in ΓN∗ , and it is almost
entropic, or aent, if f ∈ ΓN∗ .

                                             17
    A matroid is an integer valued polymatroid with the additional requirement
that the rank of each singleton is either zero or one, implying that the rank of
A ⊆ N is at most |A|. In addition to defining matroids by their rank functions,
matroids have several combinatorial axiomatizations as well, for details consult
[44]. Polymatroids can be considered “fractional” matroids, and in contrast to
matroids, they are geometrical objects rather than combinatorial ones.
    Many operations on entropic points discussed in Section 3 apply to, or gener-
alize to, polymatroids, and many have the corresponding operation on matroids.
However, the terminology sometimes differs. Operations such as restricting, fac-
toring, and duplicating in Section 3.2 generalize to polymatroids trivially, so we
discuss them only to introduce the terminology.
    The polymatroid (g, M ) is a restriction of (f, N ), or (f, N ) is an extension
of (g, M ), if M ⊆ N and g(A) = f (A) for all A ⊆ M . If N has exactly one more
element than M , then (f, N ) is a one-element, or one-point extension. The
restriction of (f, N ) to M ⊆ N is determined uniquely by the base set M and
is written as f ↾M . Deleting K ⊆ N , written as f \ K, is the same as restricting
f to the complement of K: f \ K = f ↾(N −K).
    Factoring requires an equivalence relation ∼ on N ; the base of the factor
f /∼ is the set of equivalence classes, and the rank is the f -rank of the union of
the corresponding equivalence classes. The factor is clearly polymatroid.
    The operation of duplicating the element a ∈ N in the polymatroid (f, N ) is
called parallel extension along a; the resulting one-element extension is denoted
by fa∥ , and the added element is denoted by a′ . Contracting a polymatroid
is the analogue of conditioning: the contract of (f, N ) to M = N −K, is the
                 c
polymatroid (fM    , M ) where
                           c
                          fM : A 7→ f (A|K),     A ⊆ M,

and f (A|K) = f (AK) − f (K). The contract is also written as f̸ K, and it
is easy to see that it is indeed a polymatroid. Minors of a polymatroid are
obtained by repeated applications of deletions and contracts. Actually, at most
one application of one operator followed by an optional application of the other
suffices to generate all minors.
    In matroid parlance, independence corresponds to modularity. The subsets
A and B are modular if f (A)+f (B) = f (AB)+f (A∩B). Disjoint modular sets
are sometimes called independent. Setting K = A ∩ B, A and B are modular if
and only if aK and bK are modular for all a ∈ A−K and b ∈ B −K, similarly
to the entropy case.
    The direct sum (f0 , N0 ) ⊕ (f1 , N1 ) of polymatroids mimics the direct sum
of distributions. First, the base sets N0 and N1 are made (or assumed to be)
disjoint. Then the base of the direct sum is N0 ∪ N1 , and the rank function is

               g : A 7→ f0 (A ∩ N0 ) + f1 (A ∩ N1 ),   A ⊆ N0 ∪ N1 .

Thus (fi , Ni ) is isomorphic to the restriction of the direct sum to Ni , while N0
and N1 are modular; these properties uniquely determine the direct sum. A
polymatroid is connected if it is not the direct sum of two of its restrictions. In


                                         18
other words, (f, N ) is connected if N cannot be partitioned into two (non-empty,
disjoint) modular parts.
    The subset A ⊆ N is a flat, if every subset larger than A has a strictly larger
rank. In particular, the complete set N is always a flat. The intersection of
flats is a flat, and the closure of a subset is the smallest flat containing it. Flats
of a polymatroid form a lattice with N as the largest flat, and the collection of
all elements with zero rank (which can be the empty set) as the minimal flat.
Elements of zero rank are called loops.

5    Linear polymatroids
Let F be a field, and consider the d-dimensional vector space over F, denoted as
Fd . Suppose that for each i ∈ N we have a set Vi ⊂ Fd of linearly independent
vectors. For A ⊆ N , let f (A) be   S the rank (over the field F) of the matrix
formed from the vectors in VA = {Vi : i ∈ A} as rows; it is the same value as
the dimension of the linear subspace spanned by the vectors in VA . Since the
rank equals the maximal number of independent vectors, we have f (i) = |Vi |.
Clearly, f is an integer polymatroid. Linearly representable polymatroids are
the ones that can be obtained in this way. Occasionally these polymatroids are
called multi-linear or folded-linear, see [2], to emphasize that the vector set Vi ,
assigned to the base element i, may contain several vectors. The polymatroid
is p-representable if it has a vector representation where the underlying field F
has characteristics p.
    As an example, the uniform polymatroid u : J 7→ min{|J|, 2} for all J ⊆ N
from (2) is linear over every large enough field F independently of its charac-
teristics. To show this, choose |N | two-dimensional vectors vi from F2 so that
none of them is a multiple of another. One can do it when F has |N | − 1 or
more elements. Since any two of these vectors span F2 , setting Vi = {vi } gives

           dim(Vi ) = 1,    dim(Vi Vj ) = 2,   dim(VJ ) = 2 for |J| ≥ 3,

as required by u.
    The fact that a collection of vectors forms a linear representation of a poly-
matroid can be expressed as an algebraic condition on the vector coordinates.
Actually, this condition
                       S says which subdeterminants of the matrix, formed from
the vectors in VN = {Vi : i ∈ N }, are zero and which are non-zero. Con-
sequently, the compactness theorem holds: if a polymatroid is linearly rep-
resentable over any field, then it is also representable over a finite field. In


                                         22
particular, f is 0-representable if and only if it is p-representable for every large
enough prime p. Furthermore, since the value of a determinant does not change
when switching from a field to an extension, if a polymatroid is representable
over F, then the same representation works over every extension of F.
   When polymatroids (f, N ) and (g, N ) are linearly representable over the
same field F, then f + g is also representable over the same F (using the direct
product of the representing vector spaces). As a consequence, integer conic
combinations of p-representable polymatroids are also p-representable. It is so
because the underlying fields can be assumed to be finite, say |Fi | = pni , and
then the finite field on pn1 n2 ··· elements is a common extension of all Fi . The
sum of polymatroids representable over fields of different characteristic is not
necessarily linearly representable over any field [45].
Definition 20 (Linear polymatroids). A polymatroid is linear if it is in the
closure of the multiplies of linearly representable polymatroids.
    Linear polymatroids do not necessarily form a cone, but contain the closure
of the conic hull of the p-representable polymatroids for each p.
    For |N | ≤ 3 every polymatroid on N is linear. It is so as in these cases ev-
ery extremal ray of ΓN contains a polymatroid that has a linear representation
over every field. A representation of the exceptional extremal ray u ∈ Γ3 from
Section 3.4 was discussed earlier. Non-negative integer combinations of these
polymatroids are also representable over every field, and multiples of these com-
binations form a dense subset of ΓN .
    Linear polymatroids allow for the extraction of common information. An
element z ∈ N represents the common information of A and B if both A and B
determine z (meaning f (Az) = f (A), f (Bz) = f (B)), and f (z) takes the max-
imum possible value under this condition, which is f (A, B) = f (A) + f (B) −
f (AB). The common information of A and B can be extracted if the polyma-
troid f has an extension with such an element z. Linear polymatroids even have
such a linear extension. Clearly it suffices to show that this is the case when f is
represented by the vector sets {Vi : i ∈ N } over the field F. The new element z
is assigned a maximal independent set of vectors (a base) from the intersection
of the linear span of VA and the linear span of VB . Using this extractabil-
ity property, linear polymatroids for |N | = 4 and |N | = 5 can be characterized
[35, 18]. In both cases, linear polymatroids form a closed polyhedral cone whose
extremal rays contain polymatroids that are representable over every field. For
|N | = 4 this cone is the conic hull of the 35 non-Vámos extremal rays of Γ4 (see
Section 3.4). The cone has 34 bounding hyperplanes. Among these hyperplanes

are determined by the non-negativity of the Ingleton expression

implied by a particular application of the Copy Lemma. So let (f, N ) be an aent
polymatroid, A, D ⊆ N , and the extended function f ∗ on A′ N be the A-copy of

by the non-empty subsets of A′ N , they represent the values that f ∗ assigns to
these subsets. These LP variables will be separated into two vectors. The first
one, denoted as x, contains the main variables whose index is a subset of N ;
the second vector, denoted by y, contains the remaining auxiliary variables.
    Condition (ii) in Definition 24 stipulates that f ∗ restricted to A′ D and AD
are isomorphic. Expressed differently, variables mapped by the canonical map
πA have equal values. This allows us to eliminate those auxiliary variables
whose index is a subset of A′ D but is not a subset of N ; there are (2|A| −1) · 2|D|
many of them. Condition (iii) says that A′ and E are conditionally independent
given D. This implies that every J ′ ⊆ A′ and K ⊆ E are conditionally inde-
pendent given D; this allows eliminating additional (2|A| −1)(2|E| −1) auxiliary
variables corresponding to the subsets J ′ KD; their values are equal to a linear
combination of three main variables.
    The copy (f ∗ , A′ N ) is a polymatroid if and only if all basic Shannon inequal-
ities in (B1) and (B2) hold in it. After incorporating the above reduction of aux-
iliary variables, each of these inequalities can be written as the non-negativity
of a scalar product ⟨pi , qi ⟩ · ⟨x, y⟩ ≥ 0, or as

                                 pi · x + qi · y ≥ 0,

where the constant vectors pi and qi are determined by the particular Shannon
inequality. Collecting the vectors pi and qi into matrices P and Q, (f ∗ , A′ N )
is a polymatroid if and only if the condition

                                  P xT + QyT ≥ 0                                 (10)

holds for the vectors x and y. The linear inequality e · x ≥ 0 is a consequence
of this instance of the Copy Lemma if it is a consequence of the inequalities in
(10), namely, if the following implication holds:
      if all inequalities in (10) are true for x and y, then e · x ≥ 0.
According to the Farkas’ lemma [55], the inequality e · x ≥ 0, which is the same
as ⟨e, 0⟩ · ⟨x, y⟩ ≥ 0, is a consequence of the inequalities in (10) if and only if it
is a non-negative linear combination of them. Consequently the set

                            Q = {hP : hQ = 0, h ≥ 0}                             (11)

contains exactly the coefficients e of those linear inequalities e · x ≥ 0 which are
consequences of this Copy Lemma instance. From this formulation it is clear


                                         31
that Q is a polyhedral cone in the (2|N | −1)-dimensional space. The minimal
set of inequalities (that is, vectors) in Q of which every other is a consequence
(by taking their non-negative linear combination) is the set of the extremal rays
of the cone Q. Computing all extremal rays (and all bounding hyperplanes
simultaneously) of this implicitly defined cone is the task of vertex enumeration
algorithms, see [22, 27] for such algorithms in general, and [12] for the special
case discussed here. The result of running such an algorithm on the cone Q is
the list of the minimal set of inequalities implied by this Copy Lemma instance.
This set, however, might also contain basic Shannon inequalities for N ; these
should be filtered out in a post-processing step.
     The enumeration algorithm outlined above can easily incorporate additional
constraints on the copy polymatroid. Since f ∗ is not only polymatroid but it is
also aent, it satisfies additional non-Shannon inequalities. These inequalities can
be added without much ado as new rows to the matrices P and Q. Also, rows
⟨pi , qi ⟩ where qi is the all zero vector (including rows obtained from Shannon
inequalities on the base set N ) can be deleted safely as they do not contribute to
the condition h Q = 0. A deleted ⟨pi , 0⟩ row contributes the inequality pi ·x ≥ 0
to the consequence set. The inequality specified by the coefficients in pi can be
checked to be a Shannon inequality, or a consequence of other inequalities in
the obtained list of extremal rays. If neither is the case, pi should be added to
the final list of consequences.

6.4    Iterating
Starting from an entropic (or aent) polymatroid, the Copy Lemma guarantees
an entropic (or aent) extension. Thus the Lemma can be applied iteratively to
this new polymatroid, obtaining a larger extension of the original one, and so
on. Such an iterated copy is usually specified by the sequence of subset pairs,
where the pairs are separated by semicolons, and a pair is separated by a colon,
such as
                     ⟨ A1 : D1 ; A2 : D2 ; · · · ; Ak : Dk ⟩.
Here each copied set Aj and the over set Dj is a subset of the base of the previous
copy. Since the copy of a restriction is a restriction of a copy by Proposition 25,
this iterated copy is a restriction of the iterated full copy determined solely by
the sequence ⟨D1 ; D2 ; · · · ⟩ of the over sets.
    As an example, let the base of the initial polymatroid be {a1 b1 c1 d1 }. Take
a d1 -copy over a1 b1 , and mark the “mirror image” of d1 by d2 . That is, the
canonical map π1 of this copy step gives π1 (d1 ) = d2 . Thus π1 : d1 a1 b1 ↔
d2 a1 b1 describes the copy isomorphism, while the conditional independence is
(d2 , c1 d1 |a1 b1 ) = 0. The next iteration is an a1 -copy over b1 d1 d2 naming the
image as π2 (a1 ) = a2 . The final iteration is a b1 -copy over a1 a2 d1 d2 with
π3 (b1 ) = b2 . This copy sequence can be written succinctly as

                d2 =d1 :a1 b1 ; a2 =a1 :b1 d1 d2 ; b2 =b1 :a1 a2 d1 d2 .

The third copy in this sequence is a polymatroid on seven points, as c1 is
carried over. When taking full copies the first iteration results in a six-point
polymatroid, the second iteration in a nine-point polymatroid, and the third
iteration in a 14-point polymatroid. The full-copy iteration can be specified by
the over sets a1 b1 , b1 d1 d2 , and a1 a2 d1 d2 only; the complete specification with
explicit naming of the new elements is

                                 c2 d2 =c1 d1 :a1 b1 ;
                              a2 c3 c4 =a1 c1 c2 :b1 d1 d2 ;                       (14)
                        b2 c5 c6 c7 c8 =b1 c1 c2 c3 c4 :a1 a2 d1 d2 .

Restricting this iterated full copy f ∗ to the seven element subset a1 a2 b1 b2 c1 d1 d2
gives the result of the previous iteration sequence. Observe that f ∗ contains


                                             36
eight isomorphic instances of the original polymatroid; these are at ai bj ck dℓ
where i, j, and ℓ are in {1, 2}, and k goes from 1 to 8.
    Consequences of the iterated Copy Lemma can be determined similarly to
that of a single application which was discussed in Section 6.1. First, the it-
erated copy is translated to an LP instance, and then to a vertex enumeration
problem. The main LP variables are indexed by the non-empty subsets of the
initial polymatroid base, while auxiliary LP variables are indexed by the ad-
ditional subsets of the base of the last copy. Equality constraints expressing
isomorphism induced by the canonical maps as well as the consequences of the
conditional independences are written separately for each iteration. Finally, in-
equality constraints are added, these are the basic Shannon inequalities (from
(B2) if only balanced inequalities are required) written for the base elements of
the last iteration. (Note that these include the same inequalities for smaller base
sets.) From this LP instance the translation to a vertex enumeration problem
of a polyhedral cone Q analogous to the one defined in (11) is straightforward.
The dimension of Q is determined by the number of main variables, which re-
mains the same as for a single copy-step. However, the number of auxiliary
variables and constraints can grow significantly. While the complexity of ver-
tex enumeration algorithms grows exponentially with the dimension of Q, it
grows quite modestly with the number of constraints. These vertex enumera-
tion problems are, therefore, typically tractable for a couple of iterations, and
have been used successfully to generate several hundreds of new four-variable
Shannon inequalities [19].
    A k-iterated copy has the same consequences as the very first copy instance
when the matrices P and Q of that copy step are supplemented by those linear
inequalities on A′ N that guarantee that the subsequent (k −1) iterations can be
applied successfully. The converse is also true: adding a single linear inequality
stated for A′ N provided by some (possibly iterated) application of the Copy
Lemma, can be replaced by adding an instance (or iterated instances) of the
Copy Lemma on top of A′ N which forces that inequality to hold. If more than
one such an inequality is required, further iterations can be added to the chain.
While the two methods:
  a) applying a single copy step augmented with additional (non-Shannon)
     inequalities on A′ N ;
  b) using an iterated copy sequence

are theoretically equivalent, in practice almost always the second one is used
[26]. Determining the inequalities that are needed to be added requires solving
a significantly higher dimensional vertex enumeration problem, which is almost
always numerically infeasible. Also, even a single instance of the Copy Lemma
could produce a large number of new inequalities. Adding all those inequalities
can increase the number of constraints (the rows in matrices P and Q) beyond
a manageable level, while presumably only a small fraction of these inequalities
are actually needed.


                                        37
    Finally, preconditions from Section 6.3 apply for each iteration. If any of
the conditions listed there holds for an intermediate polymatroid, the iteration
step can be omitted safely (or replaced by a larger over set) without losing any
of the consequences.

6.5    Symmetries
Symmetries of the polymatroid can be used to decrease the number of main and
auxiliary variables, thus increasing the efficiency of the polyhedral algorithms.
Symmetries of the copy polymatroid could provide additional restrictions to the
cone Q and can lead to new consequences. We start exploring these symmetries
with some definitions.
Definition 35. (i) An automorphism of the polymatroid (f, N ) is a permuta-
tion σ of the base N such that f (σ(A)) = f (A) for all A ⊆ N .
    (ii) A symmetry, or partial automorphism, of (f, N ) is a permutation σ of a
subset of N such that f (σ(A)) = f (A) for all A ⊆ dom(σ).

    Note that an automorphism of f is also a symmetry. For a permutation σ
with dom(σ) ⊆ N , the restriction of σ to the subset K ⊆ N , denoted by σ↾K,
is the maximal subpermutation of σ on K. That is,
                        [
            dom(σ↾K) = {L : L ⊆ K ∩ dom(σ), and σ(L) = L},

and (σ↾K)(x) = σ(x) when x ∈ dom(σ↾K). The restriction σ↾K can be empty
even if dom(σ) ∩ K is not empty. For an illustration let π be the canonical map
of the full copy of f over D. The canonical map of the A-copy over D, which
was denoted by πA , is just the restriction π↾A′ N .

7    Maximum Entropy method
The Copy Lemma 27 can be recovered by an ingenious application of the prin-
ciple of maximum entropy conceived in its modern form by [29]. The principle
can be formulated as “if a probability distribution is specified only partially,
take the one with the largest entropy”, see [25]. In this case “partial specifica-
tion” means that some of the marginal distributions agree with those of a fixed
distribution. So let N be theQ  base of some fixed distribution ξ = {ξi : i ∈ N }
defined on the alphabet X = {Xi : i ∈ N }. The family of subsets of N where
the marginal distributions will be required to be the same will be denoted by F.
Clearly, if for some distribution η, the marginal ηF on some F ⊆ N agrees with
the marginal ξF , then the marginals on the subsets of F will be the same. Thus
F can be extended by subsets of its elements, or, equivalently, can be restricted

                                          40
to its maximal elements. So let ΥF denote the family of those distributions
η = {ηi : i ∈ N } that are on the same alphabet X as ξ, and, for ever F ∈ F ,
have the same marginal on F that ξ does:

                      ΥF = {η : ηF = ξF for all F ∈ F}.

Since the probability weights in a marginal are sums of the probability weights
of the original distribution, the condition on the marginals is actually a large
number of linear constraints on the probability weights of η. The total Shannon
entropy H(η) is a strictly concave function of these weights, thus finding a dis-
tribution in ΥF with maximum entropy is a concave optimization problem on a
compact space (the space of all distributions on X ) with linear constraints. Con-
sequently, see [8], there is always a unique optimal η̂ ∈ ΥF where the entropy is
maximum. While structural properties of such maximum entropy distributions
are mainly unknown, they certainly satisfy some independence statements.
    To describe these statements, let us introduce some notions. A 3-partition is
a partition of the base set into three pieces as N = X ∪∗ Y ∪∗ D. Occasionally
such a 3-partition is written as ⟨X, Y |D⟩ to emphasize the distinct role of the
D component. When it is clear from the context, the simpler form N = XYD
is used. The 3-partition XYD separates the collection F of subsets of N if no
F ∈ F intersects both X and Y . Thus F is separated into two (not necessarily
disjoint) parts: those F ∈ F which are subsets of X ∪ D, and those F ∈ F ,
which are subsets of Y ∪ D.
Claim 39. Let F be a collection of subsets of N , and let XYD be a 3-partition
of N that separates F. In the maximal entropy distribution η̂ ∈ ΥF , X and Y
are conditionally independent given D, that is, Iη̂ (X, Y |D) = 0.
Proof. If Iη (X, Y |D) is not zero for some distribution η ∈ ΥF , then

                  Hη (XYD) < Hη (XD) + Hη (YD) − Hη (D).

Denote the probabilities in η ∈ ΥF by Prη (xyd) where x, y, d runs over the
alphabets XX , XY , and XD , respectively. Consider the distribution η ∗ on the

This Section discusses how all consequences of a MAXE instance can be ex-
tracted. There are two interrelated notions in a MAXE setting: the family F of
marginals to be preserved, and the family P of 3-partitions of N which separate

F. For any family F of subsets, and any family P of 3-partitions define F ⊥ as
a family of 3-partitions, and P ⊥ as a family of subsets as follows:
            def

            def

Clearly, F ⊥ is just the set of 3-partitions that separate F . These operations

its closure, F ⊥⊥ ⊇ F, have the same set of 3-partitions that separates them.

downwards. Similarly, the MAXE instance can be specified equally by a family P

then it is closed, and P ⊥ contains all non-empty subsets of XD and YD. This
latter set is the same as all subsets of N excluding those that intersect both X
and Y .

that F ⊥ = P and P ⊤ = F; this implies that both F and P are closed. The

these polymatroid values are guaranteed to be preserved by f ∗ ), and e · x ≥ 0
holds in every polymatroid on N that satisfies all conditional independence

9     Conclusions and open problems
Only a few methods and techniques are known to generate non-Shannon infor-
mation inequalities. This paper explored these methods and their relationship,
and investigated some of their variants.

9.1    Foundation
The foundation was established in Sections 3 and 4. Basic facts about the en-
tropy region ΓN∗ , determined by N jointly distributed discrete random variables,
were recalled and proved in Section 3. Natural manipulations on probability
distributions give rise to natural operations on the entropy region. These op-
erations include restricting, deleting, factoring, and independent drawing. Two
sophisticated operations on distributions, called thinning and compressing, were
defined in Section 3.7. They arise as limits of a certain type of random coding.
Similar constructions can be found in the literature as auxiliary tools in converse
theorems of coding problems, but Theorems 11 and 13, expressing properties of
the thinning and compressing operations, have not been stated in this general
form. The corresponding proofs in the Appendix separate typicality consid-
erations from random coding, thus achieving an     √ error term of order (log n)/n
instead of the best possible error term (log n)/ n when typical sequences are
used.
    Polymatroids, the second pillar of the foundation, are the abstract struc-
tures satisfying all basic Shannon inequalities. Section 4 provided basic facts
and specific terminology concerning polymatroids. Many natural operations on
the entropy region ΓN∗ extend to the set ΓN of polymatroids. However, the
terminology sometimes differs. For example, the operation corresponding to
compressing (Section 3.7) is called principal extension (Section 4.1).
    A rich source of probability distributions is linear codes over finite fields.
The corresponding polymatroid class, discussed in Section 5, originates from


                                         50
linear subspace arrangements of finite dimensional vector spaces. The definition
of linear polymatroid is more subtle, because representations over fields with
different characteristics do not mix well.

9.2    Copy Lemma
Finding a non-Shannon information inequality is equivalent to separating re-
gions ΓN∗ and ΓN . A natural strategy is to look for an operation that is ap-
plicable to entropic vectors but not to every polymatroid. In the case of the
analogous problem of separating linear and general polymatroids, it would be
an operation that applies to linear polymatroids but not to general ones. Such
an operation, defined in Section 5, is the extraction of common information.
Using this operation, Claim 21 proves that the Ingleton expression (8) is nec-
essarily non-negative for linear polymatroids, providing a separating inequality.
An important takeaway is that operations applicable equally to almost entropic
and general polymatroids cannot contribute to a linear non-Shannon inequality.
Table 2 summarizes the operations discussed previously, including all standard
operations, indicating which polymatroid classes they preserve. Clearly, none
of them can produce a separating non-Shannon inequality. Table 3 supplements
this list with two entries: the Copy Lemma from Section 6 and the Maximum
Entropy Method from Section 7, indicating that these operations could create
separating inequalities. The question mark indicates that the status of linear
polymatroids is unknown.

    The first method, discovered by Zhang and Yeung [53, 54] and later formal-
ized and named Copy Lemma by Dougherty et al. [19], was described in Section
6. Proposition 25 shows that a (partial) A-copy is a minor of a full copy and
thus cannot have more consequences. Therefore, it is theoretically sufficient to
consider full copies. In practice, however, due to their smaller base size, a par-
tial copy can be numerically tractable, while a full copy may not be. Section 6.1
describes an algorithm that extracts all consequences of a single instance of the
Copy Lemma by transforming it into a vertex enumeration problem [22, 27].
Section 6.2 proves that the new inequalities are necessarily balanced, and to
generate balanced consequences, only balanced assumptions are needed. In par-
ticular, only the balanced basic Shannon inequalities in (B2) should be used by
the extraction algorithm in Section 6.1.
    Section 6.3 presented three “preconditions” that should be checked before
applying the Copy Lemma. If any of them holds, then either the Copy Lemma
yields no result, or the set of “over” variables can be extended to a larger


                                       51
set (while keeping or reducing the computational complexity of the associated
algorithm).
    The iterated Copy Lemma, as described in Section 6.4, mimics adding one
or more non-Shannon entropy inequalities (generated by other applications of
the Copy Lemma) to the copy polymatroid while providing significantly lower
computational complexity. Symmetries, both from the original problem and
from the intrinsic symmetry of the copy polymatroid when applied iteratively,
can further reduce the computational complexity of the extraction algorithm,
as described in Section 6.5.

9.3    Maximum Entropy Method
The Maximum Entropy Method uses the fact that the probability masses of
a distribution can be modified so that some fixed marginals have the same
distribution, while the total entropy, within this constraint, is maximum. Such
a maximum entropy distribution satisfies independence statements that depend
on which marginals are fixed, see Claim 39. Two flavors of this method were
presented. The first one, dubbed MAXE, requires an aent polymatroid f , and
provides another aent polymatroid f ∗ on the same base set, which has the same
restrictions as f on a collection of subsets of the base set, and additionally
satisfies conditional independence statements, see Lemma 40. Claim 43 proves
that the Copy Lemma is equivalent to the special case of MAXE with a single
independence statement. Section 7.1 describes an algorithm that extracts all
consequences of a MAXE instance. The algorithm can easily incorporate the
symmetries of the problem. Results of the algorithm are presented for a couple
of cases in Claim 44.
    The second flavor of the Maximum Entropy Method, called GMAXE, is given
as Lemma 45. It encompasses many instances of the same base polymatroid
marked by a collection of transversals, mimicking the work of the iterated Copy
Lemma, while letting the maximum entropy principle stretch the distribution
to satisfy many conditional independence statements. Defying intuition, high
transversal density destroys the strength of GMAXE, see Claim 46.

9.4    Ahlswede-Körner method
The Ahlswede-Körner method was used in [33] to derive an infinite family of
non-Shannon inequalities. The method was described explicitly in [30]. Proven
in Claim 17, and reiterated in Table 2, polymatroids are closed under the GAK
operation. Consequently, direct applications of the Ahlswede-Körner lemma (or
any of its consequences) cannot produce new inequalities. The actual statement,
stated as Lemma 47, that was used, requires additional machinery. In Section
8 Lemma 47 is shown to be a consequence of the Copy Lemma when copying a
singleton and to be strictly weaker (as it cannot prove that the Vámos vector is
not aent).

The formal proof of Theorems 11 and 13 follows the general idea discussed in
Section 3.7. In general terms both theorems state that some piecewise linear
and continuous transformation Φ(α, f ) (which also depends on a fixed subset Z
of the base set N ) maps almost entropic points f into almost entropic points for
every non-negative value of α. Since the map Φ is continuous and homogeneous
in the sense that for every λ ≥ 0 we have Φ(λα, λf ) = λΦ(α, f ), it suffices to
prove the theorems for a collection of “special” aent elements whose multiples
are dense in ΓN∗ . Indeed, if g is such a “special” function and Φ(α′ , g) is aent
for every α′ ≥ 0, then
                               Φ(α, λg) = λΦ( αλ , g)
is also aent for every non-negative α and λ (since ΓN∗ is closed for multiplication).
By assumption, any f ∈ ΓN∗ can be approximated by a sequence of multiples
of special elements, say lim λi gi = f . Continuity of Φ gives lim Φ(α, λi gi ) =
Φ(α, f ). Thus Φ(α, f ) is a limit of aent points, therefore it is also aent, as
required.
