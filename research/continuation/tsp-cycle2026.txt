                                        1    Introduction
                                        In the traveling salesman problem (TSP), we are given a set of n cities and the costs cij of traveling
                                        from city i to city j for all i, j. The goal of the problem is to find the cheapest tour that visits each
                                        city exactly once and returns to its starting point. An instance of the TSP is called symmetric if
                                        cij = cji for all i, j; it is asymmetric otherwise. Costs obey the triangle inequality (or are metric)
                                        if cij ≤ cik + ckj for all i, j, k. All instances we consider will be symmetric and obey the triangle
                                        inequality. We treat the problem input as a complete graph G = (V, E), where V is the set of
                                        cities, and ce = cij for edge e = {i, j}.
                                             In the mid-1970s, Christofides [Chr76] and Serdyukov [Ser78] each gave a 32 -approximation
                                        algorithm for the symmetric TSP with triangle inequality. The algorithm computes a minimum-
                                        cost spanning tree and then finds a minimum-cost perfect matching on the odd degree vertices of
                                        the tree to compute a connected Eulerian subgraph. Because the edge costs satisfy the triangle
                                        inequality, any Eulerian tour of this Eulerian subgraph can be “shortcut” to a tour of no greater
                                        cost. Until very recently, this was the best approximation factor known for the symmetric TSP
                                        with triangle inequality, although over the last decade substantial progress was made for many
                                        special cases and variants of the problem.
                                             In recent years, a variation on the Christofides-Serdyukov algorithm has been considered. Its
                                        starting point is a well-known linear programming relaxation of the TSP introduced by Dantzig,
                                        Fulkerson, and Johnson [DFJ54], sometimes called the Subtour LP or the Held-Karp bound [HK71].

    In a breakthrough result, Karlin, Klein, and Oveis Gharan [KKO24] show that a variant of
the maximum entropy algorithm has performance ratio better than 3/2, although the amount by
which the bound was improved is quite small (approximately 10−36 ; the ϵ was then increased to
10−34 in [GKL24]). The achievement of the paper is to show that choosing a random spanning tree
from the maximum entropy distribution gives a distribution of odd degree nodes in the spanning
tree such that the expected cost of the perfect matching is cheaper (if marginally so) than in the
Wolsey analysis. Note that the Karlin et al. algorithm actually samples from the set of 1-trees, a
spanning tree plus one additional edge1 , and then finds a matching on the odd-degree vertices. In
this paper, we will study this algorithm based on 1-trees.

We show that the maximum entropy algorithm studied in [KKO24] is a randomized 10    7 -approximation
algorithm for a class of TSP instances known as half-integral cycle cut instances, which are defined
as follows.
Definition 1.1. A pair (G, x) is a half-integral TSP instance if G is an instance of TSP, x is a
feasible solution to the Subtour LP, and x satisfies xe ∈ {0, 21 , 1} for all e ∈ E. It is a cycle cut
instance if for any set S with x(δ(S)) = 2 (we call such a set S a tight set) and |S| ≥ 2, there is
a partition A ∪ B of S such that both A and B are tight.

     One reason why half-integral instances are interesting is a conjecture by Schalekamp, Williamson,
and van Zuylen [SWvZ14], which states that these are the worst-case instances for the integrality
gap of the subtour LP. Currently, the best known approximation ratio for half-integral TSP is
1.49776, due to Klein and Taziki [KT25], which analyzes the max entropy algorithm, and builds
upon other recent work on half integral TSP [Gup+24; HN19; KKO20]. Cycle cut instances are
also interesting because recent papers that break the 32 bound for TSP decompose the graph into
a hierarchy of cuts, where each cut in the hierarchy can be either a cycle cut or a degree cut. Our
definition of cycle cut instances corresponds to the case where all cuts in the hierarchy are cycle
cuts, and so it is plausible that studying this case could provide insights about the more general
case. The class of half-integral cycle cuts contains instances on which the integrality gap is at least
3 , including the well-known envelope graph.

bound of 34 on the integrality gap for half-integral cycle cut instances [JKW25a], and that there
exists a half-integral cycle cut instance on which the maximum entropy algorithm produces a
solution with expected cost at least 11  8 − o(1) times the optimum [JKW25b]. The latter result
shows that the maximum entropy algorithm is not a conjectured 43 -approximation algorithm for
TSP. Still, our result is the first to show that maximum entropy algorithm has an approximation
factor of better than 1.49 on a non-trivial class of instances. Moreover, the 43 -approximation
algorithm in [JKW25a] is specialized to half-integral cycle cut instances, and it is not clear how
to extend it to general TSP instances. Since there is no reason to believe that the analysis of the
maximum entropy algorithm on general instances is tight, our work provides hope (and potentially
direction) for improved analysis on other instance classes. The analysis of [JKW25a] uses the
stationary point of a Markov chain for its argument. This paper uses ideas from that analysis but
without the Markov chain, instead reasoning directly about the stationary distribution. We find it

2       Preliminaries and the Algorithm
We split every edge e with xe = 1 into two edges of value 12 so that G is a 4-regular multigraph
with xe = 12 on every edge. Note that for any tight set S (for which x(δ(S)) = 2), there are four
edges in δ(S). We construct a hierarchy of tight sets as follows.2 We begin by selecting a root
vertex r arbitrarily. Note that both {r} and V − {r} are tight cuts. Using the definition of a cycle
cut instance, we can divide V − {r} into two tight sets, and then recursively divide each of these
into two tight sets, yielding a binary tree T of tight sets. For a tight set S in the tree partitioned
into two tight sets A and B, we observe that of the four edges in δ(S) (with xe = 1/2), exactly two
are also in δ(A) and the remaining two are also in δ(B); thus there are two edges in δ(A) ∩ δ(B).
See Figures 1 and 2. Note that each leaf of the tree corresponds to a vertex of the graph.
    On cycle cut instances, max entropy coincides with a very natural sampling procedure: at each
level, choose one edge between the two children independently, sampling each edge e with probability
xe . We now describe the maximum entropy algorithm on half-integral cycle cut instances more

    It may be instructive to notice why the resulting set of edges is connected. This has a quick
inductive proof: at the bottom of the hierarchy, the vertices are connected components. Now, if we
inductively assume the two children are connected components, then by adding an edge between
them, the parent becomes a connected component as well.
    Let ν be the distribution of edges given by the maximum entropy algorithm. In what follows,
we show how to extend this distribution to a distribution over connected Eulerian graphs where
the expected cost of the graph is at most 10   7 times the LP value. In particular, we will find a
distribution µ over spanning Eulerian multi-subgraphs which stochastically dominates ν. In other
words, to produce µ, we will first sample from ν and then add edges. This will imply our result,
since then µ can be written as a distribution which first samples a tree T from ν and then adds a
matching on the odd degree vertices of T , and the max entropy algorithm adds the cheapest such
matching.

Lemma 3.2. The expected cost of an element sampled from µ is at most 10     7 c(x). Since the max
entropy algorithm adds the cheapest collection of edges making the tree Eulerian, the expected cost
