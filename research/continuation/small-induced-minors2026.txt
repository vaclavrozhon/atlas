                                           We consider the H-Induced Minor problem: for a fixed graph H, decide whether a given graph
                                           G contains H as an induced minor. While the problem is known to be NP-complete for some
                                           trees H on more than 2300 vertices, the complexity for small trees remains unresolved. In particular,
                                           the case where H is the 7-vertex tree consisting of a path on five vertices with a pendant vertex
                                           attached to the second and fourth vertex was a long-standing open problem. We show that this
                                           case is polynomial-time solvable by developing algorithms that detect a sequence of carefully chosen
                                           substructures. Complementing this, we prove that detecting some of these substructures individually
                                           is NP-hard. We also give polynomial-time algorithms for three cases where H is a graph on five
                                           vertices (that is not a tree). In this way, we completed the classification of H-Induced Minor for
                                           graphs H on five vertices and answered an open problem of Dallard, Dumas, Hilaire and Perez (2025).

                                            1     Introduction
                                           For a fixed graph H, we study the H-Induced Minor problem. This problem asks whether a
                                           given graph G contains H as an induced minor; that is, whether G can be transformed into H
                                           through a sequence of vertex deletions and edge contractions. The problem thus belongs to
                                           the broader class of graph modification problems, a central topic in both algorithmic and
                                           structural graph theory. In this setting, the goal is to determine whether a graph G can be
                                           transformed into a graph from a specified family H using a sequence of operations from a
                                           fixed set S. For example, if H = {H}, but S consists of vertex deletion, edge contraction and
                                           edge deletion, then we obtain the classical H-Minor problem. If H = {H} and S consists of
                                           edge contraction, then we obtain another well-known problem, namely H-Contraction.
                                               It is well known that H-Minor can be solved in cubic time for every graph H [19]
                                           (see [14] for an almost-linear time algorithm). However, the problem H-Contraction is far
                                           from being classified and is known to be NP-complete even for small graphs H, such as the
                                           4-vertex path P4 [4] (see [15, 16, 20] for further partial results). In contrast to H-Minor and
                                           similar to H-Contraction, Fellows, Kratochvíl, Middendorf and Pfeiffer [9] showed that
                                           H-Induced Minor can also be NP-complete. As an example, they provided a non-planar

    Known Results. Even classifying the complexity of H-Induced Minor is challenging.
    The problem was shown to be polynomial-time solvable for all trees H with at most seven
    vertices except when H is the graph H2 shown in Figure 1 [10]. It is also polynomial-time
    solvable for all subdivided stars H and for all double stars H, in which one of the two centre
    vertices has at most two leaves [10].
        The polynomial-time results for star-like trees from [10] were recently extended to
    generalized bulls, generalized houses, complete split graphs with clique number at most 4,
    and to flowers by Dallard, Dumas, Hilaire and Perez [7] (a flower is an intersection of paths,
    cycles and diamonds in one vertex; for instance, a subdivided star is a flower). Moreover,
    Bousquet et al. [3] recently proved polynomial-time solvability of H-Induced Minor if H
    is the 4-vertex wheel, the complete graph on five vertices minus an edge, or the complete
    bipartite graph K2,ℓ for all ℓ ≥ 1.
        By using the above results and resolving remaining cases, Dallard et al. [7] proved
    polynomial-time solvability for all graphs H on at most five vertices except for the three
    open cases displayed in Figure 1. In addition, Nguyen, Scott and Seymour [17] gave a
    polynomial-time algorithm for the case H = tC3 (disjoint union of t triangles) for all t ≥ 2;
    we note that the case t = 2 was a long-standing open problem; see e.g. [10, 11].
        In contrast to all these new polynomial-time results, Korhonen and Lokshtanov [13]
    recently answered the open problem of [9] by proving that there exists even a tree H (with
    more than 2300 vertices) for which H-Induced Minor is NP-complete. Afterwards, Aboulker,
    Bonnet, Picavet and Trotignon [1] solved an open problem of Korhonen and Lokshtanov [13]
    by proving NP-completeness for some specific subcubic graph H on 74 vertices, in which
    every edge is incident to a vertex of degree 2.
        From the above survey, it is readily seen that despite all the recent results, there are
    still many infinite families of graphs H for which the complexity of H-Induced Minor
    is unknown. Therefore, the problem has also been studied on special graph classes. In
    particular, it is known that for every fixed graph H, H-Induced Minor is polynomial-time
    solvable for AT-free graphs [12], chordal graphs [2], Pt -free graphs, for all fixed t ≥ 1 [7],
    planar graphs [9] or, more generally, all minor-closed graph classes that do not contain all
    graphs [20]; see [11] for additional polynomial-time results for claw-free graphs.
    Our Results. We first prove in Sections 3–5 that H-Induced Minor is polynomial-time
    solvable for every H ∈ {K, F1 , F2 }, which are the first three graphs displayed in Figure 1.
    As a common technique, we first analyse the structure of a minimum induced minor model
    in each of these three cases, and we use our insights to reduce the problem to a polynomial
    number of instances of the k-Disjoint Subgraphs problem, one of the main pillars in Graph
    Minor theory [19]. Our results answer the open problem of Dallard et al. [7] and combined
    with the aforementioned existing results (see also [7]) leads to the following classification:

 ▶ Theorem 1. For every graph H on at most five vertices, H-Induced Minor is polynomial-
 time solvable.

  In Section 6, we resolve the only open case of a tree H on seven vertices (see also [6]). Namely,
  we prove that H2 -Induced Minor is polynomial-time solvable for the tree H2 displayed in
  Figure 1 (to explain its name, H2 is obtained from the graph that looks like the letter “H”
  after subdividing the middle bar once). In addition, we observe that if H-Induced Minor
  is polynomial-time solvable, then so is (H + Pt )-Induced Minor. Namely, a graph G has
  H + Pt as an induced minor if and only if G − N [S] has H as an induced minor for some
  set S that induces a Pt in G. Since t is a fixed constant, we can branch on all possible options
  for choosing S. Now, if H is a disconnected forest on at most seven vertices, then one of
  its connected components is a path. Hence, our results combined with the aforementioned
  results from [10] imply the following:

  ▶ Theorem 2. For every forest H on at most seven vertices, H-Induced Minor is
  polynomial-time solvable.

  To detect H2 as an induced minor, we first detect specific models of it. If none of these
  models are present, we show that any model must look like what we call a windmill. We then
  rely on a shortest path detector, a technique first used in [5], to detect a windmill (under the
  assumption that the specific models of the first step are not present). To show that a more
  naive approach is likely to fail, we show in Section 7 that it is NP-complete to detect some
  kinds of windmill individually.


   2     Preliminaries
  Let G = (V (G), E(G) be a graph. The neighbourhood of a vertex v ∈ V (G) is the set
  NG (v) = {u ∈ V (G) | uv ∈ E(G)}, and the degree of v is |NG (v)|. We write N [v] = N (v)∪{v}.
  For S ⊆ V (G), we use N (S) = ∪u∈V N (u). We write G[S] for the subgraph of G induced by
  S, and say that S is connected if G[S] is connected. For v ∈ V (G) and S ⊆ V (G), we say
  that v is complete to S if S ⊆ N (v), and anti-complete to S if S ∩ N (v) = ∅. A set T ⊆ V (G)
  is (anti-)complete to S if every v ∈ T is (anti-)complete to S. Two disjoint sets S, T ⊆ V (G)
  are adjacent if there is an edge with one endpoint in S and the other one in T . Let F be
  another graph. We write F ⊆ G if V (F ) ⊆ V (G) and E(F ) ⊆ E(G).
      If u ∈ V (P ) and v ∈ V (P ) for some path P , then uP v denotes the subpath of P from u
  to v. A tripod with ends v1 , v2 , v3 is a tree of maximum degree 3 with exactly one vertex of
  degree 3 that has v1 , v2 v3 as its leaves. A triangle tripod with ends v1 , v2 , v3 is obtained by
  taking three vertex-disjoint paths Pi with end-vertices ui and vi (with possibly ui = vi ) for
  i ∈ {1, 2, 3} and adding the edges u1 u2 , u1 u3 and u2 u3 . Note that a triangle is a triangle
  tripod whose three vertices are the ends.
      Let G be a graph that contains a graph H as an induced minor. A set X = {Xy | y ∈
  V (H)} is an induced minor model, or more specifically, an H-model in G if:

  (i) for every y ∈ V (H), Xy is non-empty;
 (ii) for every y, z ∈ V (H), Xy ∩ Xz = ∅; and
(iii) for every y, z ∈ V (H), Xy and Xz are adjacent if and only if yz ∈ E(H).

  For y ∈ V (H), we write Gy = G[Xy ]. Note that we obtain H by contracting edges along a
  spanning tree in every Gy and deleting any vertex that is not in a bag Xy for some y ∈ V (H).
  Note also that a graph G may have multiple H-models for some graph H.

For fixed k ≥ 2, the k-Disjoint Subgraphs problem has as input a graph G with k pairwise
disjoint vertex subsets Z1 , . . . , Zk ⊆ V (G). It asks whether there exist k pairwise disjoint
connected vertex subsets S1 , . . . , Sk with Zi ⊆ Si for every i ∈ {1, . . . , k}. For a constant
c ≥ 1, a subclass of instances (G, Z1 , . . . , Zk ) is c-bounded if |Zi | ≤ c for every i ∈ {1, . . . , k}.
The k-Disjoint Subgraphs problem is NP-complete even if k = 2 and |Z1 | = 2 [21], but
polynomial-time solvable for c-bounded instances, as proven by Robertson and Seymour.

▶ Theorem 5 ([19]). For every pair of constants c ≥ 1 and k ≥ 2, k-Disjoint Subgraphs
is polynomial-time solvable for c-bounded instances.

▶ Theorem 7. K-Induced Minor is polynomial-time solvable.

Proof. Let G be a graph. From Lemma 6 it follows that G contains K as an induced minor
if and only if G contains some K-model X such that |X1 | = |X5 | = 1, and |N (X1 ) ∩ X2 | ≤ 3,
and |N (X5 ) ∩ X3 | ≤ 2, and |N (X5 ) ∩ X4 | ≤ 2. Hence, it suffices to suffices to decide if G
contains a K-model of this form. Our algorithm will proceed by branching over all options
for the sets X1 , X5 , N (X1 ) ∩ X2 , N (X5 ) ∩ X3 and N (X5 ) ∩ X4 as well as three edges that
will connect the sets X2 , X3 , X4 . We then decide if there exists a K-model X in G such that
these vertices/edges are contained in those bags as described.
    Namely we consider all possible O(n15 ) options of choosing a vertex u to form X1 ; a
vertex v ∈ / N (u) to form X5 ; at most three vertices a1 , a2 , a3 of N (u) \ N (v) to go into
X2 ; at most two vertices b1 , b2 of N (v) \ N (u) to go into X3 ; at most two vertices c1 , c2 of
N (v) \ N (u) to go into X4 ; an edge a4 b3 with a4 ∈         / N (v) and b3 ∈     / N (u) to go into X2 and X3 ,
respectively; an edge a5 c3 with a5 ∈     / N (v) and c3 ∈      / N (u) to go into X2 and X4 respectively;
and an edge b4 c4 with b4 ∈       / N (u) and c4 ∈    / N (u) to go into X3 and X4 , respectively. We
require that the sets {a1 , . . . , a5 }, {b1 , . . . , b4 }, {c1 , . . . , c4 } are pairwise disjoint. However,
we not require that the vertices a1 , . . . , a5 are pairwise distinct, likewise we allow duplications
in the vertices b1 , . . . , b4 and the vertices c1 , . . . , c4 . See Figure 5.
    Given that vertex 1 is anti-complete to {3, 4, 5} in K, the sets X2 , X3 and X5 cannot
contain any vertex of N [u] ∪ N [v]. We may therefore consider the graph G′ obtained from
G by removing all vertices of N [u] ∪ N [v]. We set Z1 = {a1 , . . . , a5 }, Z2 = {b1 , . . . , b4 } and
Z3 = {c1 , . . . , c4 }. We highlight that (G′ , Z1 , Z2 , Z3 ) is a 5-bounded instance of 3-Disjoint
Connected Subgraphs. Hence, by Theorem 5, we find in polynomial time whether V (G′ )
contains three pairwise disjoint connected sets S1 , S2 , S3 with Zi ⊆ Si for every i ∈ {1, 2, 3}.
If such sets exist, then we let X1 = {u}, X2 = S1 , X3 = S2 , X4 = S3 and X5 = {v}. By
construction, X is a K-model in G. Otherwise, we discard our guess, as there is no K-model
in G such that those guessed vertices are contained in the sets as described.
    The correctness of our algorithm follows from its description. Since we apply Theorem 5
O(n15 ) times, our algorithm runs in polynomial time.                                                         ◀

   ▶ Lemma 16. Let G be a graph with no small H2 -model. For every minimum H2 -model X

  (i) X1 = {x1 }, X3 = {x3 }, X4 = {x4 }, X5 = {x5 }, X7 = {x7 } for x1 , x3 , x4 , x5 , x7 ∈ V (G);

  Recall that for a frame F = (x1 , a1 , a2 , x3 , x5 , b1 , b2 , x7 ), the F-reduced graph is the graph
  GF = G − (N [x1 , x3 , x5 , b1 , b2 , x7 ] \ {a1 , a2 }). We now also define the reversed F-reduced
  graph of F as GrF = G − (N [x1 , x3 , x5 , a1 , a2 , x7 ] \ {b1 , b2 }).
     We are now ready to present our algorithm. In its correctness proof, we use the shortest
  path detector technique of [5], namely in two consecutive applications of Lemma 15.

  ▶ Theorem 17. H2 -Induced Minor is polynomial-time solvable.

  Proof. We describe an O(n9 )-time algorithm. First apply the O(n9 ) time algorithm of
  Lemma 14 to decide whether the input G contains a small H2 -model X . If so, output X .
  Else, consider all O(n8 ) 8-tuples (x1 , a1 , a2 , x3 , x5 , b1 , b2 , x7 ) that induce a subgraph of G

       with exactly the four edges x1 a1 , a2 x3 , x5 b1 , b2 x7 . For each 8-tuple, do as follows. Compute
       a shortest path R from a1 to a2 in GF and a shortest path R′ from b1 to b2 in GrF . Let
       P = x1 a1 Ra2 x3 and Q = x5 b1 R′ b2 x7 . Check if P and Q are mutually induced and if there
       exists a vertex c in G − N [x1 , x3 , x5 , x7 ] that has a neighbour in P as well as a neighbour in
       Q. If so, output the H2 -model X in G with X1 = {x1 }, X3 = {x3 }, X5 = {x5 }, X7 = {x7 },
       X4 = {c}, X2 = V (R) and X7 = V (R′ ). After considering all tuples, output no and stop.
           As processing each 8-tuples takes O(n) time and there are O(n8 ), our algorithm runs in
       time O(n9 ). We now prove correctness. It is readily seen that any output X is indeed an
       H2 -model of G. Now suppose G contains H2 as an induced minor. We will show that our
       algorithm will find an H2 -model in G.
           If G contains H2 as an induced minor, then there is a minimum H2 -model X in G that
       maximises X4 . As G contains no small H2 -model, we can apply Lemma 16. It follows that

       (i)   X1 = {x1 }, X3 = {x3 }, X4 = {x4 }, X5 = {x5 }, X7 = {x7 } for x1 , x3 , x4 , x5 , x7 ∈ V (G);
      (ii)   G2 is a path from a unique X1 -attachment a1 to a unique X3 -attachment a2 ;
     (iii)   G6 is a path from a unique X5 -attachment b1 to a unique X7 -attachment b2 ;
      iv)    x4 is complete to X2 ∪ X6 (but anti-complete to {x1 , x3 , x5 , x7 }).

       Hence, P = x1 a1 G2 a2 x3 and Q = x5 b1 G6 b2 x7 are mutually induced paths with frame
       (x1 , a1 , a2 , x3 , x5 , b1 , b2 , x7 ) and centre x. That is, P, Q form a suitable pair, which is minimum,
       as X is minimum. When our algorithm considers the 8-tuple (x1 , a1 , a2 , x3 , x5 , b1 , b2 , x7 ), it

         7      Windmills

       For positive integers a, b, c and d, a graph G is an (a, b, c, d)-windmill if G contains two
       mutually induced paths P = u1 . . . uk on k ≥ a+b+2 vertices and Q = v1 . . . vℓ on ℓ ≥ c+d+2
       vertices, plus a vertex z called the centre that is anti-complete to X = {u1 , . . . , ua } ∪
       {uk−b+1 , . . . , uk } ∪ {v1 , . . . , vc } ∪ {vℓ−d+1 , . . . , vℓ } and complete to (V (P ) ∪ V (Q)) \ X. It
       follows that z has degree at least 4. For fixed a, b, c, d, the (a, b, c, d)-Windmill problem is
       to decide if a given graph contains an (a, b, c, d)-windmill.
           Detecting a windmill is a problem similar to the main subroutine in the proof of The-
       orem 17. However, some of its variants are in general NP-complete. We rely on the following
       NP-completeness results. By 2-in-a-hole we mean the problem whose instance is a graph G
       with two prescribed vertices x and y of degree 2, and whose question is whether there is
       some chordless cycle of G containing x and y. By Induced 2-Disjoint Paths we mean the
       problem whose instance is a graph G with four prescribed vertices x′ , x′′ , y ′ , y ′′ and whose
       question is whether G contains two mutually disjoint paths from x′ to y ′ and x′′ to y ′′ . A
       hub in a graph is a vertex that has at least three neighbours of degree at least 3.

       ▶ Theorem 18 (see [8]). The problems 2-in-a-hole and Induced 2-Disjoint Paths are
       NP-complete, even when restricted to hub-free instances.
T. Eagling-Vose, B. Martin, D. Paulusma and N. Trotignon                                                                   17


Observe that in [8], only 2-in-a-Hole is proven to be NP-complete, but the proof that is
given also applies (with no modification) to Induced 2-Disjoint Paths. Also note that
solving 2-in-a-Hole can be performed by solving two instances of Induced 2-Disjoint
Paths, so it is an easier problem.

▶ Theorem 19. If a, b, c and d are four positive integers, no three of which are equal, then
(a, b, c, d)-Windmill is NP-complete.

Proof. We first address the case when {a, b} = {c, d}, say a = c, b = d up to symmetry
(so a =  ̸ b since no three integers among a, b, c and d can be equal). We consider an
instance (H, x, y) of 2-in-a-hole and denote by x′ , x′′ and y ′ , y ′′ the neighbours of x and y
respectively.
     Let us build a graph G from H by removing x and y, adding a vertex z adjacent to all
vertices known so far, four paths x′1 . . . x′a , y1′ . . . yb′ , x′′1 . . . x′′c and y1′′ . . . yd′′ and the four edges
x′ x′a , y ′ yb′ , x′′ x′′c and y ′′ yd′′ . We claim that H contains a chordless cycle containing x and y if
and only if G contains an (a, b, c, d)-windmill.
     If H contains a chordless cycle containing x and y, then this cycle must contain either
two mutually induced paths from x′ to y ′ and from x′′ to y ′′ , or two mutually induced paths
from x′ to y ′′ and from x′′ to y ′ . In either case, these paths yield an (a, b, c, d)-windmill
in G. Conversely, if some (a, b, c, d)-windmill exists in G, it must be centred at z since H
is hub-free. Hence, it must contain the four paths x′1 . . . x′a , y1′ . . . yb′ , x′′1 . . . x′′c and y1′′ . . . yd′′
and either two mutually induced paths from x′ to y ′ and from x′′ to y ′′ , or two mutually
induced paths from x′ to y ′′ and from x′′ to y ′ . Note that mutually induced paths from x′ to
x′′ and y ′ to y ′′ cannot manifest as a solution since a =              ̸ b. In either case, these two paths
yield a chordless cycle containing x and y in H.
     We now address the case where a = b and c = d (so a =                          ̸ c). The reduction to the
detection of a windmill is entirely similar, except that now we prove that G contains an
(a, b, c, d)-windmill if and only if H contains two mutually disjoint paths from x′ to y ′ and
x′′ to y ′′ (so we rely on the NP-completeness of Induced 2-Disjoint Paths).
     The remaining cases follow in the same fashion (from the NP-completeness of Induced
2-Disjoint Paths).                                                                                                    ◀

One may observe that according to our proof, some other (possibly meaningful) variants
of detecting a windmill can also be shown to be NP-complete, but we are not sure there is
sufficient motivation to list all such possible problems (see also Section 8 for some further
discussion).


 8       Conclusions
By resolving the three open cases of graphs on five vertices and one long-standing remaining
case of a tree on seven vertices, we settled the complexity of H-Induced Minor for all
graphs H on five vertices and for all forests H on at most seven vertices. We finish our paper
with some natural open problems.
    An interesting case, posed as an open problem by Fiala et al. [10], is where H is the double
star in which both centre vertices have exactly three leaves. We note that the polynomial-time
algorithm in [10] for the case where H is a double star in which one of the centre vertices
has at most two leaves cannot be used. Another interesting open case on six vertices, which
follows naturally from the result of Bousquet et al. [3] for K2,3 -Induced Minor, is the
graph H = K3,3 : the complete bipartite graph with three vertices on both sides.
18   On Detecting H-Induced Minors for Small H


          We note that our reduction in Theorem 19 does not produce any NP-completeness
     result when at least three numbers among a, b, c and d are equal. For instance, the case
     a = b = c = d = 1 is still open. The problem is that in this case, mutually induced paths
     from x′ to x′′ and from y ′ to y ′′ in our reduction yield a windmill. So, we have no “anchor”
     to force the windmill to go from the x-zone to the y-zone of H.
          We give one more related open problem: what is the complexity of 4-in-2-Paths? This
     problem was introduced in [18] and has as input a graph G with four prescribed vertices x,
     x′ , y ′ , y ′′ . The question is whether G contains two mutually disjoint paths P and Q, such
     that all ends of P and Q are in {x′ , x′′ , y ′ , y ′′ }. If 4-in-2-Path is NP-complete for hub-free
     graphs, then (a, b, c, d)-Windmill would be NP-complete for all a, b, c, d.
