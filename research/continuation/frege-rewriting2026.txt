                                                                                    Abstract
                                                     Inspired by a statement about Extended Frege proof systems by
                                                  Jain and Jin [4, Lemma 3] we prove that:
                                                     • there is a p-time binary relation ≈ between circuits that implies
                                                       their logical equivalence,
                                                     • the relation ≈ implies that each of the two circuits can be rewrit-
                                                       ten into the other one by possibly deleting some gates and adding
                                                       at most seven new gates,
                                                     • if the equivalence C ≡ D has a size s Extended Frege proof or a
                                                       Circuit Frege proof with s steps then there is a chain of circuits
                                                       Ei
                                                                            C = E0 ≈ . . . ≈ Et = D
                                                        with t ≤ sO(1) .

                                           Keywords: proof complexity, Extended and Circuit Frege proof systems,
                                           Boolean circuits, equational logic, term rewriting.


                                           Introduction
                                           Jain and Jin [4] constructed a new type of cryptographic indistinguishability
                                           obfuscator for pairs of circuits whose functional equality has short Extended
                                           Frege (EF) proofs. For this they established a certain property of EF proofs
                                           that can be informally described1 as follows. There is a p-time algorithm

of C) such that C ≡ C ′ and whenever the equivalence C ≡ D has a size s
EF-proof then C ′ can be converted into D′ by a polynomially long chain of
intermediate circuits. All these intermediate circuits have the same under-
lying dag and are obtained from C ′ in consecutive steps, each step changing
the functionality of a logarithmic number of gates scattered in the circuit in
a way that the subcircuits they induce (as defined there) compute the same
Boolean function.
    When I attempted to understand what is the exact formulation and the
proof of [4, L.3] I realized that a similarly sounding statement ought to
follow fairly directly using one the ideas behind the finite Boolean valuation
characterization of EF proof size given in [6]. That idea is to consider
the number of steps in Frege proofs instead of EF proof size and to use
equational logic to analyze Frege proofs with a bounded number of steps.
This note elaborates on that and the resulting Theorem 4.1, linking proofs
of C ≡ D with a rewriting of C into D (no padding), appears to isolate a
rather rudimentary property of Extended Frege and Circuit Frege proofs.
    The paper is organized as follows. After some preliminaries about proof
complexity and equational logic in Sections 1 and 2 we consider circuit
rewriting in Section 3. The theorem is in Section 4 and we conclude by a few
remarks. The reader requiring a more detailed proof complexity background
can find it in [7].


1    Proof complexity
We shall consider formulas and circuits using the DeMorgan language: 0, 1, ¬
and binary ∨, ∧. Formulas are terms in this language. A size s circuit C with
inputs x1 , . . . , xn is specified by a straight line program giving instructions
on how to compute values of gates yj , j = 1, . . . , s, from values of earlier
gates: yj is defined to be one of the inputs or a constant, or is computed from
j1 < j or from j1 < j2 < j by ¬ or by one of ∨, ∧, respectively. The program
determines a unique directed acyclic graph (dag) whose edges (wires) into
a gate go from the gates used in its definition. In particular, gates defined
as inputs or constants have in-degree 0 and all other have in-degree 1 or 2.
We assume that the last gate ys is the designated output gate of C and that
it is the only out-degree 0 gate. Note that the program determines also an
ordering of the wires so we can talk about first (left) or second (right) wire
entering a gate.
     A Frege proof system in any complete propositional language is given by a


                                        2
finite, sound and implicationally complete set of Frege rules. For definiteness
we shall fix any system F in the DeMorgan language that has a finite number
of axiom schemes and modus ponens
                                     α1 , ¬α1 ∨ α0
                                          α0
as the only inference rule. This choice simplifies a bit the argument for
Claim 2 in the proof of Theorem 4.1 but it is without a loss of generality as
by Reckhow’s theorem all Frege system p-simulate each other, cf. [3] or [7,
Sec.2.3].
    We shall need the following basic result about Frege systems. A proof is
tree-like if every formula is used as a hypothesis of at most one inference.

Theorem 1.1 (cf. [7, Thm.2.2.1])
   If a formula has an F-proof with k steps than it has a tree-like F-proof
with O(k log k) steps.

   F operates with formulas but any proof system can express statements
about circuits using their definition. Let Def C (x1 , . . . , xn , y1 , . . . , ys ) be the
conjunction of all instructions defining C; it can be written as a CNF. If
D(x) is a size t circuit then we can express the functional equivalence of C
and D by a formula to be denoted EqC,D :

                      [Def C (x, y) ∧ Def D (x, z)] → ys ≡ zt .                         (1)

It is another matter to be able to use circuits as lines in a proof. For this
Cook and Reckhow [3] strengthened F to the Extended Frege system EF by
augmenting F with Tseitin’s [8] extension rule. This rule allows to introduce
during a proof a new extension axiom

                                          r≡β

if the extension variable r does not occur earlier in the proof and does not
occur in β or in the last formula being proved. Using the extension axioms
one may define a circuit and thus EF can indirectly operate with circuits.
    However, it is more elegant to allow circuits directly as lines. This was
formally defined by Jeřábek [5] as Circuit Frege system CF. For a circuit C
let Fla(C) be the unique formula obtained by unwinding C: Fla(C) := C if
C is an input variable of a constant, Fla(¬C) := ¬Fla(C) and Fla(C ◦ D) :=
Fla(C) ◦ Fla(D) for ◦ = ∨, ∧ (the straight line program determines the order
of wires and so also the order of C, D in C ◦ D).

                                             3
    CF is defined as F allowing circuits and not just formulas as its lines and
it has one new inference rule:
                       C
                         , assuming Fla(C) = Fla(D) .
                       D
The property of C, D that Fla(C) = Fla(D) is p-time decidable (cf. [5]) and
CF is thus a proof system in the sense of [3]. Jeřábek [5] proved that EF
and CF polynomially simulate each other w.r.t. proofs of formulas (cf. also
[7, L.7.2.2]).
    For P one of EF or CF and τ a tautology define sP (τ ) and kP (τ ) to
be the minimal size of a P -proof of τ and the minimal number of steps in
a P -proof of τ , respectively. Using the relations between kEF , kF and sEF
from [3] (cf. [7, Sec.2.5]) it holds that the quantities:

             kEF (EqC,D ) + |C| + |D| , kF (EqC,D ) + |C| + |D| ,          (2)

                  sEF (EqC,D ) , sCF (EqC,D ) , sCF (C ≡ D)                (3)
are polynomially related. Here the equivalence C ≡ D is an abbreviation
for the circuit (C ∧ D) ∨ (¬C ∧ ¬D).


2    Equational logic and rewriting
The following set of equations BA between terms in the DeMorgan language
axiomatizes Boolean algebras:

    • ¬0 = 1, ¬1 = 0, 0 ∨ a = a, 1 ∧ a = a,

    • a ∨ ¬a = 1, a ∧ ¬a = 0,

    • a ∨ b = b ∨ a, a ∧ b = b ∧ a,

    • a ∨ (b ∨ c) = (a ∨ b) ∨ c, a ∧ (b ∧ c) = (a ∧ b) ∧ c,

    • a ∨ (b ∧ c) = (a ∨ b) ∧ (a ∨ c), a ∧ (b ∨ c) = (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c).

For two terms s, t we shall write

                                  BA ⊢ s = t

iff s = t can be derived from BA in equational logic having the following
inference rules (u, v, w are terms):


                                        4
   • reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity:
                                    u=v            u=v v=w
                               ,        ,
                       u=u          v=u              u=w
   • congruences:
                            u=v               u = v u′ = v ′
                                   ,                           ,
                           ¬u = ¬v            u ◦ u′ = v ◦ v ′
     for ◦ = ∨, ∧,

   • substitution:
                                       u=v
                                    σ(u) = σ(v)
     where σ is a substitution.

   Crucially for us the provability of an equation can be characterized by
the notion of a local replacement of equals by equals. Define the relation

                                   s ∼BA t

by the condition: there exist terms w(y), u, v where y does not occur in s, t,
and a substitution σ for variables in u, v such that it holds:

   • one of the equations u = v or v = u is in BA,

   • y has one occurrence in w,

   • term s is syntactically identical to w(y/σ(u)),

   • term t is syntactically identical to w(y/σ(v)).

Note that the relation is symmetric and denote by ∼∗BA its reflexive and
transitive closure. By Birkhoff [2] (cf. also [1, Thm.3.1.12]) it holds that

                       BA ⊢ s = t       iff      s ∼∗BA t .

The following lemma states an additional property that we shall need. A
derivation of an equation from BA is tree-like if each equation is used at
most once as a hypothesis of an inference.

Lemma 2.1
    Assume that the equation s = t has a tree-like derivation from BA with
k steps. Then there are c ≤ k and terms r0 , . . . , rc such that

                 s = r0 , . . . , ri ∼BA ri+1 , . . . , rc = t .

                                       5
Proof :
    Fix a tree-like derivation of s = t and for an equation u = v in it denote
by k(u = v) the number of nodes in the tree of the subderivation ending with
u = v. Then prove the statement for all equations u = v in the derivation
by induction on k(u = v).
    As an example of an induction steps assume that u1 ∨ u2 = v1 ∨ v2 is
derived from u1 = v1 and u2 = v2 which were proved by subproofs with k1
and k2 steps, respectively. By the induction hypothesis there are chains of
terms {ri1 } and {ri2 } of lengths ≤ k1 and ≤ k2 linking u1 with v1 and u2
with v2 , respectively.
    First use {ri1 } to link u1 ∨u2 with v1 ∨u2 and then link this with v1 ∨v2 by
using the second chain {ri2 }. The combined chain has the length ≤ k1 +k2 +1
and by the tree-likeness k1 + k2 + 1 = k(u1 ∨ u2 = v1 ∨ v2 ).

3     Circuit rewriting
Different occurrences of the same formula Fla[E] as a subformula in Fla[C]
correspond to different paths following wires from the output gate of C
towards an occurrence of E. These paths can be represented by strings
p ∈ {0, 1}∗ : the output gate corresponds to the empty string and taking the
left or the right wire out of a gate in position p corresponds to extending
p to p0 or p1, respectively (we define that the unique wire into a ¬ gate is
left). The collections of all paths in C is in a bijection with the subformulas
of Fla[C].
    Assume now that u(z) = v(z) ∈ BA (z is a tuple of at most 3 variables),
that Fla[C] ∼BA ψ and that ψ was obtained from Fla[C] by replacing the
occurrence of σ(u) determined by path p by σ(v). Assume that p defines
in C the (occurrence of the) circuit E. We want to describe how can C be
transformed into some circuit D such that ψ = Fla[D].
    Construct D by the following process:

    1. add to C a disjoint copy of v,

    2. delete the last wire in p connecting some gate g to the top gate of u
       and add a wire from g to the top gate of v,

    3. if a gate g ′ feeds into a gate of u corresponding to zi add a wire that
       feeds g ′ also into the zi of v, all zi ,


                                        6
  4. the gates corresponding to inputs zi in v are labeled by the same
     connective as zi in u,

  5. repeat as long as possible: delete any out-degree 0 gate except the
     output gate of C.

Note that D may be smaller than C but not much bigger: always |D| ≤
|C| + 7 as 7 is the largest size of a term in BA.

Lemma 3.1
   For any circuit C and formula ψ such that Fla[C] ∼BA ψ and circuit D
constructed by the above process it holds:

                               ψ = Fla[D] .

Proof :
    If a path q does not contain p as an initial segment then it is not af-
fected by the construction: any subcircuit A of C on path q corresponds to
subformula Fla[A] of both Fla[C] and ψ.
    Assume that q end-extends p. After we travel the p-part in ψ or in D
we end up at the top connective of σ(v) in ψ and on the top gate of the
newly added copy of v in D, respectively. The next few steps (one or two)
inside v define same wires. Once we get in D to a gate of v corresponding to
some input variable zi of v we are on the top gate of a subcircuit B that is
identical to that subcircuit C below the gate of u corresponding to zi . But
in Fla[C] that is σ(zi ) = Fla[B] and hence the same holds in D.

                                                                     q.e.d.

   Define the relation A ≈ B between two circuits A, B by:

                      A ≈ B iff Fla[A] ∼BA Fla[B] .

Lemma 3.2
  The relation ≈ is p-time decidable.

Proof :
  It suffices to show that the negated relation C ̸≈ D is in the class NL.
We have that C ̸≈ D iff

   • either there is a path p ∈ {0, 1}∗ such that C, D differ at subcircuits
     defined by p but

                                     7
         – not by a pair u, v with the required properties (this requires to
           check extensions of p by at most three steps), or
         – they differ by a suitable pair u, v but there is a path starting at
           some input gate zi of both u and v that encounters a difference
           in C and D,

    • or there are two different paths p, p′ such that C, D differ at the cor-
      responding positions by suitable pairs u, v and u′ , v ′ .

Choosing paths correspond to non-deterministic choices of the next gate and
proceed in parallel in both C and D.

4    From proofs to rewriting
We shall state the bound in the theorem in terms of kCF ; as kCF is majorized
by sCF such a formulation implies similar statements for all the measures
listed in (2) and (3).

Theorem 4.1
    Assume that the equivalence C ≡ D has a CF-proof with k steps. Then
there are t ≤ O(k log k) and circuits Ei , 0 ≤ i ≤ t, such that:

    • Ei ≡ C, all i,

    • E0 = C and Et = D,

    • Ei ≈ Ei+1 for all i < t,

    • |Ei | ≤ |C| + 7(i − 1), all i ≤ t.

Proof :
  Let π : A1 , . . . , Ak be a CF-proof of C ≡ D with k steps.
Claim 1: π ′ : Fla[A1 ], . . . , Fla[Ak ] is an F -proof of Fla[C] ≡ Fla[D].
Theorem 1.1 implies that there is a tree-like CF-proof π ∗ of Fla[C] ≡ Fla[D]
with O(k log k) steps.
Claim 2: There is a tree-like derivation of the equation

                              (Fla[C] ≡ Fla[D]) = 1


                                           8
from BA with O(k log k) steps.
    This is proved by translating step-by-step π ∗ into a tree-like equational
logic derivation from BA, each step B in π ∗ being translated into the equa-
tion B = 1. Any axiom of F has a constant size tree-like BA-derivation
because all tautologies get value 1 in any Boolean algebra, BA axiomatizes
them and the equational logic is complete (Birkhoff’s theorem [2]). To sim-
ulate modus ponens we just need to find a tree-like BA-derivation of b = 1
from a = 1 and ¬a ∨ b = 1 in which each of the two latter formulas occur at
most once (otherwise the translation of nested applications of modus ponens
would grow exponentially). An example of such a derivation is:

   • from the first hypothesis a = 1 derive ¬a = ¬1 and using the transi-
     tivity rule with axiom ¬1 = 0 derive ¬a = 0,

   • combine that with the axiom b = b by the congruence rule to get
     ¬a ∨ b = 0 ∨ b which yields (with axiom 0 ∨ b = b) ¬a ∨ b = b,

   • combine this with the second hypothesis ¬a ∨ b = 1 to get b = 1.

Claim 3: There is a tree-like derivation of the equation Fla[C] = Fla[D]
from BA with O(k log k) steps.
    For this we can take any fixed tree-like BA-derivation of a = b from
(a ≡ b) = 1 and append it, after substituting a := Fla[C] and b := Fla[D],
to the derivation we got in Claim 2.
Claim 3 and Lemma 2.1 imply
Claim 4: There are t ≤ O(k log k) and formulas ψi , 0 ≤ i ≤ t, such that

          Fla[C] = ψ0 , . . . , ψi ∼BA ψi+1 , . . . , ψt = Fla[D] .

    The theorem now follows from Claim 4: put E0 := C and use Lemma
3.1 repeatedly to define circuits Ei for i > 1 such that ψi = Fla[Ei ].

                                                                       q.e.d.


    Note that we may consider the sequence of circuits E0 , . . . , Et when D
is the constant 1 as a proof of C in a rewrite proof system: such proofs
are p-time recognizable by Lemma 3.2 as required by the Cook-Reckhow
definition of proof systems [3]. It follows that unless NP = coNP, given
C, D you cannot construct circuits Ei satisfying all the requirements (even


                                      9
using any other definition of A ≈ B that is p-time and that implies the
logical validity of A ≡ B) without some additional assumption (as is the
existence of a CF-proof of C ≡ D with a bounded number of steps in the
theorem).
    Also note that if the starting proof π has size s then the chain {Ei }i
has total size sO(1) . But its construction in the proof does not necessarily
run in polynomial time as the intermediate F-proofs may be exponentially
bigger (this blow-up is avoided if π is an F-proof of the equivalence of two
formulas). However, I suspect that with the ideas behind [6, Lemmas 1.3
and 1.4] (working with a proof-skeleton, information about inference rules
used but not about actual proof lines, and using a first-order term unification
argument) the proof of the theorem can be altered to construct the chain
{Ei }i in polynomial time.
