                                                       Several early quantum algorithms, including Simon’s algorithm and Shor’s period-finding, can be formu-
                                                   lated as instances of the Abelian hidden subgroup problem (HSP). The non-Abelian case is of particular
                                                   interest because some instances, such as the dihedral and symmetric group HSPs, are connected to lattice
                                                   problems and graph isomorphism, respectively. Although no efficient quantum algorithm is known for
                                                   the general non-Abelian HSP, polynomial-time algorithms have been obtained for several special families,
                                                   including certain semidirect product groups and Hamiltonian groups.
                                                       In this work, we give polynomial-time quantum algorithms for two further families containing non-Abelian
                                                   groups. First, we consider groups of the form G = A ⋊φ Zpk , with A finite Abelian, p prime, k ∈ N and the
                                                   action of Zpk is generated by the scalar automorphism a 7→ µa, for some µ ∈ Z×   Exp(A) , where Exp(A) is the
                                                   exponent of A. Our algorithm is efficient when A has bounded generator rank and Exp(A)/p = polylog(|G|).
                                                   This includes the case A = ZN for N ∈ N and k = 1, studied by Bacon, Childs and van Dam [1], and
                                                   A = Zqr with q prime and r ∈ N studied by van Dam and Dey [2]. Second, we give a polynomial-time
                                                   quantum algorithm for finite quasi-Hamiltonian groups under a mild assumption on the input structure.
                                                   Quasi-Hamiltonian groups are finite nilpotent groups with modular subgroup lattice, or equivalently the
                                                   finite groups in which every subgroup is permutable. As far as we know, this is the first quantum algorithm
                                                   to exploit the modularity of the subgroup lattice for solving the HSP. This extends, under the aforementioned
                                                   structured input assumption, the quantum algorithm for Dedekind groups given by Hallgren, Russell, and
                                                   Ta-Shma [3].


                                           1       Introduction
                                           The hidden subgroup problem serves as a unifying framework for several problems for which quantum computers
                                           have an advantage when compared to their classical counterparts [4]. Many early quantum algorithms such as
                                           Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon’s algorithm, and Shor’s period finding algorithm [5] can be cast as instances of the hidden
                                           subgroup problem (HSP). In the HSP, a group G is described by an encoding of its generators and access is
                                           given to an oracle f : G → S, where S is a set of labels. The function f hides a subgroup H ≤ G, i.e., f is
                                           constant on each left coset of H and evaluates to a different label on each left coset. The task is to find a list of
                                           generators of H. No polynomial-time classical algorithm is known for the general Abelian HSP. By contrast,
                                           when G is Abelian, there is an efficient quantum algorithm for the HSP [4, 6].
                                               No polynomial-time quantum algorithm is known for the general non-Abelian hidden subgroup problem.
                                           The interest in the non-Abelian case lies in the fact that an efficient solution to the dihedral HSP would yield
                                           efficient quantum algorithms for certain lattice problems that underlie lattice-based cryptography [7] and the
                                           graph isomorphism problem admits an HSP formulation over symmetric groups. While no efficient algorithm is

    There is a long list of works where efficient quantum algorithms have been proposed for particular non-Abelian
groups. Due to the semidirect product structure of the dihedral group DN = ZN ⋊ Z2 , several works have
studied groups with similar form. We mention some of these works in the following. In [10] the case Zkp ⋊φ Z2
with p prime and φx (a) = (−1)x a with x ∈ Z2 and Zkp . Later, in [11] the authors give an efficient algorithm for
the q-hedral case, i.e., Zp ⋊ Zq with p prime, q | (p − 1), provided that q = (p − 1)/polylog(p) and q is prime.
This result was extended in [1], where an efficient algorithm is provided for ZN ⋊ Zp . Other extensions include
[12] which solves the groups Zp ⋊ Zqs for p, q odd primes, q | (p − 1), p/q = polylog(p) and s a positive integer,
and also [2] which solves the case Zpr ⋊ Zqs where p, q are distinct primes and such that pr /q t−j ∈ polylog(pr )
for certain group-dependent parameters t, j. The previously mentioned works have a restriction on the relative
size of the groups in the semidirect product. In [13], the authors exploit the structure of a family of groups of
the form Zpr ⋊ Zp with p an odd prime to give a polynomial-time quantum algorithm. Notable, this algorithm
does not have the condition which restricts the relative size of the groups in the semidirect product. In another
direction [3], it was shown that the HSP can be solved efficiently for Dedekind groups (groups that have only
normal subgroups). For a more complete review of works on the non-Abelian HSP see [14]. In this work, we
study the hidden subgroup problem in two non-Abelian settings. First, we give a polynomial-time quantum
algorithm for groups of the form A ⋊ Zpk , with A Abelian, p prime, k positive integer, and under the condition
that the generator rank of A is constant and the exponent of A is not much larger than p. These groups contain
several semidirect product families previously studied in the HSP literature. In particular it includes the groups
ZN ⋊ Zp of [1] and the groups Zpr ⋊ Zqs from [2]. Our algorithm is based on a reduction to the Hidden Multiple
Shift problem from [16]. We also give an efficient quantum algorithm based on the pretty good measurement

technique for the groups ZN ⋊ Zpk , with N ≥ 1.
    Second, we give a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for finite quasi-Hamiltonian groups, under a structured
presentation of the ambient group. A subgroup H ≤ G is called permutable, or quasinormal, if HK = KH for
every subgroup K ≤ G, and a group is quasi-Hamiltonian if all of its subgroups are permutable. Every normal
subgroup is permutable, although the converse need not hold. Quasi-Hamiltonian groups strictly extend the
class of groups in which every subgroup is normal, which are known as Dedekind groups. The HSP for Dedekind
groups was previously studied by Hallgren, Russell and Ta-Shma [3].
    Quasi-Hamiltonian groups can also be characterized by their subgroup lattice [17]. The subgroup lattice
of a group G is simply the lattice—in the sense of order theory, do not confuse with lattice as in lattice-based
cryptography—whose elements are the subgroups of G. Quasi-Hamiltonian groups correspond to those that are
nilpotent and have a modular subgroup lattice. Informally, modularity means that the subgroups fit together in
a particularly regular way, analogous to the subspaces of a vector space. To our knowledge, this is the first HSP
algorithm which explicitly exploits modularity of the subgroup lattice.
    A natural question at this point is why care about quantum algorithm for these non-Abelian groups. Solving
the HSP for many of the non-Abelian groups studied in the literature does not have practical applications
(as far as we are aware). Nonetheless, there are a few reasons why studying them is important. As already
mentioned, there are at least two non-Abelian groups where there are interesting applications, the dihedral and
symmetric groups. Understanding what groups can be solved efficiently helps us understand what properties
can be exploited by quantum algorithms and may also shed light on their limitations for the HSP. A property
which a line of research has focused on exploiting for solving the HSP property is nilpotency [15, 18]. In [15]
an efficient quantum algorithm was given for nilpotency class 2 groups, while [18] gave a quantum algorithm
for groups with constant nilpotency class which is efficient if the prime factors of the order are bounded by a
constant. If there was an efficient solution to the HSP for nilpotent groups, we would be able to solve the HSP
for DN when N is a power of two. A heuristic algorithm for such case was already given in [19]. We view our
result on quasi-Hamiltonian groups as complementary to the results of [15, 18], given that these groups are
nilpotent and modular.
    In Appendix A we compare our results with various works in the literature. Section A.1 gives a comparison
of our results with the work of [13]. Our results on quasi-Hamiltonian groups apply to a more general family
of groups compared to their work. Nonetheless, their results apply in the black-box group model while our
results require some extra data as input. In Section A.2 it is shown that the class of quasi-Hamiltonian groups
is incomparable with that of poly-near-Hamiltonian groups in [20]. In Section A.3 it is shown that the set of
quasi-Hamiltonian groups is incomparable with the set of groups studied in [15, 18] (see Fig. 1). An interesting
question that our work leaves open is whether the HSP can be solved efficiently when the subgroup is promised
to be permutable. This would subsume the result of [3] where the normal subgroup case is studied. Note that
there are subgroups in the dihedral group that are not permutable, so solving this question would not imply a
solution to the dihedral HSP.

1.1    Main results
We now explain in more detail the main results of this work. In every case, the hidden subgroup problem is
specified by oracle access to a function that is constant and distinct on the left cosets of a hidden subgroup
H. Recovering H means outputting a set of generators for H. We assume the group operations, evaluation of

the corresponding group actions and the hiding oracle are available as reversible circuits. In the case of group
operations we assume the access is as in the black-box group setting explained in Section 2.1.3.
    Our algorithms for semidirect products depend on a reduction which generalizes the one in [1]. Given a
subgroup H ≤ A ⋊φ Zpk , for A Abelian, we first recover HA := H ∩ (A × {0}) by solving an Abelian HSP over
A. Whenever HA is normal, we factor it out. In the resulting quotient, the residual hidden subgroup has trivial
intersection with the Abelian normal factor, and its projection onto Zpk is therefore injective. Consequently, the
residual hidden subgroup is either trivial or cyclic of the form ⟨(d, pt )⟩ for some element d of an Abelian quotient
of A and some t ∈ {0, . . . , k − 1}. For a general action, if HA is not normal, the hidden subgroup is contained in
A ⋊ ⟨p⟩; iterating this observation eventually reduces the problem to the same cyclic form. Thus, the remaining
task is to recover t and d.
    We first treat the case A = ZN using a pretty good measurement construction. Although this group
family is also covered by Theorem B, the proof is independent and extends the PGM approach of [1] from a
prime-order complement to a prime-power complement. To obtain an efficient algorithm, we require the condition
N/p = polylog(N) so that the probability of success of the PGM is not negligible.
Theorem A. Let p be a prime, k ≥ 1, N ∈ N and let φ : Zpk → Aut(ZN ). If Np = polylog(N ), then the hidden
subgroup problem in ZN ⋊φ Zpk can be solved by a polynomial-time quantum algorithm.
    Next, we consider the more general family G = A ⋊φ Zpk , where A is a finite Abelian group. We assume that
A has constant generator rank (minimum number of generators) and Exp(A)/p = poly(log |G|) where Exp(A)
is the exponent of A. We assume that φ corresponds to what we define as a scalar action. We can always
decompose A into its invariant-factor form as A = ZN1 ⊕ · · · ⊕ ZNρ with N1 | N2 | · · · | Nρ . In this form, an
element of A can be written as a = (a1 , · · · , aρ ). We call φ a scalar action if

Instead, we use the algorithm from [16] for the Hidden Multiple Shift (HMS) problem. The fact that φ is a

2.1     Group theory preliminaries
In this section we give a summary of some of the concepts in group theory that are used in this work. If X is
a subset of a group G, then ⟨X⟩ is the generated subgroup of G. The exponent of a finite group G, denoted
Exp(G) is the least common multiple of the order of the elements of G. An elementary Abelian group is a group
isomorphic to Znp for some prime p and natural number n.
     Let G, K be two groups. The semidirect product G ⋊φ K is a group defined in terms of a homomorphism
φ : K → Aut(G). For simplicity, we write the automorphism φ(k) ∈ Aut(G) as φk . The underlying set of
the semidirect product is the Cartesian product G × K and the group operation is given by (g, k) · (g ′ , k ′ ) =
(g · φk (g ′ ), k · k ′ ). This defines a group with identity (eG , eK ) and inverse

    Let p be a prime. A finite p-group G is a group for which every element has order a power of p. Equivalently,

a maximal p-subgroup of G.

2.1.1    Solvable and nilpotent groups
Both solvable and nilpotent groups are defined in terms of nested series of normal subgroups. This series is
called the subnormal series.
Definition 1. A subnormal series of a group G is a sequence of subgroups {Gi }ni=0 where each is a normal
subgroup of the next and G0 = {e}, Gn = G, i.e.,
                                         {e} = G0 ⊴ G1 ⊴ · · · ⊴ Gn = G.                                       (2)
The quotients Gi+1 /Gi are called the factors.
Definition 2. A composition series is a subnormal series such that each factor Gi+1 /Gi is simple (it has no
normal subgroups except itself and the trivial group).
   Intuitively, this is saying that one cannot break up the subnormal series anymore because the factors are
simple.
Definition 3. A finite group G is solvable if it has a subnormal series whose factors are all Abelian.
   Note that this means that the composition series of a solvable finite group G has factors that are cyclic of
prime order (the only finite Abelian simple groups are the cyclic groups of prime order.)
Definition 4. Given a group G, define the commutator [G, G] as the subgroup generated by all commutators
[g, h] = g −1 h−1 gh for g, h ∈ G.
  If G is Abelian, then [G, G] = {e}. In a sense, [G, G] is measuring how far the group is from being Abelian.
We have that G/[G, G] is Abelian, because the non-commuting part of gh = hg[g, h] has been factored out.
Definition 5. The derived series of G is a sequence of groups {G(i) }i=0 such that G(i+1) = [G(i) , G(i) ] and
G(0) = G.
Proposition 6. A group G is solvable if and only if there exists an integer n ≥ 0 such that G(n) = {e}.
Definition 7. Let γ1 (G) = G and γi+1 (G) = [G, γi (G)]. A group G is nilpotent if γn (G) = {e} for some n.
    The least c such that γc+1 (G) = {e} is called the nilpotency class of G.

2.1.2   Permutability, quasi-Hamiltonian groups and lattices
Below, we explain how permutability, and lattice theory are related. We begin by defining permutability, then
explaining the basics of lattice theory required to understand the proof in this work, and then present some
results that relate both concepts.
   The definitions and results presented in this section can be found in Chapter 2 of [17] (Sections 2.1 to 2.5).
Our starting point is the notion of a permutable subgroup.
Definition 8. Let G be a group. A subgroup H ≤ G is permutable if for all K ≤ G, HK = KH.
   Permutability generalizes the notion of a normal subgroup. When a group has only permutable subgroups,
the group is called quasi-Hamiltonian. These groups can be characterized through their subgroup lattices, which
are defined in the paragraphs below.

Basics of Lattices A partially ordered set (poset) is a tuple (S, ≤) consisting of a set S and a partial order ≤.
A partial order ≤ is a relation on a set S such that for all a, b, c ∈ S one has (1) a ≤ a, (2) a ≤ b and b ≤ a
implies a = b and (3) a ≤ b and b ≤ c implies a ≤ c. A lattice is a tuple (L, ∧, ∨) consisting of a set L and two
binary commutative and associative operations ∧ and ∨ (called meet and join) satisfying the following identities:
for all a, b ∈ L, a ∨ (a ∧ b) = a and a ∧ (a ∨ b) = a. In a lattice one can define the relation ≤ as follows: a ≤ b if
and only if a = a ∧ b. It is easy to check that (L, ≤) is a poset. For simplicity, sometimes we abuse notation and
call L the lattice.
    An example of a lattice is the subset lattice. Given some set X, the set of all subsets of X is a poset with
respect to set inclusion. The operator ∨ corresponds to set union and ∧ to set intersection. In this work we
focus on subgroup lattices. Let G be a group, define the subgroup lattice L(G) as the set of all subgroups, the
operation X ∧ Y is set intersection X ∩ Y and X ∨ Y corresponds to the join of subgroups ⟨X, Y ⟩, i.e., the
generated subgroup by the union of X and Y . The partial order is given by subgroup inclusion.
    The map σ : L → L̃ is a lattice homomorphism if σ(x ∧ y) = σ(x) ∧ σ(y) and σ(x ∨ y) = σ(x) ∨ σ(y).

    An important property of a lattice is that of modularity. A lattice L is modular if for all x, y, z ∈ L, the
following holds: If x ≤ z, then x ∨ (y ∧ z) = (x ∨ y) ∧ z. An element m of a lattice L is modular in L if (1)
x ∨ (m ∧ z) = (x ∨ m) ∧ z for all x, z ∈ L and x ≤ z, (2) m ∨ (y ∧ z) = (m ∨ y) ∧ z for all y, z ∈ L with m ≤ z. A
finite group G is called modular if its subgroup lattice L(G) is modular. When G is a p-group, we also say that
G is a modular p-group.

2.1.3     Black-box groups
The results in this work require that the groups be represented in the model of black-box groups [23]. In this
model, a finite group G is given by encodings of its elements as bit strings of a fixed length, together with oracles
implementing the group operations. Thus, given encodings of g, h ∈ G, the oracle returns an encoding of gh,
and given an encoding of g, it returns an encoding of g −1 . The input also contains a list of encoded generators
(g1 , . . . , gk ), with the promise that G = ⟨g1 , . . . , gk ⟩. If N ◁ G is a known normal subgroup, the quotient G/N
can also be regarded as a black-box group. An element of G/N is a coset gN , and we encode this coset by
any representative g ∈ G. Multiplication and inversion in the quotient are implemented by multiplication and
inversion of representatives in G: (gN )(hN ) = ghN and (gN )−1 = g −1 N . Note that as described above, the
encoding is non-unique (elements of the group can be encoded in more than one way).
     Some of the algorithms in this work require a unique encoding, particularly those from [10]. As described in
Theorem 4.8 of [10], if a group G is solvable and N is normal such that G/N is Abelian, then with O(log |G|)
copies of |N ⟩ it is possible to obtain a unique encoding for G/N . To prepare the state |N ⟩, Theorem 4.3 of [10]
can be used which says that if G has a unique encoding and a subgroup H ≤ G is solvable, then |H⟩ can be
prepared efficiently with negligible error. These facts will be enough for the cases we cover in our work.
     Let ℓ denote the encoding length of a group element. We measure the running time in ℓ, and assume that
ℓ = poly(log |G|). The black-box oracles can also be implemented as unitaries [24]. In this work we assume
access to such oracles.

3.3     The hidden subgroup problem with scalar action
In this section we consider the hidden subgroup problem in G = A ⋊φ Zpk , where A is a finite Abelian group
and the action of Zpk on A corresponds to scalar multiplication (in a sense defined below). We show that HSP
in this family reduces to the Hidden Multiple Shifts (HMS) problem of [16]. We give a descriptions of the HMS
Section 3.3.1. The resulting algorithm is efficient when the generator rank of A is bounded and the exponent of
A is not much larger than p. The algorithm is correct for every finite Abelian group A.

3.3.1   The hidden multiple shifts problem
We give briefly review the Hidden Multiple Shift (HMS) problem as described in [16]. The input to this problem
is an oracle fs : Znq × R → S, for some s ∈ Znq , R ⊆ Zq with q > 1 an integer and S is a set of labels (like in
the HSP). This oracle has the property that fs (x, h) = f (x − hs) for x ∈ Znq and h ∈ R and for some injective
function f : Znq → S. The set R can be thought of as labeling the shifts of the function f . The output of the
HMS problem is the shift s ∈ Znq .
    As noted in [16], when q is a composite number, there is an ambiguity in the solution to the HMS problem,
due to the fact that the oracle only shows the shifted functions indexed by h ∈ R. If all those h ∈ R are
congruent modulo some divisor of q, then changing s in certain specific directions just translates the unknown
function f such that the oracle cannot detect the change.
    For completeness, we give more details regarding this ambiguity in the solution of the HMS problem, but the
original explanation can be found in [16]. Let δ(q, R) be the largest divisor of q such that δ(q, R) divides h − h′
for all h, h′ ∈ R, i.e., define it as                                     
                                      δ(q, R) := gcd q, {h − h0 : h ∈ R} .
Equivalently, if h0 ∈ R is fixed, then δ = δ(q, R) is the largest divisor of q such that h = h0 (mod δ) for all
h ∈ R. Let s′ ∈ Nδ Znq and write s′ = qδ y. Moreover, h − h0 = δah for some ah ∈ Zq . Then, we have that
(h − h0 )s′ = qah y = 0 (mod q) for every h ∈ R. Therefore, we have that
                                         fs+s′ (x, h) = f (x − h(s + s′ )),                                   (27)
                                                     = f (x − hs − h0 s′ ),                                   (28)
                                                     = f ′ (x − hs),                                          (29)
where in the last line we have defined the injective function f ′ (x) = f (x − h0 s′ ). Therefore, the oracle with
hidden shift s and function f ′ cannot be distinguished from the hidden shift s + s′ with function f . Given the
preceding discussion, we define the HMS problem below.

4     Polynomial-time algorithm for quasi-Hamiltonian groups
In this section we describe an efficient quantum algorithm for groups which have all their subgroups as permutable.
As mentioned in the introduction, this is a generalization of Hamiltonian and Abelian groups. By Proposition 13,
any finite quasi-Hamiltonian group can be decomposed into modular p-groups. There are already efficient
algorithms for the HSP when P is Abelian and when P is Hamiltonian as in case (1) of Proposition 14. Therefore,
we give an algorithm for the non-Hamiltonian case (2). Before describing the algorithm, we give a description of
the input model.

Input model. Throughout this section, we assume that finite quasi-Hamiltonian group G is given by a
description constructed from its Sylow decomposition. More precisely, the input is assumed to be already
factorized into the Sylow factors, i.e., the group G is decomposed as
                                                         Y
                                                   G=       Gp ,
                                                          p||G|

where each Gp is the Sylow p-subgroup of G. Since G is quasi-Hamiltonian, each Gp corresponds to one of the
cases in Proposition 14. Each is described by an encoding of the generators according to the structure of the
factor. In particular, for every non-Hamiltonian factor P = Gp , the input additionally specifies the data

                                     P = A⟨b⟩,          A ⊴ P,        P/A = ⟨bA⟩,

together with the parameters describing the action of b on A and the generators of A. Moreover, we assume
that it is known how b acts on the group A. This means that in case (2) of Proposition 14 we know the value
of p and s. This is a promise in the structure of the input. It does not restrict the class of quasi-Hamiltonian
groups under consideration, but it supplies additional computational information beyond an arbitrary black-box
description of G. By Proposition 13, every finite quasi-Hamiltonian group admits a direct product decomposition
into modular Sylow p-subgroups, and Proposition 14 gives the corresponding structure of each factor. Since
the ambient group is promised to be quasi-Hamiltonian, we choose to represent it in a form which makes this
promised structure explicit.
    The Sylow decomposition also reduces the HSP on G to the HSP on its factors. Let F : G → S hide a
subgroup H ≤ G, and define Hp := H ∩ Gp . The restriction of F to Gp hides Hp , since for x, y ∈ Gp ,

A.3     Comparison with bounded nilpotency class
In [15] it is shown that there is an efficient quantum algorithm for groups of nilpotency class 2. These groups
have derived subgroup contained in the center, i.e., if G is a group of nilpotency class 2 then [G, G] ≤ Z(G). In
a later work [18], it has been shown that the HSP can be solved efficiently for groups with nilpotency class and
prime factors of the group order bounded by constants. We show below that the groups studied in this paper
have unbounded nilpotency class.
