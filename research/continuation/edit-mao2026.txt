                                                                                               Abstract
                                                   We present novel randomized approximation schemes for the Edit Distance (ED) problem and the
                                               Longest Common Subsequence (LCS) problem that, for any constant ε > 0, compute a (1+ε)-approximation
                                                                                                               Ω(1)
                                               for ED and a (1 − ε)-approximation for LCS in time n2 /2log (n) for two strings of total length at most
                                               n. This running time improves upon the classical quadratic-time dynamic programming algorithms by a
                                               quasi-polynomial factor.
                                                   Our results yield significant insights into fine-grained complexity: Firstly, for ED, prior work indicates
                                               that any exact algorithm cannot be improved beyond a few logarithmic factors without refuting established
                                               complexity assumptions [Abboud, Hansen, Vassilevska Williams, Williams, 2016]; our quasi-polynomial
                                               speed-up shows a separation the complexity of approximate ED from that of exact ED, even for approxi-
                                               mation factor arbitrarily close to 1. Secondly, for LCS, obtaining similar approximation-time tradeoffs via
                                               deterministic algorithms would imply breakthrough circuit lower bounds [Chen, Goldwasser, Lyu, Roth-
                                               blum, Rubinstein, 2019]; our randomized algorithm demonstrates derandomization hardness for LCS
                                               approximation.

1       Introduction
The edit distance (ED) between two strings X and Y , henceforth denoted ED(X, Y ), is the minimum num-
ber of character insertions, deletions, and substitutions required to transform X to Y . The complexity of
edit distance and its maximization counterpart1 , longest common subsequence (LCS), is a long-standing,
central problem in algorithm design, with important applications, especially in bioinformatics. The simple
quadratic-time dynamic programming solution is core material for undergraduate algorithms; this running
time has been improved by one or two log factors (depending on the exact access model) [MP80, Gra16].
Despite extensive research for over half a century2 , no better algorithms for exact ED or LCS are known.
    Indeed, in one of the flagship successes of fine-grained complexity, [BI18] showed that there are no
exact algorithms for Edit Distance running in “truly subquadratic” time, namely it is impossible to improve
by nδ for any constant δ > 0, assuming fine-grained complexity hypothesis SETH (see also e.g., [AWW14,
ABW15, BK15]). Furthermore [AHWW16, AB18] showed that improving even by a few log factors would
refute other fine-grained complexity assumptions and imply new circuit complexity lower bounds.
    Given the limitations of exact algorithms, and especially in light of fine-grained hardness results, ap-
proximation algorithms for Edit Distance have been extensively studied: A sequence of earlier works gave
super-constant-factor approximation factors in n1+o(1) time [ADG+ 03, BEK+ 03, BJKK04, BES06, OR07,
AK10, AN10, AKO10, AO12]. More recently, constant-factor approximation algorithms times were ob-
tained by [CDG+ 20, BEG+ 21, And20, BR20, GRS20, KS20b, AN20]. The current state of the art has
several algorithms with different trade-offs between running time and approximation (see Table 1). While
we cannot cover all the techniques used in previous work on approximate edit distance, at a high level we
seem to have hit a fundamental obstacle: all known constant-factor approximation algorithms for ED rely
on a triangle inequality technique that inherently incurs at least a 3-factor in the approximation [Rub18]. No
better-than-3-approximation algorithms are known with any non-trivial running time.
    Remarkably, good multiplicative approximations for LCS are even more scarce than ED: despite sig-
nificant attention (e.g., [HSSS19, RS20, AV21, RSSS25, BCD23, HL23]), for worst-case instances the best
previous known approximation factor is just barely sub-polynomial [Nos21]. Furthermore, [CGL+ 19] shows
that obtaining such approximation-time tradeoffs for approximate LCS with deterministic algorithms would
imply new breakthrough circuit lower bounds3 .

       • Algorithmic improvement: The most obvious achievement of our main result is a quasi-polynomial
         improvement in running time. While the quantitative improvement is modest, in the field of fine-
         grained complexity quasi-polynomial [Wil18, LW17, AFK+ 24] and even smaller improvements (e.g.,
         [GMW19, JX24, DMM+ 25]) are often celebrated as significant steps towards pinpointing the com-

         importantly, perhaps, this improvement shows the algorithmic potential of our new structural insights
         (see discussion below on “tree total deviation”).

       • Separation between (1 + ε)-Approximate and Exact Computation (both ED and LCS): For exact
         ED and LCS, prior work [AHWW16, AB18] shows that even shaving off a few logarithmic factors
         is highly unlikely without refuting established fine-grained complexity assumptions. Although a se-
         quence of approximation algorithms faster than this lower bound have recently been known [CDG+ 20,
         BEG+ 21, And20, BR20, GRS20, KS20b, AN20], none of them achieve an approximation factor better
         than 3. Therefore, subject to certain circuit complexity hardness assumptions (see [AHWW16, AB18]
         for details), our result is the first to separate the complexity of (1 + ε)-approximation from exact com-
         putation of ED and LCS.

       • Separation Between Randomized and Deterministic Algorithms (LCS): For LCS, obtaining sim-
         ilar approximation-time tradeoffs using deterministic algorithms would imply breakthrough circuit
         lower bounds [CGL+ 19] (see also [AB17, AR18]). Indeed, many randomized approximation algo-
         rithms for both LCS and ED have been proposed, but it was not clear whether this randomization
         was necessary. Our algorithm is the first to break the frontier of deterministic lower bounds by using
         randomization, thereby demonstrating a fundamental separation: subject to certain circuit complexity
         hardness assumptions (see [CGL+ 19]), randomized algorithms for approximate LCS are strictly faster
         than their deterministic counterparts.

Additional related Work
In addition to aforementioned works on approximate ED and LCS on worst case inputs, many works also give
improved guarantees under assumptions on the inputs such as small edit distance4 [CGK16, GKS19, KS20a,
BCR20, BCFN22a, BCFN22c, GKKS22, KS23, RSSS25], semi-random strings [AK12, Kus19, BSS20],
or edit distance to error correcting codewords [HRS19]. See also e.g., [KR09, CGKK18, HSS19, BGS21,
KPS21, CFH+ 21, JNW21, BCFN22b, KS24, BCFK24] for additional related works in other models.
    Ideas related to the breakthrough constant factor approximation algorithm for edit distance have also
been applied to related problems, such as tree edit distance [SS22] and, more recently, Frechet distance
[CHZ25]. For tree edit distance, we leave it for future work whether our techniques can also apply to improve
[BGHS19]’s state of the art (nearly) quadratic running time for (1 + ε)-approximation. For the Frechet
distance, existing hardness of approximation results rule out polynomial savings assuming SETH [Bri14],
and it remains open whether quasi-polynomial saving is possible.
    Our work gives a quasi-polynomial improvement in running times for central problems of interest in
fine-grained complexity. Other notable examples of fine-grained complexity with quasi-polynomial improve-
ments include All-Pairs Shortest Paths [Wil18] (equivalent to the Min-plus matrix multiplication), online
matrix-vector multiplication [LW17], and combinatorial boolean matrix multiplication [AFK+ 24].

     Before we describe our main techniques for ED and LCS, we begin with a simple observation about sums
of sequences and their sub-sequences (Section 2.1). This observation plays a crucial role in our algorithm,
and analogous results have been applied to other areas of research, (see, for example, [Dru13]5 , [Pen25]).
We then setup the notation with a standard reduction to optimal path problems on a 2D grid (Section 2.2).

2.2    Setup and Notation (a Fairly Standard Reduction to Shortest/Longest Path on a 2D Grid)
We reduce ED to a somewhat more general problem of finding the shortest path on a 2D grid6 with diagonal
short-cuts (Definition 5.1 or Fig. 1), where the weight of going from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u, v + 1⟩⟩ or ⟨⟨u + 1, v⟩⟩ is
always 1 (corresponding to insertion or deletion), and the weight of a short-cut edge from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u +
1, v + 1⟩⟩ is either 0 (corresponding to a match) or 1 (corresponding to a substitution). The goal is to find the
length (sum of weights) of the shortest path from ⟨⟨0, 0⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨n, n⟩⟩. This shortest path problem corresponds
to the standard DP algorithm for ED.
     Similarly, we reduce LCS to a somewhat more general problem of finding the longest path on a 2D grid
with diagonal short-cuts (Definition 5.2), where the weight of going from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u, v + 1⟩⟩ or ⟨⟨u + 1, v⟩⟩
is always 0, and the weight of a short-cut edge from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u + 1, v + 1⟩⟩ is either 0 (no match) or 1
(match). The goal is to find the length of the longest path from ⟨⟨0, 0⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨n, n⟩⟩. This longest path problem
corresponds to the standard DP algorithm for LCS.
     We now use a 45°-rotated 2−poly log n "system (see Fig. 2) to describe 2D grids with the transformation
⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ → ⟨u + v, v − u + n⟩ (Definition 5.3). We use ⟨x, y⟩ when referring to the rotated coordinated
system and refer to the x- and y-coordinates defined with respect to the first and second entries of ⟨x, y⟩. In
this notation, we are looking for the shortest/longest path from ⟨0, n⟩ to ⟨2n, n⟩. We use column to refer to a
subset ⟨x, ·⟩ of the vertices, and row for ⟨·, y⟩. It is easy to see that any ⟨0, n⟩-to-⟨2n, n⟩ path visits exactly
one vertex of each column. A path p in this notation can be given as a function p : {0, . . . .2n} → {0, . . . .2n}
mapping each x-coordinate to the corresponding y-coordinate. Importantly, such a rotation transform ensures
that adjacent vertices the path visits differ in their rows by at most 1 (i.e., |p(i) − p(i − 1)| ≤ 1), which will
be crucial to bounding the curvature of a path.

2.4      Multiplicative Approximations
To obtain multiplicative (1 ± o(1))-factor approximations for both ED and LCS, we need to introduce a few
additional ideas. Here also the differences between ED and LCS become more important.

Challenge 1 (LCS): vertical difference could be much larger than weight of the path For ED, we use
the fact that the vertical difference |p(l) − p(r)| is at most the length of the ⟨l, p(l)⟩ − ⟨r, p(r)⟩ path to get a
bound on the curvature of p inside the interval (l, r]. For LCS, the vertical difference is no longer bounded
by the weight of the path so locally the curvature could generally be much higher than the contribution to
LCS.
    Intuitively, we can simply discard intervals with very high curvature compared to low contribution to
the LCS. To bound the total error incurred by discarding such intervals, we introduce a scaled variant of the
tree total deviation (Section 4.2). We use it to show that indeed most of the contribution to LCS comes from
segments of the path where the ratio between curvature and weight is bounded, so we can safely discard the
remaining segments.

Challenge 2 (ED+LCS): uneven contribution from sub-paths For a fixed candidate path, if we simply
split it into M parts with equal widths, sample a random subset of M/2 parts, and let our estimate be twice
the answer in the sample, one would hope that the estimate would concentrate around the true sum. However,
this concentration may not hold when the sub-paths on different parts have very different weights; e.g., in the
extreme case, the sub-path in one of the M parts has a much higher weight than the rest combined, and the
estimate would fluctuate greatly based on whether this part is in the sample or not.
    Fortunately, if we apply the total tree deviation result on the “weight distribution” of the path, we can
also see that on average, the sub-paths should not differ too much in lengths. It turns out that it suffices to
obtain an estimate that satisfies the following desiderata:

       • The estimate is “(nearly) sound”, i.e., it does not underestimate the answer for ED (by less than (1 −
         o(1))), and does not overstimate the answer for LCS (by more than (1 + o(1))).

       • As long as the sub-paths do not differ too much in lengths, the estimate is also “(nearly) complete” ,
         i.e., it does not overestimate the answer for ED (by more than (1 + o(1))), and does not underestimate
         the answer for LCS (by less than (1 − o(1))).

       • The estimate also has “back-up” worst-case error guarantee, i.e., even if the sub-paths lengths vary
         greatly, we incur an additive error of at most O(1) times the length of the path.

3.4        Rounding
To avoid fractional results after divisions, for λ = 2⌊0.99 log(n)⌋ = Θ(n0.99 ), we ensure that the lengths of
two input strings are multiples of λ by padding them with arbitrary characters. The error we introduce with
this rounding is O(λ), which is within (1 + o(1)) when the ED/LCS is ω(λ). When the ED/LCS is O(λ),
we can use Lemma 3.3 or Lemma 3.4 to compute the answer exactly in O(nλ) = O(n1.99 ) time. Thus we
can assume that input strings both have a length that is a multiple of a power of 2 of order Ω(n0.99 ).

5     Shortest Path Problems on Grids
5.1     Introduction
5.1.1    Grid Graph for LCS and Edit Distance
Given two strings X, Y of length nX and nY , we define an edge-weighted graph, corresponding to the cost of
moving between states in the standard quadratic-time DP algorithm for ED (closely related to the alignment
graph defined in e.g., [CKW23]):

                                                                  19
Definition 5.1 (Edit Distance Grid Graph). The Edit Distance Grid Graph is a grid graph with diagonal
shortcuts that can be used to compute ED(X, Y ), and is defined as follows:

Vertices The grid points of [0, nX ] × [0, nY ]. The vertex at the intersection of the x-th column and the y-th
      row is ⟨⟨x, y⟩⟩;

Edges The set of edges includes standard grid edges and diagonal shortcut edges:

         • Standard grid edges from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u, v+1⟩⟩ or ⟨⟨u+1, v⟩⟩, always with weight 1 (corresponding
           to insertion or deletion);
         • Diagonal shortcut edges from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u+1, v+1⟩⟩, whose weight is 0 if X[u] = Y [v] (match)
           and 1 otherwise (substitution).

Objective ED(X, Y ) is the length (sum of weights) of the shortest (0, 0)-(nX , nY ) path.

    Given two strings X, Y of length nX and nY , we define an edge-weighted graph, corresponding to the
profit of moving between states in the standard quadratic-time DP algorithm for LCS:

Definition 5.2 (LCS Grid Graph). The LCS Grid Graph is a grid graph with diagonal shortcuts that can be
used to compute LCS(X, Y ), and is defined as follows:

Vertices The grid points of [0, nX ] × [0, nY ]. The vertex at the intersection of the x-th column and the y-th
      row is ⟨⟨x, y⟩⟩;

Edges The set of edges includes standard grid edges and diagonal shortcut edges:

         • Standard grid edges from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u, v+1⟩⟩ or ⟨⟨u+1, v⟩⟩, always with weight 0 (corresponding
           to no match between the two characters);
         • Diagonal shortcut edges from ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ to ⟨⟨u+1, v+1⟩⟩, whose weight is 1 if X[u] = Y [v] (match)
           and 0 otherwise (no match).

Objective LCS(X, Y ) is the length (sum of weights) of the longest (0, 0)-(nX , nY ) path.

5.1.2   Rotated Grid Graph: ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ → ⟨u + v, v − u + nX ⟩
To describe our algorithm, we will rotate the grids by 45 degrees and obtain graphs where for any fixed path,
each column (corresponding to a diagonal in the original grid) is visited exactly once.

Definition 5.3 (Rotated Edit Distance Grid Graph). We use ⟨⟨x, y⟩⟩ to denote a vertex in the (original) Edit
Distance Grid Graph at the intersection of column x and row y and ⟨x, y⟩ to denote a vertex in the Rotated
Edit Distance Grid Graph at the intersection of column x and row y. The description of the new graph on
the rotated grid is as follows:

Vertices The vertex for ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ in the original grid corresponds to ⟨u + v, v − u + nX ⟩ on the new grid;

Edges We start with the same set of edges as in the original graph. Then, we divide each shortcut edge with
     weight w (∈ {0, 1}) from ⟨u, v⟩ to ⟨u + 2, v⟩ into two edges, one from ⟨u, v⟩ to ⟨u + 1, v⟩ with weight
     0 and the other from ⟨u + 1, v⟩ to ⟨u + 2, v⟩ with weight w.

Objective The length of the shortest ⟨0, nX ⟩-⟨nX + nY , nY ⟩ path equals ED(X, Y ).


                                                      20
Definition 5.4 (Rotated LCS Grid Graph). We use ⟨⟨x, y⟩⟩ to denote a vertex in the (original) LCS Grid Graph
at the intersection of column x and row y and ⟨x, y⟩ to denote a vertex in the Rotated LCS Grid Graph at the
intersection of column x and row y. The description of the new graph on the rotated grid is as follows:

Vertices The vertex for ⟨⟨u, v⟩⟩ in the original grid corresponds to ⟨u + v, v − u + nX ⟩ on the new grid;

Edges We start with the same set of edges as in the original graph. Then, we divide each shortcut edge with
     weight w (∈ {0, 1}) from ⟨u, v⟩ to ⟨u + 2, v⟩ into two edges, one from ⟨u, v⟩ to ⟨u + 1, v⟩ with weight
     0 and the other from ⟨u + 1, v⟩ to ⟨u + 2, v⟩ with weight w.

Objective The length of the longest path from ⟨0, nX ⟩ to ⟨nX + nY , nY ⟩ equals LCS(X, Y ).

      For the remainder of the paper, a path refers to a route from any ⟨l, yl ⟩ to any ⟨r, yr ⟩. Due to the split
edges, any path p in the grid graph from any column l to any column r visits each column x ∈ [l, r] exactly
once. We denote by p(x) the y-coordinate (row) of the vertex ⟨x, p(x)⟩ where the path crosses column x.
By the construction of the rotated grid graphs, |p(x) − p(x − 1)| ≤ 1 for every l < x ≤ r. For an interval
(l′ , r′ ] ⊂ (l, r], we write p(l′ , r′ ] to denote the sub-path of p from l′ to r′ . We define w(p) as the length (total
weight of edges) of p.
      We have the following observation:

Observation 5.5. For a path p, and for any two columns i ≤ j that the path visits,

                                                    |p(i) − p(j)| ≤ j − i.

Proof. We prove this by induction by increasing value of j − i. When i = j, the claim is trivially true since
the path visits each column exactly once. Now suppose that the claim holds for i, j − 1. The only edges from
⟨j − 1, p(j − 1)⟩ go to ⟨j, p(j − 1)⟩, and (possibly) ⟨j, p(j − 1) ± 1⟩. Therefore,

                         |p(i) − p(j)| ≤ |p(i) − p(j − 1)| + 1 ≤ (j − 1 − i) + 1 = j − i.



   Due to Observation 5.5, given two vertices ⟨l, yl ⟩ and ⟨r, yr ⟩, if ⟨r, yr ⟩ is reachable from ⟨l, yl ⟩, we must
have |yl − yr | ≤ r − l.
