Moreover, the resulting ciphertext can be further computed with ciphertexts under other keys. In 2016, Peikert and Shiehian presented a dynamic MFHE scheme that relies on using a Common Reference String (CRS) and circular security assumptions. Subsequently, various variants of dynamic MFHE schemes were proposed based on this scheme, but these variant schemes still rely on a CRS and circular security assumption. Therefore, constructing a MFHE GSW scheme relies solely on the LWE assumption and does not require a CRS remains a valuable challenge. This paper introduces a new dynamic MFHE scheme. Compared to existing solutions, our scheme does not require the circular security assumption and depends solely on the LWE assumption, which theoretically provides an enhanced level of security. Additionally, it eliminates the need for a shared CRS but instead replaces the CRS with each party independently generate random matrices. This decentralized approach enhances the ability of users to generate keys independently. The size of ciphertexts and public keys in our scheme is comparable to other existing solutions, thus maintaining efficiency in both computation and ciphertext extension. Associative Rings and Algebras Commutative Rings and Algebras Computational Number Theory Dynamic Combinatorial Chemistry Multilinear Algebra Cryptology Attribute-Based Encryption in Cloud Computing Security Introduction Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) is a highly potential encryption method. This technology allows unauthorized third parties to compute on ciphertexts without requiring the data of the ciphertext to be revealed. Gentry presented the first FHE scheme in 2009 (Gentry 2009 ), marking a groundbreaking advancement in the field. Following this, several improved variants were developed, leading to rapid progress in FHE research. In 2012, Lopez-Alt, Tromer, and Vaikuntanathan ( 2012 ) introduced the LTV12, which was the first to realize MFHE. This innovation addressed the limitations of traditional FHE in the context of multiparty computation, further expanding the application scenarios of homomorphic encryption by allowing homomorphic operations on ciphertexts under different keys. This indicates that multiple participants are able to independently encrypt their data and perform homomorphic computations without sharing their private keys. Moreover, the resulting ciphertext after computation can only be decrypted if all participants provide their respective keys, thereby ensuring data privacy and security. In secure multiparty computation, each party encrypts its data and interacts with the data of other parties through a carefully designed protocol, ensuring the security of the data during the entire process. During the entire computation, no party can access the private data of others and can only access the computation result after the computation is finished. Secure two-party computation was first introduced by Yao in 1982. This idea was later extended to encompass secure multiparty computation, enabling multiple parties to ensure data privacy in more complex applications. Multi-key FHE is an effective means of constructing MPC protocols. In LTV12, the concept of on-the-fly MPC was introduced, where the computation function and participants can be dynamically selected in an untrusted cloud environment. This allows participants to engage in computation without determining the number of parties beforehand, and they can go offline once they have provided their encrypted data. The server then performs homomorphic operations on the ciphertexts from each participant, and finally, the participants can decrypt the ciphertext by executing an interactive protocol. This approach reduces the interaction complexity between participants and enables independent computation during the online and offline phases. In CM15 (Clear et al. 2015 ), a MFHE scheme based on an LWE problem and a GSW (Gentry et al. 2013 ) variant was constructed, and MW16 built on this foundation by proposing a MFHE scheme using a CRS and constructing a two-round MPC protocol (Mukherjee et al. 2015 ). However, both of these works and their variants are static schemes, meaning that the number of participants and keys must be predetermined before the computation begins. In such static settings, the ciphertext generated under the predetermined keys cannot be further computed with ciphertexts under additional keys, unless the entire computation is restarted. This static nature limits the flexibility of users during the computation process, as participants cannot dynamically adjust based on real-time needs. On the contrary, in a dynamic fully homomorphic encryption scheme, the number of participants or keys does not need to be determined before computation. It allows ciphertexts to be extended to additional keys, and the resulting ciphertexts from computations under multiple keys can be further computed with ciphertexts under other additional keys. Therefore, dynamic FHE schemes better meet the needs of multiparty computation applications. Significant research has been done on dynamic MFHE schemes. BP16 constructed a dynamic MFHE based on LWE (Brakerski et al. 2016 ), supporting an unlimited number of homomorphic operations and participants. In this scheme, the size of the ciphertext increases linearly with the number of participants. However, expensive bootstrapping operations are required during every homomorphic multiplication/NAND operation and when extending the ciphertexts. This scheme relies on a CRS and the circular security assumption to ensure its security. Subsequently, PS16 proposed two (leveled) dynamic MFHE schemes (Peikert et al. 2016 ). The first scheme only requires the LWE assumption, but its extended ciphertexts are relatively large, and homomorphic operations are slower. In practice, homomorphic operations are more frequent than ciphertext extensions, making this scheme less efficient overall. The second scheme introduces the circular security assumption, where the ciphertexts are consistent with standard GSW ciphertexts, requiring no additional components, and allowing for proper homomorphic operations. However, the public key is large because it includes the information necessary for ciphertext extension. Although the ciphertext extension operation is slower, the overall efficiency is better than that of the first scheme. It is important to note that both schemes in PS16 require a CRS. Since these schemes are built on a CRS, a trusted setup is needed to ensure all participants have the same CRS, which diminishes the ability of participants to independently generate keys. Accordingly, constructing a dynamic MFHE scheme without a CRS is essential. Recently, Biswas and Dutta ( 2022 ) proposed a scheme that claims to not require a CRS and does not require the circular security assumption. However, the public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = {\mathbf{R}} + \left( {{\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right)\) in this scheme encrypts the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) with only a binary matrix \({\mathbf{R}}\) , not using \({\mathbf{AR}}\) , so it cannot rely on the LWE assumption for security. As a result, the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) is highly vulnerable to leakage, and the security of the encryption process cannot be ensured. Moreover, when decrypting an extended ciphertext, the scheme does not require additional new keys, which is not suitable for real-world scenarios. Shortly afterward, Chen et al. ( 2021 ), based on the second scheme in PS16, proposed a (leveled) dynamic MFHE scheme under asymmetric key settings, where ciphertext extension is distributed, and claimed that it does not require CRS and the circular security assumption. However, in reality, a CRS is still used during the setup phase, and since the public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = \left( {{{\varvec{\upomega}}} \otimes {\mathbf{AR}}} \right) + \left( {{\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right)\) hides the secret key \({\mathbf{t}}\) by using the public key \({\mathbf{A}}\) , the circular security problem still exists. Our contribution To address the above issues, we propose the first truly dynamic multi-key GSW scheme that does not require a CRS or the circular security assumption. The CRS serves to link ciphertexts encrypted under different keys during ciphertext extension, thereby ensuring the correctness of the scheme. In MW16 and PS16, the CRS must be generated and shared among all participants before the \({\text{KeyGen}}\) phase. This requirement either necessitates a trusted \({\text{Setup}}\) or an interactive distributed CRS generation protocol, both of which reduce the practicality and decentralization of the scheme. In our scheme, however, there is no need to generate a shared CRS during the \({\text{Setup}}\) phase. Instead, each participant independently generates a random matrix during the \({\text{KeyGen}}\) phase, without any coordination with others. This design results in a more decentralized key generation process, allowing each user to autonomously generate their key material. Consequently, the scheme enhances user independence and eliminates the need for any trusted third party or \({\text{Setup}}\) . Furthermore, our scheme avoids encrypting private keys with public keys, thereby eliminating the need for the circular security assumption to guarantee the scheme’s security. This not only theoretically enhances the security of the scheme but also avoids the potential risks associated with circular security assumptions. Additionally, we ensure the security of the keys, addressing the risk of leakage. Decrypting extended ciphertexts requires all participants’ keys, preventing scenarios where decryption can occur without the involvement of additional key holders. In our scheme, parties need to send their public keys to others during key extension, which introduces additional communication overhead. However, in current MFHE schemes, the primary cost still lies in ciphertext computation. Therefore, the extra communication overhead in our scheme is negligible. Our scheme is based on inspiration from PS16 and the scheme presented by Kim et al. ( 2018 ). Reviewing related work, the second scheme in PS16 generates a CRS during the \({\text{Setup}}\) phase to encrypt the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) , while Chen et al. ( 2021 ) opts to use the public key to encrypt \({\mathbf{t}}\) . As a result, the scheme presented by Chen et al. ( 2021 ) has a circular security problem. In our scheme, there is no need to generate a shared CRS for all participants during the \({\text{Setup}}\) phase. Instead, participant \(P_{i}\) independently generates a random matrix \({\mathbf{D}}_{i}\) during the \({\text{KeyGen}}\) phase and uses this matrix \({\mathbf{D}}_{i}\) to encrypt the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) , thereby avoiding circular security issues. However, during the ciphertext extension process, noise in the form of \({\mathbf{t}}_{j} {\mathbf{D}}_{i} {\mathbf{R}}_{i}\) is introduced. Since \({\mathbf{D}}_{i}\) is not a common reference string, we cannot use the GSW. Lcomb algorithm to eliminate this noise. Therefore, we chose to apply the LinkAlgo algorithm from the scheme presented by Kim et al., which links \({\mathbf{t}}_{j}\) and \({\mathbf{D}}_{i}\) . We partially modified the dimensions in the LinkAlgo algorithm and applied the modified algorithm to effectively eliminate the noise. Our public key consists of three components: \({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{D}}\) where \({\mathbf{A}}\) is used for encrypting information, \({\mathbf{P}}\) is a public extension key, and the random matrix \({\mathbf{D}}\) is used to hide the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) . Additionally, our public key size is smaller than that in PS16 and comparable to the scheme presented by Kim et al. ( 2018 ), while the ciphertext size remains the same as in PS16 and the scheme presented by Kim et al. ( 2018 ). Furthermore, our scheme is also applicable for dynamic multiparty computation. By using bootstrapping operation, it can supports an unlimited number of homomorphic evaluations with any polynomial number of keys (Table 1 ). \({\mathbf{D^{\prime}}}_{l}^{row}\) denotes the \(l - th\) row of \({\mathbf{D^{\prime}}}\) and \({\mathbf{R}}_{l}^{col}\) denotes the \(l - th\) column of \({\mathbf{R}}\) . Therefore, $${\mathbf{tZ = t}}\sum\limits_{u,v} {{\mathbf{R}}\left[ {u,v} \right]{\mathbf{L}}_{u,v} + \sum\limits_{u,v} {{\mathbf{e^{\prime}}}_{u,v} } } = {\mathbf{tD^{\prime}R}} + {\mathbf{e}},$$ where \(\left\| {{\mathbf{e}}_{u,v} } \right\|_{\infty } \le \beta\) , \({\mathbf{e^{\prime}}}_{u,v} = {\mathbf{e}}_{u,v} {\mathbf{G}}^{ - 1} \left( {{\mathbf{L}}_{u,v} } \right)\) has norm \(\left\| {{\mathbf{e^{\prime}}}_{u,v} } \right\|_{\infty } \le nl\beta\) , and \({\mathbf{e}} = \sum\limits_{u = 1}^{m} {\sum\limits_{v = 1}^{{n^{2} l}} {{\mathbf{e^{\prime}}}_{u,v} } }\) has norm \(\left\| {\mathbf{e}} \right\|_{\infty } \le mn^{3} l^{2} \beta\) . Our dynamic MFHE scheme Construction of dynamic MFHE scheme \({\text{params }} \leftarrow {\text{Setup}}(1^{\lambda } ,1^{L} )\) : The input consists of a given security parameter \(\lambda\) , the maximum evaluated depth \(L\) , it chooses a dimension \(n = n(\lambda ,L)\) , a \(B_{\chi } - bounded\) discrete Gaussian distribution \(\chi = \chi (\lambda ,L)\) with parameter \(2\sqrt n\) , and a modulus \(q\) . Details on how to instantiate the modulus \(q\) will be provided later. Let parameter \(m = 2nl\) , where \(l = \left\lceil {\log q} \right\rceil\) . This algorithm returns \({\text{params}} = (n,m,\chi ,q,B_{\chi } )\) . It is important to emphasize that \({\text{params}}\) are implicitly used as inputs in other algorithms, although it is not explicitly stated. \({\text{KeyGen(params)}}\) : Perform the following steps to generate the secret key \({\mathbf{t}}\) and the public key \(({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{D}})\) . 1. Sample \({\overline{\mathbf{t}}} \leftarrow \chi^{n - 1}\) and define \(sk = {\mathbf{t}} = ( - {\overline{\mathbf{t}}},1) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) . Sample \({\mathbf{e}} \leftarrow \chi^{m}\) and \({\mathbf{B}} \leftarrow {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{(n - 1) \times m}\) . Set \(b = \overline{t}B + e \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{m}\) . Then define \({\text{A = }}\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\text{B}} \\ {\text{b}} \\ \end{array} } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times m}\) . We can find that \({\mathbf{tA}} = {\mathbf{e}}\) , and this is due to \({\mathbf{tA}} = ( - {\overline{\mathbf{t}}},1)\left( {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\mathbf{B}} \\ {\mathbf{b}} \\ \end{array} } \right) = - {\overline{\mathbf{t}}\mathbf{B}} + {\mathbf{b}} = {\mathbf{e}}\) . 2. Choose a random matrix \({\mathbf{D}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times m}\) , a short random matrix \({\mathbf{R}} \leftarrow \left\{ {0,1} \right\}^{{m \times n^{2} l}}\) and define public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = {\mathbf{DR}} + ({\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) . Finally, it returns the public key \(pk = ({\mathbf{A}},{\mathbf{P}},{\mathbf{D}})\) . \({\text{Encrypt}}(\mu ,pk)\) : Sample a uniform matrix \({\mathbf{M}} \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}^{m \times nl}\) . Then encrypt a message \(\mu \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}\) under the \(pk\) as \({\mathbf{C}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) , where $${\mathbf{C = AM}} + \mu {\mathbf{G}}.$$ Obvious that the ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}\) is generated by encrypting \(\mu\) using \({\mathbf{A}}\) under a secret key \({\mathbf{t}}\) . Because \({\mathbf{tA}} = {\mathbf{e}}\) and \(\left\| {\mathbf{e}} \right\|_{\infty } \le B_{\chi }\) , \({\mathbf{tC}} = {\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathbf{C}}} + \mu {\mathbf{tG}} = {\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathbf{C}}} + \mu \left( {{\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right)\) where \({\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathbf{C}}} {\mathbf{ = eM}}\) such that \(\left\| {{\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathbf{C}}} } \right\|_{\infty } \le mB_{\chi }\) . Hence \({\mathbf{C}}\) is the ciphertext of \(\mu\) with noise \(mB_{\chi }\) under the secret key \({\mathbf{t}}\) . And we let \(\beta = mB_{\chi }\) . \({\text{Extend}}(\left( {pk_{1} , \ldots ,pk_{k} } \right),pk^{*} ,C)\) : The ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) is the encryption of \(\mu\) under the corresponding concatenation secret key \({\mathbf{t}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}}_{1} ,{\mathbf{t}}_{2} , \ldots {\mathbf{t}}_{k} } \right)\) where \({\mathbf{t}}_{i} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) for \(i \in \left[ k \right]\) . In this algorithm, we will extend \({\mathbf{C}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) to \({\mathbf{C^{\prime}}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n(k + 1) \times n(k + 1)l}\) under an additional secret key \({\mathbf{t}}^{ * } \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) through a series of public extension keys. The result ciphertext \({\mathbf{C^{\prime}}}\) is the encryption of \(\mu\) under the new key \({\mathbf{t^{\prime}}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}},{\mathbf{t}}^{ * } } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n(k + 1)}\) . The detailed steps will be provided in the following section. \({\text{Decrypt(params}},\left( {sk_{1} , \ldots ,sk_{k} } \right),C)\) : The input is a series of secret keys \(\left( {{\mathbf{t}}_{1} = sk_{1} , \ldots ,{\mathbf{t}}_{k} = sk_{k} } \right)\) and an extended ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}\) . Let \({\mathbf{t}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}}_{1} , \ldots ,{\mathbf{t}}_{k} } \right)\) , it is apparent that \({\mathbf{C}}\) is the ciphertext corresponding to \({\mathbf{t}}\) . To decrypt the ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}\) , define \({{\varvec{\upomega}}} = \left( {0, \ldots ,0,\left\lceil {{q \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {q 2}} \right. \kern-0pt} 2}} \right\rceil } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk}\) , then compute \({\mathbf{v}} = {\mathbf{tCG}}_{k}^{ - 1} \left( {{{\varvec{\upomega}}}^{\rm T} } \right) = {\mathbf{e^{\prime}}} + \mu \left\lceil {{q \mathord{\left/ {\vphantom {q 2}} \right. \kern-0pt} 2}} \right\rceil\) such that \({\mathbf{e^{\prime}}} = \left\langle {{\mathbf{e}},{\mathbf{G}}_{k}^{ - 1} \left( {{{\varvec{\upomega}}}^{\rm T} } \right)} \right\rangle\) and \(\left\| {{\mathbf{e^{\prime}}}} \right\|_{\infty } \le km\beta\) . Finally, it output \(\left| {\left\lfloor {\frac{\text{V}}{{q/2}}} \right\rceil } \right|\) as the decrypted message. \({\text{Eval(params}},{\mathbb{C}},C_{1} , \ldots ,C_{k} )\) : The input \(k\) extended (or evaluated or fresh) ciphertexts are just GSW ciphertexts, so simply use the GSW homomorphic evaluation algorithm: \({\text{Add}}\left( {{\text{C}}_{{1}} {\text{,C}}_{{2}} } \right)\) : Output \({\mathbf{C}}_{1} + {\mathbf{C}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) . \({\text{Mult}}\left( {{\text{C}}_{{1}} {\text{,C}}_{{2}} } \right)\) : Output \({\mathbf{C}}_{1} {\mathbf{G}}^{ - 1} \left( {{\mathbf{C}}_{2} } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) . For security, in scheme PS16, \({\mathbf{P}}\) hides \({\mathbf{t}}\) using \({\mathbf{R}}\) , \({\mathbf{D}}\) is the encryption of \({\mathbf{R}}\) under \({\mathbf{t}}\) , and in the scheme presented by Chen et al. ( 2021 ), the public key is used to encrypt the secret key \({\mathbf{t}}\) , so the security of the encryption process in these schemes need the circular security assumption to be ensured. However, in our scheme, the encryption of \({\mathbf{R}}\) is not required. Meanwhile, the private key is encrypted without the public key. So we do not need the circular security assumption to guarantee security. We will provide the security analysis below. Ciphertext extension Suppose there is a ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) , which is the encryption of \(\mu\) under the secret key \({\mathbf{t}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}}_{1} , \ldots ,{\mathbf{t}}_{k} } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk}\) where \({\mathbf{t}}\) is the combination of \(k\) individual secret keys and \({\mathbf{t}}_{i} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) , namely, $${\mathbf{tC}} = {\mathbf{E}}_{{\mathbf{C}}} + \mu \left( {{\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right).$$ Now we need to extend the ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) into the new ciphertext \({\mathbf{C^{\prime}}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n(k + 1) \times n(k + 1)l}\) which encrypts \(\mu\) under the new key \({\mathbf{t^{\prime}}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}},{\mathbf{t}}^{ * } } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n(k + 1)}\) . More precisely, we want $${\mathbf{t^{\prime}C^{\prime}}} = {\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C^{\prime}}}}} + \mu \left( {{\mathbf{t^{\prime}}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right) = {\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C^{\prime}}}}} + \mu \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {{\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} & {{\mathbf{t}}^{ * } \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \\ \end{array} } \right).$$ In order to achieve this, we set the extended ciphertext as $${\mathbf{C^{\prime}}} = \left[ {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\mathbf{C}} & {\mathbf{Y}} \\ {\mathbf{O}} & {{\mathbf{Y}}^{ * } } \\ \end{array} } \right] \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n(k + 1) \times n(k + 1)l} .$$ The structure of the extended ciphertext in our scheme is similar to that of PS16, but the components are different because our scheme does not require a common reference string and also does not need the circular security assumption by introducing the \({\text{LinkAlgo}}\) algorithm in the scheme presented by Kim et al. To more clearly describe the steps of ciphertext extension in our scheme, we provide an algorithm here to explain the process in detail. In the ciphertext extension algorithm, the input parameter \({\mathbf{C}}\) is a ciphertext to be extended with an additional secret key \({\mathbf{t}}^{ * }\) . The input parameter \({\textrm{pk}}_{{1}}, {\text{... ,pk}}_{k} ,{\textrm{pk}}^{*}\) are the public keys of each party. In order to obtain the ciphertext \(\left\{ {{\mathbf{V}}_{ * ,j}^{(s,t)} } \right\}_{{s \in [m],t \in [n^{2} l]}}\) , party \(P_{j}\) for \(j \in \left[ k \right]\) must encrypt each element of the respective random matrix \({\mathbf{R}}_{j}\) using the public key \(pk^{ * }\) . Subsequently, party \(P_{j}\) executes the algorithm \({\text{Modified LinkAlgo}}\left( {\left\{ {V_{ * ,j}^{(s,t)} } \right\}_{{s \in [m],t \in [n^{2} l]}} ,pk_{j} } \right)\) , and returns a matrix \({\mathbf{Z}}_{j}\) . Party \(P^{ * }\) encrypts each element of the random matrix \({\mathbf{R}}^{ * }\) using a sequence of public keys \(\left\{ {pk_{j} } \right\}_{j \in \left[ k \right]}\) , then executes the algorithm \({\text{Modified LinkAlgo}}\left( {\left\{ {V_{j, * }^{(s,t)} } \right\}_{{s \in [m],t \in [n^{2} l]}} ,pk^{ * } } \right)\) , and returns a matrix \({\mathbf{Z}}_{j}^{ * }\) . Furthermore, it is possible for party \(P_{j}\) to be offline as long as the parameters \({\mathbf{P}}_{j}\) and \({\mathbf{Z}}_{j}\) are published, and similarly, party \(P^{ * }\) can be offline as long as the parameters \(\left\{ {{\mathbf{Z}}_{j}^{ * } } \right\}_{j \in \left[ k \right]}\) are published. Algorithm 1 Parameters setting Before the parameter setting, we must define the maximum noise growth when homomorphically evaluating a circuit of depth \(L\) . Let \({\mathbf{C}}_{1} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) and \({\mathbf{C}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk \times nkl}\) be the ciphertexts (extended or evaluated) of \(\mu_{1}\) and \(\mu_{2}\) under the secret key \({\mathbf{t}} = \left( {{\mathbf{t}}_{1} , \ldots ,{\mathbf{t}}_{k} } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{nk}\) , and satisfy \({\mathbf{tC}}_{1} = \mu_{1} \left( {{\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right) + {\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C}}_{1} }}\) and \({\mathbf{tC}}_{2} = \mu_{2} \left( {{\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}} \right) + {\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C}}_{2} }}\) with error bound \(\max \left\{ {\left\| {{\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C}}_{1} }} } \right\|_{\infty } ,\left\| {{\mathbf{E}}_{{{\mathbf{C}}_{2} }} } \right\|_{\infty } } \right\} \le {\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) . For homomorphic NAND of the two ciphertexts, we have that their error bound is \(\left( {nkl + 1} \right){\mathbf{E^{\prime}}} = poly\left( {n,k,l} \right){\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) , where \(poly\left( \cdot \right)\) represents a polynomial function. Suppose a ciphertext with the error bound within \({\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) is to be extended, then the error of the extended ciphertext is bounded by \(kmn^{7} l^{4} \beta + B_{\chi } {\mathbf{E^{\prime}}} = poly\left( {n,k,l} \right)\beta \le poly\left( {n,k,l} \right){\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) , because \(\beta \le {\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) . Hence, after fresh ciphertexts encrypted under \(k\) keys perform homomorphic computation at a depth \(L\) , the final ciphertext has an error bound of \(poly\left( {n,k,l} \right)^{k + L} {\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) . In conclusion, for the modulus \(q\) , we should choose a modulus \(q \ge 4poly\left( {n,k,l} \right)^{k + L} {\mathbf{E^{\prime}}}\) . As \(l = \left\lceil {\log q} \right\rceil = \Theta \left( {\log q} \right) = \tilde{O}\left( {k + L} \right)\) , where \(\Theta\) describes the asymptotically tight bound and \(\tilde{O}\) hides the logarithmic terms. The LWE problem with this parameterization is considered hard. Security This section provides a security proof for the encryption scheme. Different from previous schemes, we do not need the circular security assumption because there is no circular security problem in the ciphertext extension process, and we do not use public keys to encrypt private keys. The scheme is demonstrated to be IND-CPA secure only under the DLWE assumption. Theorem 4.1 The \({\text{MFHE}}\) scheme presented above is IND-CPA secure, based solely on the DLWE assumption. Proof Here, we assume that challenger \({\text{C}}\) is the party that creates the ciphertext, while the adversary \({\text{A}}\) is the other party whose public key is associated with the ciphertext. We set up several hybrid games in the real and ideal worlds: Game 0: This represents a real-world security game executed between \({\text{C}}\) and \({\text{A}}\) , defined as follow: 1. The challenger \({\text{C}}\) executes the \({\text{Setup}}(1^{\lambda } ,1^{L} )\) algorithm to generate a public parameter \({\text{params}}\) , then executes the key generation algorithm to generate the key pair \(\left( {sk,pk} \right)\) where \(pk\) contains a public matrix \({\mathbf{A}} = \left( {\begin{array}{*{20}c} {\mathbf{B}} \\ {\mathbf{b}} \\ \end{array} } \right) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times m}\) , a random matrix \({\mathbf{D}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times m}\) , and a public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = {\mathbf{DR}} + ({\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) , \(sk = {\mathbf{t}} = ( - {\overline{\mathbf{t}}},1) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) . The challenger \({\text{C}}\) publishes \(pk\) to \({\text{A}}\) . 2. The adversary \({\text{A}}\) chooses messages \(\mu_{0} ,\mu_{1} \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}\) and sends them to \({\text{C}}\) . 3. The challenger \({\text{C}}\) samples a random matrix \({\mathbf{M}} \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}^{m \times nl}\) and chooses a bit value \(b \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}\) , then computes a ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}_{b} = {\mathbf{AM}} + \mu_{b} {\mathbf{G}}\) and sends it to adversary \({\text{A}}\) . 4. Finally, the adversary \({\text{A}}\) gives a guess value \(b^{\prime}\) for \(b\) . If \(b^{\prime} = b\) , the game outputs 1, otherwise, it outputs 0. We define \(S_{0}\) as the event that the adversary guesses the bit \(b\) correctly in the real world, \(\Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right]\) as the probability of this event occurring. Game 1: This is an ideal world execution of the security game and it is similar to Game 0 with the following differences: 1. In the key generation protocol \({\text{KeyGen(params)}}\) , the challenger \({\text{C}}\) chooses a uniform random matrix \({\mathbf{U}}_{1} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) , generates the public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = {\mathbf{U}}_{1} + ({\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) . In addition, \({\mathbf{DR}}\) is indistinguishable from a uniform distribution over \({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) under the DLWE assumption, So it can perfectly hide the key \({\mathbf{t}}\) . 2. The challenger \({\text{C}}\) samples a random matrix \({\mathbf{U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) and chooses a bit \(b \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}\) , then computes a ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}_{b} = {\mathbf{U}}_{2} + \mu_{b} {\mathbf{G}}\) and sends it to \({\text{A}}\) . We define \(S_{1}\) as the event that \(b^{\prime} = b\) in the ideal world, and \(\Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right]\) as the probability of this event occurring. The ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}_{b} = {\mathbf{U}}_{2} + \mu_{b} {\mathbf{G}}\) and public extension key \({\mathbf{P}} = {\mathbf{U}}_{1} + ({\mathbf{I}}_{n} \otimes {\mathbf{t}} \otimes {\mathbf{g}}) \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) are uniformly random because \({\mathbf{U}}_{1} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) and \({\mathbf{U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) are uniformly random matrices. Therefore, \(\Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right] = \tfrac{1}{2}\) in the ideal world. If Lemma 1 holds, our scheme is IND-CPA secure depends on the DLWE problem, we will now proceed to prove Lemma 1 . Lemma 1 \(\left| {\Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right] - \Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right]} \right| = \left| {\Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right] - \tfrac{1}{2}} \right| = {\text{negl}}\left( \lambda \right)\) , where \({\text{negl}}\left( \lambda \right)\) is a negligible function. Proof In the real world, the ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}_{b} = {\mathbf{AM}} + \mu_{b} {\mathbf{G}}\) where \({\mathbf{A}}\) is a LWE matrix and \({\mathbf{M}}\) is a random matrix, while the ciphertext \({\mathbf{C}}_{b} = {\mathbf{U}}_{2} + \mu_{b} {\mathbf{G}}\) in the ideal world where \({\mathbf{U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) is a random matrix. We define two distributions: $$D_{REAL} = \left\{ \begin{gathered} C_{b} \in Z_{q}^{n \times m} :C_{b} = {\text{AM}} + \mu_{b} {\text{G where A}} \in Z_{q}^{n \times m} {\text{ is }} \hfill \\ {\text{a LWE matrix and M}} \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}^{m \times nl} {\text{ is a random matrix}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right\}$$ $$D_{IDEAL} = \left\{ \begin{gathered} {\text{C}}_{b} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times m} :{\text{C}}_{b} = {\text{U}}_{2} + \mu_{b} {\text{G where U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl} {\text{ is a }} \hfill \\ {\text{random matrix}} \hfill \\ \end{gathered} \right\}$$ where \(\mu_{b} \in \left\{ {0,1} \right\}\) is the message that needs to be encrypted. If \({\text{A}}\) is able to distinguish between these two distributions, then \({\text{A}}\) can distinguish between \({\mathbf{AM}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) and \({\mathbf{U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) . Therefore, we define $$\Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right] = \Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{REAL} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right]$$ $$\Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right] = \Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{IDEAL} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right]$$ If the advantage difference between real and ideal worlds is negligible, in other words, the advantage of \({\text{A}}\) distinguishing between \({\mathbf{AM}}\) and \({\mathbf{U}}_{2}\) is negligible, namely, $$\begin{gathered} \Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right] - \Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right] \\ = \Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{{{\text{REAL}}}} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right] - \Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{{{\text{IDEAL}}}} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right] \\ = {\text{negl}}\left( \lambda \right) \\ \end{gathered}$$ Then our scheme is IND-CPA secure. According to the DLWE assumption, the equation holds. The work of Regev et al. ( 2009 ) indicates that the difficulty of the LWE problem is identical to that of approximating the SVP in lattices. Let \(params = (n,m,q,\chi ,B_{\chi } )\) be chosen such that the DLWE assumption holds, then DLWE assumption states that \({\mathbf{AM}}\) is computationally indistinguishable from a uniform distribution sampled from \({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) . Therefore, the advantage of \({\text{A}}\) in distinguishing between \({\mathbf{AM}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) and \({\mathbf{U}}_{2} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n \times nl}\) is negligible, so that \(\Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{REAL} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right] - \Pr_{{C_{b} \leftarrow D_{IDEAL} }} \left[ {{\text{A}}\left( {C_{b} } \right) = 1} \right] = negl\left( \lambda \right)\) , namely, \(\Pr \left[ {S_{0} } \right] - \Pr \left[ {S_{1} } \right] = {\text{negl}}\left( \lambda \right).\) In addition, the secret key \({\mathbf{t}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{n}\) is hidden by \({\mathbf{DR}} \in {\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) , which is indistinguishable from the uniform distribution over \({\mathbb{Z}}_{q}^{{n \times n^{2} l}}\) under the DLWE assumption. We can conclude that the ciphertext and the public key are uniformly random and independent. With respect to the secret key, it does not need to be involved in the key extension process. Each user's secret key is stored locally and remains undisclosed throughout all stages. Furthermore, even if an adversary observes ciphertexts under multiple keys, they are unable to recover the plaintext or infer the secret key. Hence, the security of the secret key is well ensured. In addition, our scheme supports threshold decryption. For more details, please refer to Sect. 5.4 of MW16. Finally, our scheme achieves IND-CPA secure. Conclusion We propose a novel dynamic MFHE scheme that does not require a CRS and is based solely on the DLWE assumption. Unlike previous dynamic MFHE schemes, our approach eliminates the need for a shared CRS during the setup phase. Instead, each participant independently generates a random matrix during the key generation phase, thus decentralizing the key generation process. This innovation removes the need to encrypt private keys with public keys, thereby avoiding the potential security risks associated with a circular security assumption and enhancing the theoretical security. Moreover, our scheme only needs the DLWE assumption, and the size of public keys and ciphertexts both are comparable to those of other dynamic MFHE schemes. It also demonstrates strong performance regarding computational efficiency and ciphertext extension. Future research directions include extending this scheme to support multi-bit encryption, constructing a threshold dynamic MFHE scheme that does not depend on a CRS or a circular security assumption, and exploring more efficient dynamic MFHE schemes without a CRS. These efforts aim further to advance the fields of privacy protection and data security. Data availability Not applicable.
