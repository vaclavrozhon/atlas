                                                                                                  Abstract
                                                     We prove that the six-vertex graph with edge set {ab, bc, cd, de, af, bf, df } has the Erdős–
                                                  Hajnal property. The proof adapts the iterative-sparsification method of Nguyen, Scott, and
                                                  Seymour within the comb-based framework of Huang, Ju, and Zhou.

1        Introduction
For a graph G, let α(G) and ω(G) denote its stability number and clique number. Erdős and Hajnal
conjectured that for every graph H there is a constant c = c(H) > 0 such that every H-free graph
G satisfies
                                   max{α(G), ω(G)} ≥ |G|c .                                 (1.1)
Here H-free means that no induced subgraph of G is isomorphic to H. When (1.1) holds, H is Erdős–
Hajnal ; equivalently, it has the Erdős–Hajnal property. The conjecture predicts a polynomial-size
clique or stable set, in sharp contrast with the logarithmic bound furnished by Ramsey’s theorem for
unrestricted graphs. Erdős and Hajnal proved, for every fixed H and all sufficiently large |G|, the
general lower bound                                          √
                                    max{α(G), ω(G)} ≥ 2cH log |G| ,
and Bucić, Nguyen, Scott, and Seymour improved it to
                                                   √
                            max{α(G), ω(G)} ≥ 2cH log |G| log log |G| .
The conjectured polynomial bound remains open for general H; see [3, 6].
    The property is invariant under complementation. A second fundamental closure theorem, due
to Alon, Pach, and Solymosi, says that it is preserved by vertex substitution [1]. Thus it is enough
to prove the conjecture for prime graphs, that is, graphs with no nontrivial module.
    The small prime graphs have been central to the subject. The property is classical for P4 ; indeed,
every P4 -free graph G satisfies α(G)ω(G) ≥ |G|. Thus all graphs on at most four vertices are
Erdős–Hajnal. Among prime five-vertex graphs, the bull was settled by Chudnovsky and Safra [4],
and the 5-cycle by Chudnovsky, Scott, Seymour, and Spirkl [5]. Nguyen, Scott, and Seymour recently
proved the conjecture for the five-vertex path P5 [9]. Together with complementation and the
substitution theorem, this completes the conjecture for every graph on at most five vertices.

1.1        Six-vertex cases and the contribution of this paper
Six is the first order for which the conjecture is not settled. There are 26 prime graphs on six vertices,
forming 13 complementary pairs. A direct enumeration gives ten pairs with a member containing an
induced P5 or P5 , and three with no such member.1

1.2       Main theorem and proof strategy
Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1. The graph F is Erdős–Hajnal.

     The proof uses the iterative-sparsification method of Nguyen, Scott, and Seymour [8], in the
comb-based framework developed by Huang, Ju, and Zhou [7]. In that framework, leaf-reducibility
together with a comb statement called property (∗) yields generalized niceness; generalized niceness,
leaf-reducibility, and wonderfulness then yield the Erdős–Hajnal property. We use this architecture
and several of its ideas.
     Some inputs and reduction steps are needed here in relative-size or complement-symmetric forms.
A few passages in the preprint were not clear to us in exactly those forms, and we may have missed
an intended convention or normalisation. We therefore cite the inputs used unchanged and state
and prove the variants and reduction steps needed here. This concerns only the general framework:
our proof of F neither uses nor reproves the graph-specific arguments for the E-graph or the Bird,
and those results remain entirely due to Huang, Ju, and Zhou.
     Put T = F . Section 3 verifies three preliminary facts. First, {F } is leaf-reducible because e is a
leaf of F and F − e = P5 . Second, the leaf/co-leaf closure and the known P5 theorem imply that the
family {F, T } is Erdős–Hajnal; this family result is used later for one pure blockade pattern. Third,
{F } satisfies the bidirectional form of wonderfulness required by the reduction.
     We do not prove property (∗) for F . Instead, we use the quantitative comb condition in
Definition 7.1, whose pure outcome may retain only a fixed positive power of the teeth, rather
than all of them. Concretely, Theorem 8.7 gives constants b, g, p, q > 0 such that every equipped
(ℓ, w)-comb in a T -free graph, with ℓ, w ≥ 4, has one of the following two outcomes:

                   (U) a complete or anticomplete (k, w/k b )-blockade with k ≥ ℓg ;
                   (P) a pure (k, w/ℓq )-blockade with ℓp ≤ k ≤ ℓ.

Here “equipped” means that a vertex outside the comb is complete to every tooth and anticomplete to
every handle. The homogeneous-set outcome allowed in Definition 7.1 is not needed for F . Section 7
proves, by adapting the five-stage reduction of Huang–Ju–Zhou, that this weaker quantitative
condition still implies generalized niceness for a leaf-reducible family.
    We next describe the graph-specific part of the proof. Write hi and Bi for the handles and teeth
of the comb. For sufficiently large ℓ, a pivot–true-twin extraction gives either many clique handles,
or a polynomial-size index set I on which every handle edge has an anticomplete tooth interface.
In the clique case, a propagation lemma for induced copies of T and the multipartite aggregation
lemma of Section 6 give outcome (U).
    In the other case, the component–anticomponent extraction either gives outcome (U) immediately
or, for every i ∈ I, produces an anticonnected set Xi contained in a connected carrier Ci ⊆ Bi . Only
after this extraction do the one-edge and three-edge rectangle exclusions show that every retained
interface Xi –Xj is additively separable over F2 . Interfaces over handle edges are already constant
zero, so every mixed interface lies over a handle nonedge.
    The rest of the argument is organised by two auxiliary graphs. The first, M , records the mixed
interfaces. Every hole of M has even length, and hence M is C5 -free. The Erdős–Hajnal theorem
for C5 supplies a polynomial-size set on which all interfaces are pure or all are mixed. The all-pure
case gives outcome (P). In the all-mixed case, the corresponding handles are pairwise nonadjacent,
and a second graph K records the interfaces whose two endpoint functions are both nonconstant.
We prove that K is a cluster graph.
    A large component of K has synchronised endpoint cuts. Choosing one fibre in each corresponding
tooth produces a pure blockade whose pattern is {F, T }-free; the Erdős–Hajnal property of the family
{F, T } then gives outcome (U). If every component of K is small, we choose one index from each of
many components. The remaining interfaces are one-sided, their orientations are transitive, and their
endpoint cuts are nested. The resulting pure pattern is an interval graph, whose clique-or-stable-set
bound again gives outcome (U).
    Section 8 carries out the exponent bookkeeping and handles bounded ℓ separately, proving
the quantitative comb condition for {F }. Lemma 7.12 then combines this condition with leaf-
reducibility to make {F } generalized nice. Finally, Theorem 7.7 combines generalized niceness with
leaf-reducibility and wonderfulness to prove Theorem 1.1.
    To place Theorem 1.1 in the six-vertex picture, the graphs N and J are prime [8, Figure 1
and p. 22]. The E-graph and the Bird are prime by Lemma 2.1, applied to P5 and the bull; in
neither graph is the attachment vertex the neighbour of a leaf. These base graphs are connected
and prime [7, p. 3], and F is prime by Lemma 3.1. The five graphs and their complements have
pairwise distinct degree sequences. Consequently, the Huang–Ju–Zhou theorems for E and the Bird,
the cited results for N, J, and Theorem 1.1 settle five of the thirteen complementary pairs. Of the
eight remaining pairs, two have no member containing an induced P5 or P5 , while six do. To the
best of our knowledge, all eight remain open as of 26 August 2026.
    The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 records the terminology and the general reductions.
Section 3 verifies the framework inputs and develops the local rectangle and affine-interface lemmas.
Sections 4 and 5 analyse the two auxiliary graphs, and Section 6 proves the clique-handle aggregation
lemma. Section 7 gives the quantitative reductions in the forms used here. Finally, Section 8 assembles
the structural alternatives, verifies the quantitative comb condition, and proves Theorem 1.1.

2     Preliminaries and the iterative-sparsification framework
All graphs are finite, simple, and nonempty unless explicitly stated otherwise. We write V (G) for
the vertex set of a graph G, |G| = |V (G)|, G[X] for the subgraph induced by X ⊆ V (G), and G for
the complement of G. For a positive integer k, put [k] = {1, . . . , k}. A graph is H-free, for a family
H, if it is H-free for every H ∈ H. We put H = {H : H ∈ H} and

                                        hom(G) = max{α(G), ω(G)}.

A finite family H has the Erdős–Hajnal property if there is c > 0 such that every H-free graph G
satisfies hom(G) ≥ |G|c .
    Let X, Y be disjoint vertex sets. We say that X is complete to Y if every pair in X × Y is an
edge, and anticomplete to Y if no such pair is an edge. The pair (X, Y ) is pure if it is complete or
anticomplete and is mixed otherwise. A graph is anticonnected if its complement is connected; an
anticomponent is a connected component of the complement.
    For x ∈ (0, 1), the set X is x-sparse to Y if every vertex of X has at most x|Y | neighbours in Y .
The pair (X, Y ) is weakly x-sparse if |E(X, Y )| ≤ x|X||Y |. A graph G is x-restricted if G or G has
maximum degree at most x|G|.

2.1    Blockades and combs
We use the terminology of Nguyen–Scott–Seymour and Huang–Ju–Zhou [7, 8]. An (ℓ, w)-blockade in
G is a sequence B = (B1 , . . . , Bk ) of pairwise disjoint subsets of V (G) such that k ≥ ℓ and |Bi | ≥ w
for every i. Thus ℓ and w are lower bounds and need not be integers, although the actual number
and orders of the blocks are integers. The blockade is complete, anticomplete, or pure when every
pair of distinct blocks has the corresponding property. It is x-sparse if Bi is x-sparse to Bj whenever
i > j. When B is pure, its pattern is the graph on [k] in which ij is an edge precisely when Bi is
complete to Bj .
    For a positive integer ℓ and a real number w > 0, an (ℓ, w)-comb is a sequence

                                               ((hi , Bi ) : i ∈ [ℓ])

such that (B1 , . . . , Bℓ ) is an (ℓ, w)-blockade, the vertices h1 , . . . , hℓ are distinct and disjoint from all
the blocks, and, for distinct i, j, the vertex hi is complete to Bi and anticomplete to Bj . We call the
hi the handles and the Bi the teeth. Property (∗) below also assumes a vertex
                                                [
                      r∈ / {h1 , . . . , hℓ } ∪   Bi ,
                                                i                                                             (2.1)
                      r is complete to every tooth and anticomplete to every handle.

We will always say explicitly that the comb is equipped with such a vertex r.
   For two teeth Xi ⊆ Bi and Xj ⊆ Bj , their interface is the bipartite adjacency relation between
them. Its adjacency matrix is denoted by

                                Mij (x, y) = 1xy∈E(G)         (x ∈ Xi , y ∈ Xj ).

All additions of entries of such a matrix are in F2 and are denoted by ⊕.

 2.2    Modules and substitution
 A set S ⊆ V (H) is a module if every vertex outside S is either complete or anticomplete to S. It is
 nontrivial if 1 < |S| < |H|, and H is prime if it has no nontrivial module.

 Lemma 2.1 (Prime leaf extension). Let H be connected and prime, let v ∈ V (H), and let H + be
 obtained from H by adding a leaf x adjacent to v. If H has no leaf whose neighbour is v, then H + is
 prime.

 Proof. Suppose that M is a proper nontrivial module of H + . If x ∈  / M , then M is a module of H,
 so primeness gives M = V (H). This is impossible because x is mixed on V (H).
     Now let x ∈ M . If v ∈ M , every vertex of H \ M is anticomplete to M . Connectedness of H
 then gives M = V (H + ), a contradiction. If v ∈/ M , put S = M \ {x}. The vertex v is complete to
 S, and every other vertex of H \ S is anticomplete to S; hence S is a module of H. Since v ∈/ S and
 H is prime, |S| = 1. Its unique vertex is a leaf of H adjacent to v, contrary to the hypothesis.

     If H1 , H2 are graphs and v ∈ V (H1 ), substitution of H2 for v replaces v by a copy of H2 which
 is complete to every neighbour of v in H1 and anticomplete to every nonneighbour of v. We use the
 following theorem.

 Theorem 2.2 (Alon–Pach–Solymosi [1]). If H1 and H2 have the Erdős–Hajnal property, then every
 graph obtained by substituting H2 for a vertex of H1 also has the Erdős–Hajnal property.

     We also use the following two established cases.

 Theorem 2.3 (Nguyen–Scott–Seymour [9]). The graph P5 , and therefore also P5 , has the Erdős–
 Hajnal property.

 Theorem 2.4 (Chudnovsky–Scott–Seymour–Spirkl [5]). There is a constant τ > 0 such that every
 C5 -free graph Q satisfies
                                   hom(Q) ≥ |Q|τ .

8.3    Components, anticomponents, and additive separation
We use the following component–anticomponent extraction, with explicit width bounds.
Lemma 8.4 (Components and anticomponents). Let teeth Bi , i ∈ I, have order at least w, and let
L ≥ 2 be an integer. One of the following holds.
   1. G has a complete or anticomplete blockade of length L and width at least w/L3 ;
   2. for every i ∈ I there are Xi ⊆ Ci ⊆ Bi such that G[Ci ] is connected, G[Xi ] is anticonnected,
      and
                                             |Xi | ≥ w/L2 .

If, in addition, every handle edge on I has an anticomplete tooth interface, then in the second outcome
every Xi –Xj matrix is additively separable over F2 :

                                     Mij (x, y) = λji (x) ⊕ λij (y) ⊕ γij .                            (8.3)
Proof. Partition a tooth into its connected components. If none has order at least w/L, greedily
group whole components into L unions, each of order at least w/L2 . Indeed, a completed union has
order less than w/L2 + w/L, and after L − 1 such unions the unused total is still greater than w/L2 .
The unions form an anticomplete blockade. Otherwise retain a connected component Ci of order at
least w/L.
     Inside Ci , apply the complementary argument to its anticomponents. It either gives a complete
L-blockade of width at least |Ci |/L2 ≥ w/L3 , or gives an anticomponent Xi of order at least
|Ci |/L ≥ w/L2 . Carry this out in every tooth unless an immediate blockade has already occurred.
     It remains to prove (8.3). If hi hj is an edge, the additional hypothesis says that the interface is
constant zero, which has the form (8.3). Suppose hi hj is a nonedge. If x, x′ ∈ Xi are nonadjacent
and y, y ′ ∈ Cj are adjacent, the rectangle {x, x′ } × {y, y ′ } cannot contain exactly one edge: after
relabelling, its witness is
                                  (a, b, c, d, e, f ) 7−→ (x, x′ , y, hi , r, y ′ ).
It cannot contain exactly three edges either: if x′ y ′ is its unique nonedge, a witness is
                                  (a, b, c, d, e, f ) 7−→ (x, x′ , y ′ , hi , y, hj ).                 (8.4)
The last nonedge required in (8.4) is precisely hi hj . Thus every such rectangle has even parity. Join
two rows by a path in G[Xi ] and two columns by a path in G[Cj ], and sum the elementary parity
equalities. Every rectangle of the full Xi –Cj matrix has even parity. Fixing one row and one column
gives the displayed additively separable representation; restricting the columns from Cj to Xj proves
(8.3).

   The results of Sections 4 and 5 give the following extraction. Its hypotheses state explicitly
where handle nonadjacency is used.

holds:

   2. a complete or anticomplete blockade of length at least mσ and width at least W/m.

blockade is at most m.

Proof. This is Theorem 5.7. Its hypotheses are exactly the hypotheses displayed above.

8.4     Clique handles
We also use the following aggregation statement.
