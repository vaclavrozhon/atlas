Distributed Algorithms, the Lovász Local Lemma,
and Descriptive Combinatorics

Abstract.
In this paper we consider coloring problems on graphs and other combinatorial structures on standard Borel spaces. Our goal is to obtain sufficient conditions under which such colorings can be made well-behaved in the sense of topology or measure. To this end, we show that such well-behaved colorings can be produced using certain powerful techniques from finite combinatorics and computer science. First, we prove that efficient distributed coloring algorithms (on finite graphs) yield well-behaved colorings of Borel graphs of bounded degree; roughly speaking, deterministic algorithms produce Borel colorings, while randomized algorithms give measurable and Baire-measurable colorings. Second, we establish measurable and Baire-measurable versions of the Symmetric Lovász Local Lemma (under the assumption
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)^{8}\leqslant 2^{-15}
, which is stronger than the standard LLL assumption
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant e^{-1}
but still sufficient for many applications). From these general results, we derive a number of consequences in descriptive combinatorics and ergodic theory.
1.  Introduction
A coloring, broadly construed, is a mapping that assigns to each element of a given structure one of a (typically finite) number of “colors” in a way that fulfills a prescribed set of constraints. The prototypical example is a proper
k
-coloring of a graph
G
, i.e., a mapping
f\colon V(G)\to[k]
such that
f(x)\neq f(y)
whenever the vertices
x
and
y
are adjacent in
G
. Classical problems in combinatorics often seek to identify sufficient conditions under which a coloring of a certain type exists.
In the last twenty or so years, a rich theory has emerged concerning the behavior of colorings and other combinatorial notions under additional regularity requirements. For instance, suppose that
G
is a graph whose vertex set
V(G)
is a standard probability space (e.g., the unit interval
[0,1]
with the usual Lebesgue measure). Does
G
admit a proper
k
-coloring
f\colon V(G)\to[k]
that is measurable, meaning that
f^{-1}(c)
is a measurable subset of
V(G)
for every color
c
? Questions of this type are the subject matter of
descriptive combinatorics
, which facilitates the fusion of ideas from combinatorics and descriptive set theory. For a state-of-the-art introduction to this area, see the survey
[
KM19
]
by Kechris and Marks.
Throughout this paper, we shall work under the assumption that the set of all available colors is countable (although there are interesting questions about uncountable colorings as well, see
[
KM19
, §§3 and 6]
). For concreteness, we can then assume that the colors form a subset of
{\mathbb{N}}=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}
, so a coloring of a structure
X
induces a partition
X=X_{0}\sqcup X_{1}\sqcup X_{2}\sqcup\ldots
of
X
into countably many labeled pieces, called the
color classes
. Assuming that
X
is a standard Borel space, we can then classify such colorings according to the regularity properties of their color classes. For instance, one can study
Borel
colorings, i.e., colorings in which every color class
X_{i}
is a Borel subset of
X
. This notion turns out to be rather restrictive, so one often considers colorings in which every color class is not necessarily Borel but
measurable
with respect to some probability Borel measure
\mu
on
X
or
Baire-measurable
with respect to some compatible Polish topology
\tau
on
X
. On the other hand, sometimes one can aim for something even stronger than Borel; for instance, one might consider
continuous
colorings, in which every color class is a clopen set.
The goal of this paper is to adapt certain powerful techniques from finite combinatorics to the descriptive setting. Specifically, we establish the following two groups of results:
(A)
If a coloring problem on finite graphs can be solved by an efficient distributed algorithm, then on infinite graphs the same problem admits solutions with some regularity properties. (See Theorems
2.10
,
2.13
,
2.14
, and
2.15
in §
2.B
for the precise statements.)
(B)
There is a measurable/Baire-measurable version of the Symmetric Lovász Local Lemma. (See Theorem
2.20
in §
2.C
for the precise statement.)
From these general facts, we derive a variety of new results in descriptive combinatorics, presented in §
3
. Here are just two examples (see §
3
for the definitions of the terms used):
Theorem 1.1
(see Theorem
3.4
)
.
There is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Fix integers
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and
c
. Let
G
be a Borel graph of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
. If
c\leqslant\sqrt{\Delta+1/4}-5/2
, then the following statements are equivalent:
(i)
the chromatic number of
G
is at most
\Delta-c
;
(ii)
the
\mu
-measurable chromatic number of
G
is at most
\Delta-c
.
Theorem 1.2
(see Theorem
3.9
)
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
. If
G
contains no cycles of length
3
(
resp. at most
4
)
, then the
\mu
-measurable chromatic number of
G
is at most
(4+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta
(
resp.
(1+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta
)
.
Theorem
1.1
was previously known only for
c\leqslant 0
: the
c<0
case is due to Kechris, Solecki, and Todorcevic
[
KST99
, Proposition 4.6]
and the
c=0
case is due to Conley, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CMT16
]
. The upper bound on
c
in the statement of Theorem
1.1
is within
2
of best possible (see Proposition
3.6
). The only previously known general upper bound on the measurable chromatic number of Borel graphs without
3
-cycles, or even with no cycles at all, in terms of their maximum degree was
\Delta
, which is implied by the result of Conley, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CMT16
]
. Lyons and Nazarov
[
LN11
]
observed (see also
[
KM19
, Theorem 5.46]
)
that there are acyclic Borel graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
and measurable chromatic number at least
(1/2+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta
, which means that the bounds in Theorem
1.2
are optimal up to a constant factor, even for acyclic graphs.
Let us now give some more details about bullet points
(A)
and
(B)
. Distributed computing is an area of computer science that, among other things, investigates questions of the following form: Given a graph coloring problem, how far in the graph should an individual vertex be allowed to “see” in order to be able to compute its own color? This idea is formalized in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model of distributed computation introduced by Linial
[
Lin92
]
. We describe this model here in a somewhat informal way; a precise definition, in the form needed for our purposes, is given in §
2.A
.
In the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model an
n
-vertex graph
G
abstracts a communication network where each vertex plays the role of a processor and edges represent communication links. The algorithm proceeds in
rounds
. During each round, the vertices first perform arbitrary local computations and then synchronously broadcast messages to all their neighbors. At the end, each vertex should output its own part of the global solution (i.e., its own color). There are no restrictions on the complexity of the local computations involved or on the length of the messages that the vertices send to each other, and the only measure of efficiency for such an algorithm is the number of communication rounds required.
We emphasize that in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, every vertex is executing the same algorithm. This means that, to make this model nontrivial, the vertices must be given a way of breaking symmetry. There are two standard symmetry-breaking approaches, leading to the distinction between deterministic and randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms:
—
In the
deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, each vertex is assigned, as part of its input, a unique
\Theta(\log n)
-bit identifier. The algorithm executed at each vertex is deterministic and must always output a correct solution to the problem, regardless of the specific assignment of the identifiers.
—
In the
randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, each vertex may generate an arbitrarily long finite sequence of independent uniformly distributed random bits. The algorithm may fail to produce a correct solution to the problem, but the probability of failure must not exceed
1/n
.
We remark that deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms can be simulated in the randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model: each vertex can simply generate a random sequence of
\lceil 3\log_{2}n\rceil
bits and use it as an identifier—the probability that two identifiers generated in this way coincide is less than
1/n
.
Notice that in a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm that runs on a graph
G
for
T
rounds, each vertex only has access to information in its radius-
T
neighborhood. Conversely, every
T
-round
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm can be transformed into one in which every vertex first collects all the information contained in its radius-
T
neighborhood and then makes a decision, based on this information alone, about its color. This alternative way of thinking about
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms makes it clear how they measure the “locality” of graph coloring problems and is often more convenient to work with. (Indeed, this is the approach we shall use in §
2.A
to define
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms formally.) For further background on distributed coloring algorithms, see the book
[
BE13
]
by Barenboim and Elkin.
Now we can describe some of our results in group
(A)
; for their precise statements, see §
2.B
. Fix
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We show that if a coloring problem can be solved by a deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm that runs in
o(\log n)
rounds on
n
-vertex graphs of maximum degree
\Delta
, then the same problem for Borel graphs of maximum degree
\Delta
admits Borel solutions (see Theorem
2.10
). Furthermore, under extra topological assumptions, “Borel” here may be replaced by “continuous” (see Theorem
2.13
). Similarly, randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms that run in
o(\log n)
rounds yield both measurable and Baire-measurable colorings (see Theorem
2.14
), and “measurable” may be upgraded to “Borel” under additional assumptions on the growth rate of the underlying graph (see Theorem
2.15
). These general facts enable one to prove new results in descriptive combinatorics simply by alluding to known distributed algorithms; for several such examples, see §
3
.
While the proofs of Theorems
2.10
and
2.13
(dealing with deterministic algorithms) are relatively straightforward, Theorem
2.14
(concerning randomized algorithms) is more involved. In particular, it relies on a novel measurable version of the Lovász Local Lemma that we establish in this paper, which brings us to bullet point
(B)
.
The
Lovász Local Lemma
(the
LLL
for short) is a powerful tool in probabilistic combinatorics, introduced by Erdős and Lovász in the mid-1970s
[
EL75
]
. The LLL is mostly used to obtain existence results, and it is particularly well-suited for finding colorings that satisfy some “local” constraints. Roughly speaking, in order for the LLL to apply in this context, two requirements must be met: First, a random coloring must be “likely” to fulfill each individual constraint; second, the constraints must not interact with each other “too much.” The precise statement of the LLL requires a few technical definitions, so we postpone it until §
2.C.1
.
It has been a matter of interest to determine if the LLL can yield “constructive” conclusions (rather than pure existence results). The first such “constructive” version of the LLL was the
algorithmic LLL
due to Beck
[
Bec91
]
. Beck’s result requires somewhat stronger numerical assumptions than the ordinary LLL. This discrepancy has been eventually eliminated in the breakthrough work of Moser and Tardos
[
MT10
]
; for the long list of intermediate results, see the references in
[
MT10
]
.
The Moser–Tardos method was later adapted to derive “constructive” analogs of the LLL in a variety of different contexts. For example, Rumyantsev and Shen
[
RS14
]
proved a
computable
version of the LLL. Here we are interested in the behavior of the LLL in the
descriptive
setting. When can the LLL be used to obtain Borel, measurable, or Baire-measurable colorings? Partial answers to this question have been given in
[
Ber19
]
by the present author and in
[
Csó+22
]
by Csóka, Grabowski, Máthé, Pikhurko, and Tyros. The main results of both
[
Ber19
]
and
[
Csó+22
]
only apply under rather special circumstances:
[
Ber19
, Theorem 6.6]
is a measurable version of the LLL for structures induced by the Bernoulli shift actions
\Gamma\curvearrowright[0,1]^{\Gamma}
of countable groups
\Gamma
, while
[
Csó+22
, Theorem 1.3]
is a Borel LLL for coloring problems on graphs of subexponential growth.
In this paper we establish the first measurable/Baire-measurable version of the LLL that works without any such special restrictions (see Theorem
2.20
). The drawback is that, as in the seminal algorithmic LLL of Beck, we require stronger numerical bounds than in the ordinary LLL. Nevertheless, just like Beck’s result,
our measurable LLL
is sufficient for many combinatorial applications. In particular, as mentioned above, it plays the central role in our proof that
sublogarithmic randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms yield measurable/Baire-measurable colorings
, and hence it lies at the heart of the measurable coloring results presented in §
3
. Of course,
our measurable LLL
can also be used without any reference to distributed algorithms. We give an example of such a direct application to a problem in ergodic theory in §
3.E
.
We remark that, while we use
the measurable LLL
to establish a relationship between randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms and measurable colorings, our proof of
the measurable LLL
itself relies on the randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for the LLL that was recently developed by Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
and sharpened by Ghaffari, Harris, and Kuhn
[
GHK18
]
. Curiously, both the Fischer–Ghaffari algorithm and our proof of the measurable LLL do not invoke the Moser–Tardos method and instead go back essentially to the original ideas of Beck.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. We give the necessary definitions and state our main results precisely in §
2
. Next we present a variety of applications in §
3
. In §
4
, we explain how to turn distributed algorithms into colorings with regularity properties. Specifically, §
4
contains the proofs of our results concerning deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms and reduces the results about randomized algorithms to the LLL. Finally, we prove our measurable LLL in §
5
.

2.  Main definitions and results
2.A.  Distributed algorithms for graph coloring problems
In this section we formally introduce the terminology pertaining to the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model of distributed computation. The nature of our main results requires us to be somewhat more pedantic with our definitions than is standard in the distributed algorithms literature. The reader who is already familiar with the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model is encouraged to only skim this section in order to familiarize herself with our notation and quickly move on to §
2.B
, where we connect
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms to Borel/measurable colorings.
2.A.1.
Graphs and structured graphs
Given a set
A
, we write
A^{<\infty}
(resp.
[A]^{<\infty}
) to denote the set of all finite sequences (resp. finite sets) of elements of
A
. Unless otherwise specified, by a “graph” we mean a simple undirected graph. Our graph-theoretic terminology and notation are standard; see, e.g., Diestel’s book
[
Die17
]
. In particular, the vertex and edge sets of a graph
G
are denoted by
V(G)
and
E(G)
respectively. As usual, we write
|G|\coloneqq|V(G)|
and
\|G\|\coloneqq|E(G)|
. For
U\subseteq V(G)
, the
neighborhood
of
U
in
G
(i.e., the set of all vertices with a neighbor in
U
) is denoted by
N_{G}(U)
. For a vertex
x\in V(G)
, we set
N_{G}(x)\coloneqq N_{G}(\{x\})
and use
\deg_{G}(x)\coloneqq|N_{G}(x)|
to denote the
degree
of
x
in
G
. The
maximum degree
of
G
is defined by
\Delta(G)\coloneqq\sup_{x\in V(G)}\deg_{G}(x)
. A graph
G
is said to be
locally finite
if
\deg_{G}(x)<\infty
for all
x\in V(G)
. Throughout this paper we only work with locally finite graphs; in fact, we mostly focus on graph whose maximum degree is finite.
Sometimes one needs to consider graphs that support some additional structure. For instance, one might wish to work with directed graphs, weighted graphs, or graphs with a fixed coloring of the vertices. We capture this idea in the general notion of a “structured graph”:
Definition 2.1
(Structured graphs)
.
A
structure map
on a graph
G
is a partial function
\sigma\colon V(G)^{<\infty}\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
such that for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every tuple
\bm{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
is of length at most
\ell
. A
structured graph
is a pair
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
, where
G
is a graph and
\sigma
is a structure map on
G
.
In the above definition, we only use
{\mathbb{N}}
as the range of
\sigma
for convenience, as having the range of
\sigma
fixed once and for all will simplify some of the forthcoming definitions. In applications, any countable set could be used instead of
{\mathbb{N}}
, and indeed we shall often abuse terminology by referring to structured graphs whose structure maps range over different countable sets. For instance, we can view a graph
G
equipped with a finite sequence
\sigma_{1}
, …,
\sigma_{k}
of structure maps as a structured graph by replacing the tuple
(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
with the single function
\sigma\colon\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}\mathrm{dom}(\sigma_{i})\to({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\ast\})^{k}\colon\bm{x}\mapsto(\sigma_{1}(\bm{x}),\ldots,\sigma_{k}(\bm{x})),
where
\ast
is a special symbol distinct from all the elements of
{\mathbb{N}}
and
\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})=\ast
means that
\sigma_{i}(\bm{x})
is undefined. This is an acceptable construction since the set
({\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\ast\})^{k}
is countable. As a special case, given a structured graph
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
and another structure map
\tau
on
G
, we can interpret the pair
(\bm{G},\tau)
as a new structured graph (with some “extra structure” added to that of
\bm{G}
). This convention will become important when we formally define
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms.
Example 2.2
(Directed graphs)
.
A directed graph
G
can naturally be viewed as a structured graph, since we can encode the directions of the edges of
G
using the function
V(G)^{2}\to\{0,1\}
that sends a pair
(x,y)
to
1
if and only if there is a directed edge from
x
to
y
in
G
.
Example 2.3
(Multigraphs)
.
If the edges of a graph
G
are allowed to have (finite) multiplicities, then we can view
G
as a structured graph equipped with the function
V(G)^{2}\to{\mathbb{N}}
that assigns to each pair
(x,y)
the multiplicity of the edge
xy
.
Example 2.4
(List-coloring)
.
Sometimes, instead of assigning to each vertex of a graph
G
a color from a fixed set, such as
[k]
, one is required to pick a color for every
x\in V(G)
from its own list
L(x)
of available colors (this is called the
list-coloring problem
; see
[
Die17
, §5.4]
). If every list
L(x)
is a finite subset of
{\mathbb{N}}
(or of any other countable set), then the pair
(G,L)
can be naturally viewed as a structured graph (since the set
[{\mathbb{N}}]^{<\infty}
is countable).
We naturally extend all the standard graph-theoretic notation, such as
V
,
\Delta
, etc., to structured graphs; that is, for a structured graph
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
, we write
V(\bm{G})\coloneqq V(G)
,
\Delta(\bm{G})\coloneqq\Delta(G)
, etc.
Given a graph
G
and a subset
U\subseteq V(G)
,
G[U]
denotes the
subgraph of
G
induced by
U
, i.e., the graph with vertex set
U
in which two vertices
x
,
y\in U
are adjacent if and only if they are adjacent in
G
. Similarly, if
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
is a structured graph and
U\subseteq V(G)
, then
\bm{G}[U]
is the structured graph given by
\bm{G}[U]\coloneqq(G[U],{{\sigma}|{U^{<\infty}}})
. (Here and in what follows we use the notation
{{f}|{X}}
to indicate the restriction of a function
f
onto the set
X\cap\mathrm{dom}(f)
.)
As usual, the
distance
{\mathrm{dist}}_{G}(x,y)
between two vertices
x
,
y\in V(G)
is the smallest number of edges on a path in
G
that starts at
x
and ends at
y
(if there is no such path, then
{\mathrm{dist}}_{G}(x,y)\coloneqq\infty
). For a vertex
x\in V(G)
and a number
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define
B_{G}(x,R)
to be the
ball
of radius
R
around
x
in
G
, i.e., the subgraph of
G
induced by the set
\{y\in V(G)\,:\,{\mathrm{dist}}_{G}(x,y)\leqslant R\}
. The definition of
B_{\bm{G}}(x,R)
for structured graphs
\bm{G}
is the same,
mutatis mutandis
.
We say that two structured graphs
\bm{G}_{1}=(G_{1},\sigma_{1})
and
\bm{G}_{2}=(G_{2},\sigma_{2})
are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism between their underlying graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
that turns the function
\sigma_{1}
into
\sigma_{2}
. More formally, each function
\varphi\colon V(G_{1})\to V(G_{2})
can be extended to a map
V(G_{1})^{<\infty}\to V(G_{2})^{<\infty}
in the obvious way; namely, given a tuple
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in V(G_{1})^{<\infty}
, define
\varphi(\bm{x})\,\coloneqq\,(\varphi(x_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(x_{k}))\,\in\,V(G_{2})^{<\infty}.
An
isomorphism
between
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
is a function
\varphi\colon V(G_{1})\to V(G_{2})
such that:
—
\varphi
is an isomorphism of the graphs
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
;
—
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma_{2})=\varphi(\mathrm{dom}(\sigma_{1}))
; and
—
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma_{1})
,
\sigma_{1}(\bm{x})=\sigma_{2}(\varphi(\bm{x}))
.
We say that
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
are
isomorphic
, in symbols
\bm{G}_{1}\cong\bm{G}_{2}
, if there is an isomorphism between
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
. The isomorphism class of a structured graph
\bm{G}
is denoted by
[\bm{G}]
. The set of all the isomorphism classes of finite structured graphs is denoted by
\mathsf{FSG}
. Notice that
\mathsf{FSG}
is a countable set (here we exploit the fact that the range of
\sigma
in Definition
2.1
is assumed to be the fixed set
{\mathbb{N}}
).
A
rooted graph
is a pair
(G,x)
, where
G
is a graph and
x\in V(G)
is a distinguished vertex, called the
root
. Isomorphisms between rooted graphs are required to preserve the roots. One similarly defines
rooted structured graphs
(\bm{G},x)
. To simplify the notation, we denote the isomorphism class of a rooted structured graph
(\bm{G},x)
by
[\bm{G},x]
(instead of
[(\bm{G},x)]
). The set of all the isomorphism classes of finite rooted structured graphs is denoted by
\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}
. Again, the set
\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}
is countable.
2.A.2.
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms
Now we have enough notation to start defining the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model.
Definition 2.5
(
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms)
.
A
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
is a function
\mathcal{A}\colon\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}\to{\mathbb{N}}
. Given a locally finite structured graph
\bm{G}
and a natural number
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
output of
\mathcal{A}
on
\bm{G}
after
T
rounds
is the function
\mathcal{A}(\bm{G},T)\colon V(\bm{G})\to{\mathbb{N}}
given by
\mathcal{A}(\bm{G},T)(x)\,\coloneqq\,\mathcal{A}\left([B_{\bm{G}}(x,T),x]\right)\qquad\text{for all }x\in V(\bm{G}).
Note that since
\bm{G}
is locally finite,
B_{\bm{G}}(x,T)
is finite, so this definition makes sense.
Informally, Definition
2.5
says that a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
operates on a locally finite structured graph
\bm{G}
as follows. Each vertex
x\in V(\bm{G})
“sees” the isomorphism type of its radius-
T
ball
B_{\bm{G}}(x,T)
, which is viewed as a structured graph rooted at
x
. The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is then a “rule” that uses this information to output a “color”
\mathcal{A}(\bm{G},T)(x)\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The same comment as after Definition
2.1
applies here: instead of the set
{\mathbb{N}}
in Definition
2.5
, any other countable set of possible outputs could be (and often is) used.
So far we have defined how
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms operate. Now we need to describe what problems they can solve. The problems we consider are sometimes called “locally checkable labeling problems” in the distributed computing literature (this term was introduced in the seminal work of Naor and Stockmeyer
[
NS95
]
). However, in order to stay closer to the terminology in descriptive combinatorics and in graph theory, we prefer to use the word “coloring” instead of “labeling.”
Recall that if
\bm{G}
is a structured graph and
f\colon V(\bm{G})\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
is a partial function (or, more generally, a structure map), then the pair
(\bm{G},f)
can be thought of as a structured graph in its own right, in which the function
f
is “added” to the structure (see the comments after Definition
2.1
). For brevity, we denote this structured graph by
\bm{G}_{f}
.
Definition 2.6
(Local coloring problems)
.
A
local coloring problem
(or a
locally checkable labeling problem
) is a pair
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
, where
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}\to\{0,1\}
. Here we interpret
\mathcal{P}
as a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm. Let
\bm{G}
be a locally finite graph and suppose that we are given a function
f\colon V(\bm{G})\to{\mathbb{N}}
. We say that
f
is a
\Pi
-coloring
of
\bm{G}
if
\mathcal{P}(\bm{G}_{f},t)(x)=1
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
, i.e., if the output of the algorithm
\mathcal{P}
on the structured graph
\bm{G}_{f}
after
t
rounds is the constant
1
function.
What Definition
2.6
says is that, for a local coloring problem, there is a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm,
\mathcal{P}
, that, given a coloring
f
, can verify whether
f
is “correct” in a constant number of rounds, namely
t
. To put this another way, whether or not a coloring
f
is “correct” is completely determined by the restrictions of
f
to balls of radius
t
.
Example 2.7
(Proper coloring)
.
The prototypical example of a local coloring problem is proper
k
-coloring of graphs, since whether or not a coloring is proper is determined by its restrictions to radius-
1
balls. Explicitly, let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and define a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}\to\{0,1\}
as follows: Given (the isomorphism type of) a finite rooted structured graph of the form
(G,f,x)
with
f\colon V(G)\to[k]
and a root
x
, set
\mathcal{P}([G,f,x])\coloneqq 1
if and only if
f(y)\neq f(z)
for every pair of adjacent vertices
y
,
z\in V(G)
; in all other cases set
\mathcal{P}
to
0
. Now if we let
\Pi\coloneqq(1,\mathcal{P})
, then a
\Pi
-coloring of a locally finite graph
G
is exactly the same as a proper
k
-coloring of
G
.
2.A.3.
Complexity of local coloring problems
As mentioned in the
introduction
, we must distinguish between the deterministic and the randomized versions of the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model.
Definition 2.8
(Deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity)
.
Let
\Pi
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)\leqslant T
if and only if there is a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with the following property:
Let
\bm{G}
be an
n
-vertex structured graph such that
[\bm{G}]\in\mathsf{G}
and let
\mathsf{id}\colon V(\bm{G})\to[n]
be an arbitrary bijection. Then the function
\mathcal{A}(\bm{G}_{\mathsf{id}},T)
is a
\Pi
-coloring of
\bm{G}
.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, define
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)
to be the least
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)\leqslant T
if such
T
exists, and
\infty
otherwise. The function
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}\colon{\mathbb{N}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
is called the
deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity
of the problem
\Pi
on the class of structured graphs
\mathsf{G}
.
Let us make a few remarks about the above definition. The set
\mathsf{G}
in Definition
2.8
is the collection of (the isomorphism classes of) the finite structured graphs on which we attempt to solve the given coloring problem
\Pi
. For example,
\mathsf{G}
may contain all graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
, all triangle-free graphs, all trees, all cycles, all directed graphs, etc. If
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)\leqslant T
, it means that there is a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
that solves the problem
\Pi
on
n
-vertex graphs
\bm{G}
from the class
\mathsf{G}
in
T
rounds, given as part of its input an arbitrary assignment
\mathsf{id}
of unique identifiers from the set
[n]
to the vertices of
\bm{G}
. The reader may recall from the
introduction
that usually the identifiers are sequences of bits of length
\Theta(\log n)
, or, equivalently, elements of
[n^{c}]
for some constant
c\geqslant 1
. In practice it does not matter what constant
c
one uses, so, for concreteness, we fix
c
to be
1
(technically, making
c
as small as possible only makes our main results stronger).
Definition 2.9
(Randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity)
.
Let
\Pi
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Given
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)\leqslant T
if and only if there are
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with the following property:
Let
\bm{G}
be an
n
-vertex structured graph such that
[\bm{G}]\in\mathsf{G}
. Pick a function
\vartheta\colon V(\bm{G})\to[m]
uniformly at random (that is, each function
V(\bm{G})\to[m]
is chosen with probability
1/m^{n}
). Then
\mathbb{P}\left[\text{$\mathcal{A}(\bm{G}_{\vartheta},T)$ is a $\Pi$-coloring of $\bm{G}$}\right]\,\geqslant\,1\,-\,\frac{1}{n}.
For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, define
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)
to be the least
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)\leqslant T
if such
T
exists, and
\infty
otherwise. The function
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}\colon{\mathbb{N}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
is called the
randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity
of the problem
\Pi
on the class of structured graphs
\mathsf{G}
.
In Definition
2.9
,
\mathsf{G}
is again the class of finite structured graphs on which we wish to solve a given coloring problem. The algorithm
\mathcal{A}
takes, as part of its input, an assignment of random numbers from
[m]
to the vertices of
\bm{G}
, and, while it may fail to output a
\Pi
-coloring, the probability of failure cannot exceed
1/n
. Notice that there is no a priori upper bound on
m
, i.e., the algorithm is allowed to use “unlimited randomness.” However, we do require that the same
m
must work for all
n
-vertex graphs
\bm{G}
with
[\bm{G}]\in\mathsf{G}
. This restriction is harmless, at least for our purposes, as we shall only apply Definition
2.9
to classes
\mathsf{G}
that contain finitely many isomorphism types of
n
-vertex graphs for each
n
.
2.B.  From distributed algorithms to descriptive combinatorics
2.B.1.
Deterministic algorithms and Borel colorings
We use standard descriptive set-theoretic terminology; see, e.g., Kechris’s book
[
Kec95
]
or Tserunyan’s notes
[
Tse16
]
. Recall that a separable topological space is
Polish
if its topology is induced by a complete metric. A
standard Borel space
is a set
X
equipped with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}(X)
of
Borel sets
generated by a Polish topology on
X
. We say that a Polish topology on a standard Borel space
X
is
compatible
if it generates
\mathfrak{B}(X)
.
By a
Borel graph
we mean a graph
G
whose vertex set
V(G)
is a standard Borel space and such that
\{(x,y)\,:\,\text{$x$ and $y$ are adjacent in $G$}\}
is a Borel subset of
V(G)\times V(G)
. Similarly, a
Borel structured graph
is a structured graph
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
such that
G
is a Borel graph and
\sigma\colon V(G)^{<\infty}\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
is a Borel function (which just means that for every
c\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\sigma^{-1}(c)
is a Borel subset of
V(G)^{<\infty}
).
Let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
be a collection of isomorphism types of finite structured graphs and let
R
,
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We say that a structured graph
\bm{G}
is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
if for each vertex
x\in V(\bm{G})
, there is an
n
-vertex structured graph
\bm{H}
with
[\bm{H}]\in\mathsf{G}
such that
[B_{\bm{G}}(x,R),x]=[B_{\bm{H}}(y,R),y]
for some
y\in V(\bm{H})
. For instance, suppose that
\mathsf{G}
is a class of finite graphs (with no other structure) closed under adding isolated vertices. Examples of such classes are the class of all planar graphs, the class of all graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
for some fixed
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the class of all triangle-free graphs, etc. It is not hard to see that in this case a graph
G
is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
provided that for all
x\in V(G)
,
[B_{G}(x,R)]\in\mathsf{G}
and
|B_{G}(x,R)|\leqslant n
. Now we can state our first result:
Theorem 2.10
(Deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms yield Borel colorings)
.
Let
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
such that
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)<\infty
and set
R\coloneqq\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)+t
. If
\bm{G}
is a Borel structured graph that is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
and such that
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,2R)|\leqslant n
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
, then
\bm{G}
has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring.
Note that a structured graph
\bm{G}
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
2.10
must have finite maximum degree, since
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,R)|\leqslant n<\infty
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
.
Usually the precise value of
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)
is not known and one only has access to asymptotic upper bounds. For instance, assume that
\mathsf{G}
is a class of finite graphs (with no other structure), and let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
all of whose finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathsf{G}
. We claim that if
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
is a local coloring problem such that
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)=o(\log n)
, then
G
has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring. Indeed, we may assume that
G
is infinite (otherwise
G
itself is a finite graph in
\mathsf{G}
, and hence it has an—automatically Borel—
\Pi
-coloring by assumption). This implies that
\mathsf{G}
is closed under adding isolated vertices. Since
t
is a constant independent of
n
,
R(n)\coloneqq\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)+t=o(\log n)
as well. Therefore, for each
x\in V(G)
,
(2.11)
|B_{G}(x,R(n))|\,\leqslant\,|B_{G}(x,2R(n))|\,\leqslant\,1+\Delta^{2R(n)}\,=\,1+\Delta^{o(\log n)}\,=\,n^{o(1)}.
Thus, Theorem
2.10
may be applied for any large enough value of
n
.
It is possible to incorporate into such calculations extra assumptions on the growth rate of
G
. For example, say that a graph
G
is of
subexponential growth
if for each
\varepsilon>0
, there is
R_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that if
x\in V(G)
and
R\geqslant R_{0}
, then
|B_{G}(x,R)|<(1+\varepsilon)^{R}
. As before, let
\mathsf{G}
be a class of finite graphs and let
G
be a Borel graph of subexponential growth all of whose finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathsf{G}
. The same calculation as in the last paragraph shows that
G
has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring whenever
\Pi
is a local coloring problem with
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)=O(\log n)
. Stronger assumptions on the growth rate of
G
enable one to further relax the necessary bound on
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)
.
The reasoning in the preceding two paragraphs will apply, almost verbatim, to all the other results in this section.
2.B.2.
Deterministic algorithms and continuous colorings
Under certain circumstances, the word “Borel” in the conclusion of Theorem
2.10
can be replaced by “continuous.” In order to state this result precisely, we require a few definitions. In what follows, we view
{\mathbb{N}}
as a discrete topological space. Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
\bm{G}_{1}
,
\bm{G}_{2}
be finite structured graphs with
V(\bm{G}_{1})
,
V(\bm{G}_{2})\subseteq X
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we say that
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
are
\varepsilon
-isomorphic
(with respect to the metric
d
) if there is an isomorphism
\varphi\colon V(\bm{G}_{1})\to V(\bm{G}_{2})
between
\bm{G}_{1}
and
\bm{G}_{2}
such that
d(x,\varphi(x))<\varepsilon
for all
x\in V(\bm{G}_{1})
. Recall that a topological space is
zero-dimensional
is it has a base consisting of clopen sets.
Definition 2.12
(Topological graphs)
.
A
topological structured graph
is a locally finite structured graph
\bm{G}
whose vertex set
V(\bm{G})
is a zero-dimensional Polish space and that has the following property for some (hence any) metric
d
inducing the topology on
V(\bm{G})
: For each
x\in V(\bm{G})
,
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\delta>0
such that if
y\in V(\bm{G})
satisfies
d(x,y)<\delta
, then the rooted structured graphs
(B_{\bm{G}}(x,R),x)
and
(B_{\bm{G}}(y,R),y)
are
\varepsilon
-isomorphic.
Note that the above definition is indeed independent of the choice of the metric
d
(we could have stated it purely topologically, by fixing an open neighborhood for each vertex in
B_{\bm{G}}(x,R)
instead of using the parameter
\varepsilon
). If we are hoping to find continuous colorings of
\bm{G}
, it is natural to assume that the topology on
V(\bm{G})
is zero-dimensional, so that
V(\bm{G})
has enough clopen subsets and hence there are many continuous functions
V(\bm{G})\to{\mathbb{N}}
. The rest of Definition
2.12
describes the interplay between the combinatorics of
\bm{G}
and the topology on
V(\bm{G})
. In particular, if
\bm{G}
is a topological graph, then the map
V(\bm{G})\to\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}\colon x\mapsto[B_{\bm{G}}(x,R),x]
is continuous for all
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where we view the countable set
\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}
as a discrete space.
We prove a continuous version of Theorem
2.10
for topological graphs:
Theorem 2.13
(Deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms yield continuous colorings)
.
Let
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
such that
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)<\infty
and set
R\coloneqq\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)+t
. If
\bm{G}
is a topological structured graph that is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
and such that
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,2R)|\leqslant n
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
, then
\bm{G}
has a continuous
\Pi
-coloring.
Observe that Theorem
2.13
is a strengthening of Theorem
2.10
. Indeed, it follows from standard results in descriptive set theory that if
\bm{G}
is a locally finite Borel structured graph, then there is a compatible zero-dimensional Polish topology on
V(\bm{G})
that makes
\bm{G}
a topological structured graph
[
Kec95
, §13]
. (Nevertheless, we will give a direct proof of Theorem
2.10
as well.)
An ample supply of natural examples of topological graphs is provided by continuous actions of finitely generated groups. Let
X
be a zero-dimensional Polish space and let
\Gamma
be a group generated by a finite set
S\subseteq\Gamma
. Assume additionally that
S
does not contain the identity element of
\Gamma
and that
S
is
symmetric
, meaning that if
\gamma\in S
, then
\gamma^{-1}\in S
as well. Given a free action
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright X
of
\Gamma
on
X
by homeomorphisms, let the
Schreier graph
of
\alpha
be the graph
G_{\alpha}
with vertex set
X
in which two vertices
x
,
y\in X
are adjacent if and only if
x=\gamma\cdot_{\alpha}y
for some
\gamma\in S
. Because
\alpha
is free, it is easy to see that
G_{\alpha}
is a topological graph. If
S
is not assumed to be symmetric, one can similarly define a directed version of the Schreier graph, which is a topological directed graph.
2.B.3.
Randomized algorithms
Recall that a set or a function is
measurable
(resp.
Baire
-
measurable
) if it differs from a Borel set or function on a null (resp. meager) set. In order to obtain measurable or Baire-measurable colorings, it is enough to work with randomized rather than deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms:
Theorem 2.14
(Randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms yield measurable colorings)
.
Let
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
such that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)<\infty
and set
R\coloneqq\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)+t
. Let
\bm{G}
be a Borel structured graph that is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
and such that
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,2R)|\leqslant n^{1/8}/4
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
. Then the following conclusions hold:
(i)
If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on
V(\bm{G})
, then
\bm{G}
has a
\mu
-measurable
\Pi
-coloring.
(ii)
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
V(\bm{G})
, then
\bm{G}
has a
\tau
-Baire-measurable
\Pi
-coloring.
Although the bound on
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,2R)|
required in Theorem
2.14
is stronger than that in Theorem
2.10
, it remains polynomial in
n
. In particular, inequalities (
2.11
) still show that if
\mathsf{G}
is a class of finite graphs
and
G
is a Borel graph of finite maximum degree all of whose finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathsf{G}
, then
G
is measurably and Baire-measurably
\Pi
-colorable provided that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)=o(\log n)
. Furthermore, if
G
is of subexponential growth, then
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)=O(\log n)
suffices. Actually, in this case we can even make the coloring Borel:
Theorem 2.15
(Randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms yield Borel colorings of subexponential growth graphs)
.
Let
\Pi=(t,\mathcal{P})
be a local coloring problem and let
\mathsf{G}\subseteq\mathsf{FSG}
. Fix
n\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
such that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)<\infty
and set
R\coloneqq\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}}(n)+t
.
If
\bm{G}
is a Borel structured graph of subexponential growth that is
(R,n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}
and such that
|B_{\bm{G}}(x,2R)|\leqslant n/e
for all
x\in V(\bm{G})
, then
\bm{G}
has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring.
In the statement of Theorem
2.15
,
e=2.71\ldots
is the base of the natural logarithm. Theorem
2.15
contributes to the growing body of results showing that various combinatorial problems on graphs of subexponential growth can be solved in a Borel way, whereas in general one can only hope for a measurable solution; see, e.g.,
[
Csó+22
,
CT21
,
Tho22
]
.
2.C.  The Lovász Local Lemma
As mentioned in the
introduction
, the key ingredient in our proof of Theorem
2.14
is a new measurable version of the Lovász Local Lemma (the LLL for short), which is an interesting result in its own right. Indeed, the original motivation for this paper was to develop a better understanding of the behavior of the LLL in the measurable setting.
2.C.1.
Constraint satisfaction problems and the LLL
Recall that for a set
A
,
[A]^{<\infty}
denotes the set of all finite subsets of
A
. Similarly, for sets
A
and
B
, let
[A\rightharpoonup B]^{<\infty}
be the set of all partial functions
\varphi\colon A\rightharpoonup B
with finite domains.
Definition 2.16
(CSPs)
.
Fix a set
X
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
. An
(X,m)
-constraint
(or simply a
constraint
if
X
and
m
are clear from the context) is a set
B\subseteq[X\rightharpoonup[m]]^{<\infty}
such that
\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)=\mathrm{dom}(\psi)
for all
\varphi
,
\psi\in B
. If a constraint
B
is nonempty, then its
domain
is the set
\mathrm{dom}(B)\coloneqq\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)
for some (hence all)
\varphi\in B
; the domain of the empty constraint is defined to be
\varnothing
. A
constraint satisfaction problem
(a
CSP
for short)
\mathscr{B}
on
X
with range
[m]
, in symbols
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
, is a set of
(X,m)
-constraints. A
solution
to a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
is a function
f\colon X\to[m]
such that for all
B\in\mathscr{B}
, the restriction
{{f}|{\mathrm{dom}(B)}}
of
f
onto
\mathrm{dom}(B)
is not a member of
B
.
In other words, in a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
, each constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
is interpreted as a set of “forbidden patterns” that are not allowed to appear in a solution
f\colon X\to[m]
. There are obvious similarities between CSPs in the above sense and local colorings problems in the sense of Definition
2.6
, and, indeed, a CSP can be viewed as local coloring problem on an auxiliary graph (see §
5.D
for details).
Fix a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
. The
probability
\mathbb{P}[B]
of a constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
is defined by
\mathbb{P}[B]\,\coloneqq\,\frac{|B|}{m^{|\mathrm{dom}(B)|}}.
Notice that if we form a random coloring
f\colon X\to[m]
by assigning to each
x\in X
a color from
[m]
uniformly at random, then
\mathbb{P}[B]
equals the probability that
{{f}|{\mathrm{dom}(B)}}\in B
, i.e., that the constraint
B
is violated by
f
. The
neighborhood
of a constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
is the set
\mathrm{N}(B)\subset\mathscr{B}
given by
\mathrm{N}(B)\,\coloneqq\,\{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}\setminus\{B\}\,:\,\mathrm{dom}(B^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)\neq\varnothing\}.
The most widely used form of the LLL, called the
Symmetric LLL
, invokes the parameters
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\mathbb{P}[B]\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}|\mathrm{N}(B)|.
Theorem 2.17
(Symmetric LLL; see
[
AS00
, Corollary 5.1.2]
)
.
If
\mathscr{B}
is a CSP such that
(2.18)
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)\,\leqslant\,e^{-1},
where
e=2.71\ldots
is the base of the natural logarithm, then
\mathscr{B}
has a solution.
Theorem
2.17
is a special case of a stronger result, known as the
General LLL
, in which instead of bounding
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
uniformly, one establishes a more delicate—but somewhat less transparent—relationship between
\mathbb{P}[B]
and
|\mathrm{N}(B)|
for each constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
:
Theorem 2.19
(General LLL; see
[
AS00
, Theorem 5.1.1]
)
.
If
\mathscr{B}
is a CSP such that there is a function
\eta\colon\mathscr{B}\to[0,1)
satisfying
\mathbb{P}[B]\,\leqslant\,\eta(B)\prod_{B^{\prime}\in\mathrm{N}(B)}(1-\eta(B^{\prime})),\qquad\text{for all }B\in\mathscr{B},
then
\mathscr{B}
has a solution.
A standard calculation (see
[
AS00
, proof of Corollary 5.1.2]
) shows that the bound (
2.18
) implies the existence of a function
\eta
as in Theorem
2.19
, and hence the Symmetric LLL is indeed a special case of the General LLL.
We remark that, due to its origin in finite combinatorics, the LLL is often stated in the case when the ground set
X
is finite; however, the case of infinite
X
follows via a straightforward compactness argument (see, e.g.,
[
AS00
, proof of Theorem 5.2.2]
).
Several applications of the LLL in combinatorics and graph theory can be found in
[
AS00
,
MR02
]
. For most applications, the full power of the General LLL is not needed and the Symmetric LLL is sufficient. Furthermore, in many cases the bound (
2.18
) is quite far from being sharp: one can often prove that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
is at most
\exp(-\log^{1+\varepsilon}\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}))
or even
\exp(-\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})^{\varepsilon})
for some constant
\varepsilon>0
. For example, this usually happens when the upper bound on
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
is obtained via a concentration of measure argument.
2.C.2.
Measurable Symmetric LLL
If
X
is a standard Borel space, then
[X]^{<\infty}
also carries a natural standard Borel structure. Namely, a subset of
[X]^{<\infty}
is said to be Borel if and only if its preimage in
\bigsqcup_{k=0}^{\infty}X^{k}
under the map
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1})\mapsto\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}\}
is Borel. Next, for any standard Borel space
Y
, the set
[X\rightharpoonup Y]^{<\infty}
also becomes a standard Borel space when viewed as a Borel subset of
[X\times Y]^{<\infty}
by identifying each partial function
\varphi\in[X\rightharpoonup Y]^{<\infty}
with its graph, i.e., with the set
\{(x,\varphi(x))\,:\,x\in\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)\}
. Finally, since, by definition, every
(X,m)
-constraint is a finite set of partial functions with a common finite domain, the set of all
(X,m)
-constraints is a Borel subset of
[[X\rightharpoonup[m]]^{<\infty}]^{<\infty}
. Therefore, we may speak of
Borel CSPs
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
, i.e., Borel sets of
(X,m)
-constraints.
Given a Borel CSP, it is natural to ask whether it has a solution with some regularity properties. To that end, we establish the following measurable/Baire-measurable analog of Theorem
2.17
under a stronger polynomial bound on
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
:
Theorem 2.20
(Measurable Symmetric LLL)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a Borel CSP such that
(2.21)
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2^{-15}.
Assume additionally that
\sup\{|\mathrm{dom}(B)|\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\}<\infty
. Then the following conclusions hold:
(i)
If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on
X
, then
\mathscr{B}
has a
\mu
-measurable solution.
(ii)
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
X
, then
\mathscr{B}
has a
\tau
-Baire-measurable solution.
As mentioned earlier, one can often bound
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
from above by a super-polynomially small function of
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
, making Theorem
2.20
applicable. We call CSPs
\mathscr{B}
such that
\sup\{|\mathrm{dom}(B)|\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\}<\infty
bounded
. The boundedness assumption in Theorem
2.20
can likely be eliminated by a routine modification of the proof. However, we are not aware of any applications where this assumption is not satisfied, and so we elected to include it in order to make our arguments more transparent.
Theorem
2.20
is implied by Theorem
2.14
: a CSP
\mathscr{B}
can be encoded as a local coloring problem on an auxiliary graph (see §
5.D
for details), and Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
designed a randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm that finds solutions to such local coloring problems in a sublogarithmic number of rounds under a polynomial bound on
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
similar to (
2.21
). The actual bound that we use comes from a sharpened version of the Fischer–Ghaffari algorithm developed in
[
GHK18
]
by Ghaffari, Harris, and Kuhn. Together with Theorem
2.14
, their algorithm immediately yields Theorem
2.20
. The logic of our argument, however, goes in the opposite direction: We shall first prove Theorem
2.20
and then derive Theorem
2.14
from it. Nevertheless, our proof of Theorem
2.20
does invoke the Ghaffari–Harris–Kuhn algorithm, albeit in a more subtle way.

3.  Applications
3.A.  Colorings with the number of colors close to
\Delta
Recall that the
chromatic number
\chi(G)
of a graph
G
is the smallest cardinality of a set
Y
such that
G
admits a proper coloring
f\colon V(G)\to Y
. In the descriptive setting, one defines the
Borel chromatic number
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)
of a Borel graph
G
as the smallest cardinality of a standard Borel space
Y
such that
G
has a Borel proper coloring
f\colon V(G)\to Y
. Similarly, given a probability Borel measure
\mu
or a compatible Polish topology
\tau
on
V(G)
, the
\mu
-measurable chromatic number
\chi_{\mu}(G)
and the
\tau
-Baire-measurable chromatic number
\chi_{\tau}(G)
are defined to be the smallest cardinality of a standard Borel space
Y
such that
G
admits a
\mu
-measurable, resp.
\tau
-Baire-measurable, proper coloring
f\colon V(G)\to Y
. It is clear that
\chi(G)\leqslant\chi_{\mu}(G)
,
\chi_{\tau}(G)\leqslant\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)
. We shall only work with bounded degree graphs, and for them all these parameters are finite:
Theorem 3.1
(Kechris–Solecki–Todorcevic
[
KST99
, Proposition 4.6]
)
.
Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
. Then
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta+1
.
In general, the bound
\chi(G)\leqslant\Delta(G)+1
is best possible, since a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
may contain
\Delta+1
pairwise adjacent vertices (i.e., a
(\Delta+1)
-clique
), all of which would have to receive distinct colors in a proper coloring of
G
. Conversely, a classical theorem of Brooks (see
[
Die17
, Theorem 5.2.4]
) asserts that if
\chi(G)=\Delta(G)+1
for a graph
G
with
3\leqslant\Delta(G)<\infty
, then
G
contains a
(\Delta(G)+1)
-clique.
It is natural to ask whether Brooks’s theorem can be extended to the setting of Borel, measurable, or Baire-measurable colorings. Marks
[
Mar16
]
showed that, in the Borel context, Brooks’s theorem fails in a very strong sense. A graph
G
is called
\Delta
-regular
if every vertex in
G
has degree
\Delta
, and
acyclic
if it contains no cycles (acyclic graphs are also known as
forests
). It is easy to see that acyclic graphs are bipartite, i.e., have chromatic number at most
2
. Nevertheless, Marks proved the following:
Theorem 3.2
(Marks
[
Mar16
]
)
.
For each
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists an acyclic
\Delta
-regular Borel graph
G
such that
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)=\Delta+1
.
In contrast to Theorem
3.2
, Conley, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CMT16
]
succeeded in extending Brooks’s theorem to the setting of measurable and Baire-measurable colorings:
Theorem 3.3
(Measurable Brooks; Conley–Marks–Tucker-Drob
[
CMT16
]
)
.
Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant 3
without a
(\Delta+1)
-clique.
(i)
If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
, then
\chi_{\mu}(G)\leqslant\Delta
.
(ii)
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
V(G)
, then
\chi_{\tau}(G)\leqslant\Delta
.
In view of Brooks’s theorem, an equivalent way of phrasing Theorem
3.3
is that a Borel graph
G
of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant 3
is measurably/Baire-measurably
\Delta
-colorable if and only if it is
\Delta
-colorable abstractly, i.e., without any regularity restrictions. Can this result be extended to colorings with fewer colors? We show that the answer is positive for
(\Delta-c)
-colorings, where
c
can be as large as roughly
\sqrt{\Delta}
:
Theorem 3.4.
There is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Fix integers
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and
c
. Let
G
be a Borel graph of maximum degree at most
\Delta
and let
\mu
(
resp.
\tau
)
be a probability Borel measure
(
resp. a compatible Polish topology
)
on
V(G)
. If
c\leqslant\sqrt{\Delta+1/4}-5/2
, then the following statements are equivalent:
(i)
\chi(G)\leqslant\Delta-c
;
(ii)
\chi_{\mu}(G)\leqslant\Delta-c
;
(iii)
\chi_{\tau}(G)\leqslant\Delta-c
.
Proof
.
Implications
(ii)
,
(iii)
\Longrightarrow
(i)
are trivial, so we only need to argue that
(i)
\Longrightarrow
(ii)
,
(iii)
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Pi(k)
denote the local coloring problem that encodes proper
k
-coloring of graphs (see Example
2.7
). Fix
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
with
c\leqslant\sqrt{\Delta+1/4}-5/2
and let
\mathsf{G}(\Delta,c)
be the set of all isomorphism classes of finite graphs

of maximum degree at most
\Delta
satisfying
\chi(G)\leqslant\Delta-c

\Delta
is large enough, Bamas and Esperet
[
BE19
, Theorem 1.3]
established the following bounds on the randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity of proper
(\Delta-c)

(3.5)
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi(\Delta-c),\mathsf{G}(\Delta,c)}(n)\,\leqslant\,\exp(O(\sqrt{\log\log n}))\,=\,o(\log n).
(Here the implicit constants in the asymptotic notation may depend on
\Delta
, which we treat as fixed.) If
G
is a Borel graph with
\Delta(G)\leqslant\Delta
and
\chi(G)\leqslant\Delta-c
, then every finite induced subgraph of
G
is in
\mathsf{G}(\Delta,c)

2.14

is both measurably and Baire-measurably

∎
Note that if in the statement of Theorem
3.4
we additionally assume that

is of subexponential growth, then, replacing Theorem
2.14
by Theorem

are also equivalent to

\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta-c
.
Marks’s Theorem
3.2
shows that the subexponential growth assumption cannot be removed.
The distributed algorithm of Bamas and Esperet
[
BE19

MR14

The bound on

Proposition 3.6.
Let

\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\Delta\geqslant k\geqslant 2
and
\Delta-k\geqslant\sqrt{\Delta-3/4}-1/2

and a probability Borel measure
\mu
(
resp. a compatible Polish topology
\tau
)

\Delta(G)\leqslant\Delta

\chi(G)\leqslant k

(

\chi_{\tau}(G)>k

Proof
.
We adapt the proof of
[
EHK98
, Theorem 1.4]

G
of finite maximum degree with a probability Borel measure
\mu
on
V(G)
such that
\chi(G)=k
but
\chi_{\mu}(G)>k
. For instance, let
(X,\mu)

\Delta\geqslant k

\Delta-k\geqslant\sqrt{\Delta-3/4}-1/2

\mu
on

such that
\chi(G)\leqslant k
,
\chi_{\mu}(G)>k
, and
\Delta(G)

3.B.  Graphs without short cycles
The distinctions between the three regularity notions—“Borel,” “measurable,” and “Baire

measurable”—are clearly demonstrated by colorings of acyclic graphs. Recall that, by Marks’s Theorem

, the Borel chromatic number of an acyclic Borel graph of maximum degree

\Delta+1
. In contrast to this, the Baire-measurable chromatic number of a locally finite acyclic graph is always at most

, which is a consequence of the following general result of Conley and Miller:
Theorem 3.8
(Conley–Miller
[
CM16
]
)
.
Let
G
be a locally finite Borel graph and let
\tau
be a compatible Polish topology on
V(G)
. If
\chi(G)
is finite, then
\chi_{\tau}(G)\leqslant 2\chi(G)-1
.
Since
\chi(G)\leqslant 2
for an acyclic graph
G
, Theorem
3.8
implies that the Baire-measurable chromatic number of an acyclic locally finite Borel graph is indeed at most
2\cdot 2-1=3
. It is also not hard to see that this upper bound is best possible; see, e.g.,
[
CMT16
, §6]
.
Now we turn to measurable colorings. Lyons and Nazarov
[
LN11
]
(see also
[
KM19
, Theorem 5.46]
) constructed acyclic Borel graphs with maximum degree
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
and measurable chromatic number at least
(1/2+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta
. This shows that acyclic Borel graphs of bounded degree can have arbitrarily large measurable chromatic numbers, contrary to the situation with Baire-measurable colorings. Although the present author showed
[
Ber19
, Corollary 1.2]
that
\Theta(\Delta/\log\Delta)
is the correct order of magnitude in the Lyons–Nazarov examples, the best heretofore known general upper bound on measurable chromatic numbers of acyclic graphs in terms of their maximum degree was
\Delta
, which is a consequence of the measurable Brooks’s Theorem
3.3
of Conley–Marks–Tucker-Drob. Here we improve this to
(1+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta
, which falls within a factor of
2
of best possible. Furthermore, we do not even require acyclicity—it suffices to only forbid cycles of length at most
4
.
Theorem 3.9.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
.
(i)
If
G
contains no cycles of length at most
4
, then
\chi_{\mu}(G)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta
.
(ii)
If
G
contains no cycles of length
3
, then
\chi_{\mu}(G)\leqslant(4+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta
.
Proof
.
To derive this theorem, we invoke the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms for graph coloring developed by Chung, Pettie, and Su in
[
CPS17
]
. We shall first prove
(ii)
. For each
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Pi(k)
denote the local coloring problem that encodes proper
k
-coloring of graphs (see Example
2.7
). Fix
\varepsilon>0
and
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
and set
k\coloneqq\lfloor(4+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta\rfloor
. Let
\mathsf{G}(\Delta)
be the set of all isomorphism classes of finite graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
with no
3
-cycles. It follows from
[
CPS17
, Theorem 9]
that there exist positive real numbers
C
and
\delta
, depending only on
\varepsilon
, such that, for all large enough
\Delta
and
n
,
(3.10)
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi(k),\mathsf{G}(\Delta)}(n)\,\leqslant\,C\Delta^{-\delta}\log n.
Even though this upper bound is weaker than
o(\log n)
, it still suffices for an application of Theorem
2.14
. Indeed, let
G
be a Borel graph of maximum degree
\Delta
without cycles of length
3
and set
R(n)\coloneqq\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi(k),\mathsf{G}(\Delta)}(n)+1
. If
\Delta
is large enough and
n
is much larger than
\Delta
, then for each vertex
x\in V(G)
,
|B_{G}(x,2R(n))|\,\leqslant\,1+\Delta^{2C\Delta^{-\delta}\log n+2}\,=\,1+\exp(2C\Delta^{-\delta}\log\Delta\log n+2\log\Delta)\,<\,n^{1/8}/4.
Similarly,
|B_{G}(x,R(n))|<n
, and, since
\mathsf{G}(\Delta)
is closed under adding isolated vertices,
G
is
(R(n),n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}(\Delta)
.
Hence, Theorem
2.14
allows us to conclude that
G
is measurably
k
-colorable, which proves
(ii)
. The proof of
(i)
is virtually the same, except that
[
CPS17
, Theorem 9]
is replaced by the randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm of Chung, Pettie, and Su for
((1+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta)
-coloring graphs without cycles of length at most
4
(see the remark in
[
CPS17
]
after
[
CPS17
, Theorem 9]
).
∎
Again, if
G
is of subexponential growth, then, due to Theorem
2.15
, the upper bounds on
\chi_{\mu}(G)
given by Theorem
3.9
also hold for
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)
.
We remark that, although this was not needed in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, the bound
(
3.10
) can actually be improved to
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi(k),\mathsf{G}(\Delta)}(n)=o(\log n)
. Indeed, the algorithm developed by Chung, Pettie, and Su in order to prove (
3.10
) invokes as a subroutine a certain distributed version of the LLL. Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
later designed a more efficient distributed version of the LLL, and using their result reduces the complexity of the Chung–Pettie–Su algorithm to
o(\log n)
.
The upper bound
\chi(G)\leqslant(1+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta
for finite graphs
G
without cycles of length at most
4
is due to Kim
[
Kim95
]
. The existence of a constant
C>0
such that
\chi(G)\leqslant(C+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta
for finite graphs
G
without
3
-cycles was first established by Johansson
[
Joh96
]
(Johansson’s original paper is hard to access, but a detailed presentation of his argument can be found in
[
MR02
, §13]
). Johansson’s original proof gave the value
C=9
. Pettie and Su
[
PS15
]
improved this to
C=4
(which is the value appearing in Theorem
3.9
). Recently, Molloy
[
Mol19
]
further reduced the constant to
C=1
. We leave the question of whether Molloy’s result also holds for measurable colorings as an open problem:
Open Problem 3.11
.
Is it true that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property? Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
. If
G
contains no cycles of length
3
, then
\chi_{\mu}(G)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\Delta/\log\Delta
.
One way to solve Problem
3.11
would be to develop a sublogarithmic randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for
((1+o(1))\Delta/\log\Delta)
-coloring graphs without
3
-cycles.
3.C.  A result on list-coloring
In this section we apply Theorem
2.14
to obtain a useful result concerning list-colorings of Borel graphs. For an introduction to the theory of list-coloring, see
[
Die17
, §5.4]
. A
list assignment
for a graph
G
is a function
L\colon V(G)\to[{\mathbb{N}}]^{<\infty}
. For each vertex
x\in V(G)
, the set
L(x)
is called the
list
of
x
, and the elements of
L(x)
are the colors
available
to
x
. An
L
-coloring
of
G
is a map
f\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
such that
f(x)\in L(x)
for all
x\in V(G)
. An
L
-coloring
f
is
proper
if
f(x)\neq f(y)
whenever
x
and
y
are adjacent in
G
. We say that
L
is an
(\ell,d)
-list assignment
if it has the following two properties:
—
for all
x\in V(G)
,
|L(x)|\geqslant\ell
; and
—
for all
x\in V(G)
and
\alpha\in L(x)
,
|\{y\in N_{G}(x)\,:\,\alpha\in L(y)\}|\leqslant d
.
The following is a result of Reed and Sudakov:
Theorem 3.12
(Reed–Sudakov
[
RS02
]
)
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
d_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a graph and let
L
be an
(\ell,d)
-list assignment for
G
, where
d\geqslant d_{0}
and
\ell\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)d
. Then
G
admits a proper
L
-coloring.
A version of Theorem
3.12
with the bound
\ell\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)d
replaced by
\ell\geqslant 2ed
was first proved by Reed
[
Ree99
]
; this was then improved by Haxell
[
Hax01
]
to
\ell\geqslant 2d
. While it is weaker than Theorem
3.12
for large
d
, Haxell’s result holds for all positive integers
d
and not only for sufficiently large ones. Reed
[
Ree99
]
conjectured that in fact
\ell\geqslant d+1
should suffice, but this conjecture was refuted by Bohman and Holzman
[
BH02
]
.
Theorem
3.12
(or its weaker versions mentioned in the previous paragraph) is a somewhat technical but rather useful fact that plays a crucial role in the proofs of many graph coloring results (see
[
MR02
]
for a number of examples). Here we establish a version of Theorem
3.12
for measurable and Baire-measurable colorings:
Theorem 3.13.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is
d_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a Borel graph and let
L
be a Borel
(\ell,d)
-list assignment for
G
, where
d\geqslant d_{0}
and
\ell\geqslant(1+\varepsilon)d
. Let
\mu
(
resp.
\tau
)
be a probability Borel measure
(
resp. a compatible Polish topology
)
on
V(G)
. Then
G
admits a
\mu
-measurable
(
resp.
\tau
-Baire-measurable
)
proper
L
-coloring.
Proof
.
We shall use another
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm due to Chung, Pettie, and Su
[
CPS17
]
. As explained in Example
2.4
, we can interpret pairs
(G,L)
, where
G
is a graph and
L
a list assignment for
G
, as structured graphs in the sense of Definition
2.1
. Furthermore, proper list-coloring can naturally be encoded as a local coloring problem
\Pi
. Explicitly, let
\mathcal{P}\colon\mathsf{FSG}_{\bullet}\to\{0,1\}
be the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm defined as follows: Given (the isomorphism type of) a finite rooted structured graph of the form
(G,L,f,x)
with
L
a list assignment for
G
and
f\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
, set
\mathcal{P}([G,L,f,x])\coloneqq 1
if and only if
f
is a proper
L
-coloring of
G
; in all other cases set
\mathcal{P}
to
0
. Now if we let
\Pi\coloneqq(1,\mathcal{P})
, then a
\Pi
-coloring of
(G,L)
is precisely the same as a proper
L
-coloring of
G
.
Let
\mathsf{G}(\ell,d)\subset\mathsf{FSG}
denote the set of all isomorphism types of finite structured graphs corresponding to pairs of the form
(G,L)
, where
G
is a finite graph and
L
is an
(\ell,d)
-list assignment for
G
. The result of Chung, Pettie, and Su presented in
[
CPS17
, §4.4]
implies that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exist positive real numbers
C
and
\delta
such that, for all large enough
d
and
n
,
(3.14)
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}((1+\varepsilon)d,d)}(n)\,\leqslant\,Cd^{-\delta}\log n.
As in the proof of Theorem
3.9
, this upper bound is weaker than
o(\log n)
but sufficient for an application of Theorem
2.14
. Indeed, let
G
be a Borel graph and let
L
be a Borel
((1+\varepsilon)d,d)
-list assignment for
G
, where
d
is a large positive integer. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
|L(x)|=\ell
for all
x\in V(G)
, and, by removing from
G
all the edges
xy
such that
L(x)\cap L(y)=\varnothing
, we may arrange that
\Delta(G)\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)d^{2}\leqslant d^{3}
. Set
R(n)\coloneqq\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}((1+\varepsilon)d,d)}(n)+1
. For all
x\in V(G)
,
|B_{G}(x,2R(n))|\,\leqslant\,1+d^{6Cd^{-\delta}\log n+6}\,=\,1+\exp(6Cd^{-\delta}\log d\log n+6\log d)\,<\,n^{1/8}/4,
where the last inequality holds whenever
d
and
n
are large enough. Similarly,
|B_{G}(x,R(n))|<n
, and hence the pair
(G,L)
is
(R(n),n)
-locally in
\mathsf{G}((1+\varepsilon)d,d)
. Therefore, by Theorem
2.14
,
G
admits a measurable/Baire-measurable proper
L
-coloring, as desired.
∎
Again, for graphs of subexponential growth, we can use Theorem
2.15
to upgrade the conclusion of Theorem
3.13
to a Borel proper
L
-coloring. Like (
3.10
), the bound (
3.14
) can be improved to
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathsf{G}((1+\varepsilon)d,d)}(n)=o(\log n)
by using the Fischer–Ghaffari distributed LLL
[
FG17
]
.
3.D.  Sparse graphs
Among the oldest topics in graph theory is studying colorings of planar graphs. For the purposes of this paper, we say that an infinite graph is planar if all its finite subgraphs are planar. One of the most celebrated results in graph theory is the
Four Color Theorem
of Appel and Haken
[
Die17
, Theorem 5.1.1]
that asserts that
\chi(G)\leqslant 4
for all planar graphs
G
. Furthermore, if
G
is a planar graph without
3
-cycles, then
\chi(G)\leqslant 3
—this is a theorem of Grötzsch
[
Die17
, Theorem 5.1.3]
. On the other hand, the Lyons–Nazarov examples
[
LN11
]
mentioned in §
3.B
(see also
[
KM19
, Theorem 5.46]
) show that bounded degree acyclic (hence planar) Borel graphs can have arbitrarily large measurable chromatic numbers. Nevertheless, we show that under the additional assumption of subexponential growth, Borel chromatic numbers of planar Borel graphs are bounded by
5
, and can be further lowered for graphs without short cycles:
Theorem 3.15.
Let
G
be a planar Borel graph of subexponential growth and define
k\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}3&\text{if $G$ contains no cycles of length at most $4$};\\
4&\text{if $G$ contains a $4$-cycle but no $3$-cycles};\\
5&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
Then
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant k
. Furthermore, if
G
is also a topological graph
(
in the sense of Definition
2.12
)
, then
G
admits a continuous proper
k
-coloring.
Proof
.
This is a consequence of the recent work of Postle
[
Pos19
]
.
Let
\mathsf{P}
denote the set of all isomorphism classes of finite planar graphs and let
\mathsf{P}(g)\subseteq\mathsf{P}
be the set of all isomorphism classes of finite planar graphs without cycles of length strictly less than
g
. Letting
\Pi(k)
denote the local coloring problem corresponding to proper
k
-coloring (see Example
2.7
), Postle
[
Pos19
, Theorem 1.3]
established the following bounds:
\max\{\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi(5),\mathsf{P}}(n),\,\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi(4),\mathsf{P}(4)}(n),\,\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi(3),\mathsf{P}(5)}(n)\}\,=\,O(\log n).
Combined with Theorems
2.10
and
2.13
, this yields the desired results. (Theorems
2.10
and
2.13
may be applied as graphs of subexponential growth have finite maximum degree.)
∎
An important feature of planar graphs is their sparsity: by Euler’s formula, a planar graph
G
with
n\geqslant 3
vertices can have at most
3n-6
edges. A convenient measure of sparsity for an arbitrary graph is its
arboricity
, defined as follows. Recall that
|G|
and
\|G\|
are the cardinalities of the vertex and the edge sets of
G
, respectively. The
arboricity
of a graph
G
with
|G|\geqslant 2
is the quantity
a(G)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{H}\left\lceil\frac{\|H\|}{|H|-1}\right\rceil,
where the supremum is taken over all the finite subgraphs
H
of
G
with
|H|\geqslant 2
. For graphs
G
with
|G|\leqslant 1
,
a(G)\coloneqq 0
by definition. A theorem of Nash-Williams
[
Die17
, Theorem 2.4.4]
asserts that if
G
is a finite graph, then
a(G)
is equal to the smallest
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G
has
k
acyclic subgraphs
F_{1}
, …,
F_{k}
with
E(G)=E(F_{1})\cup\ldots\cup E(F_{k})
. Since acyclic graphs are also called “forests,” this explains the term “arboricity.”
Many classes of graphs have bounded arboricity. For instance, all planar graphs have arboricity at most
3
. It is not hard to see that graphs
G
of finite arboricity satisfy
\chi(G)\leqslant 2a(G)
(and this bound is, in general, best possible). On the other hand, the measurable chromatic number of a bounded degree acyclic (i.e., arboricity
1
) Borel graph can be arbitrarily large. As in the case of planar graphs, the situation improves under the subexponential growth assumption:
Theorem 3.16.
If
G
is a Borel graph of subexponential growth, then
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant 2a(G)+1
. Furthermore, if
G
is also a topological graph, then
G
admits a continuous proper
(2a(G)+1)
-coloring.
Proof
.
Let
\mathsf{G}(a)
denote the set of all isomorphism classes of finite graphs of arboricity at most
a
, and let
\Pi(k)
be the local coloring problem corresponding to proper
k
-coloring. Barenboim and Elkin
[
BE10
, §4]
proved that for all
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi(2a+1),\mathsf{G}(a)}\,=\,O(\log n),
where the implicit constants in the asymptotic notation may depend on
a
. (The statement in
[
BE10
, §4]
is more general and involves an additional positive parameter
\varepsilon
; the bound that we need is obtained by setting
\varepsilon=1/(a+1)
.) It remains to apply Theorems
2.10
and
2.13
. ∎
The bound
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant 2a(G)+1
for Borel graphs
G
of subexponential growth is, in general, sharp, since there exist acyclic
2
-regular Borel graphs
G
with
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)=3
, and such graphs are of subexponential (in fact, linear) growth; see, e.g.,
[
CMT16
, §6]
. Nevertheless, we conjecture that acyclic graphs are an exception and the bound
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant 2a(G)
should hold whenever
a(G)\geqslant 2
:
Conjecture 3.17.
If
G
is a Borel graph of subexponential growth, then
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant\max\{2a(G),3\}
.
As evidence for Conjecture
3.17
, we show that it holds for graphs whose order of growth is somewhat lower than just subexponential:
Theorem 3.18.
Let
G
be a Borel graph and suppose that for each
\varepsilon>0
, there is
R_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that if
x\in V(G)
and
R\geqslant R_{0}
, then
|B_{G}(x,R)|<\exp(\varepsilon R^{1/3})
. Then
\chi_{\mathrm{B}}(G)\leqslant\max\{2a(G),3\}
. Furthermore, if
G
is also a topological graph, then
G
admits a continuous proper
\max\{2a(G),3\}
-coloring.
Proof
.
As in the proof of Theorem
3.16
, let
\mathsf{G}(a)
be the set of all isomorphism classes of finite graphs of arboricity at most
a
, and let
\Pi(k)
be the local coloring problem encoding proper
k
-coloring. Aboulker, Bonamy, Bousquet, and Esperet
[
Abo+19
, Corollary 1.4]
showed that for
a\geqslant 2
,
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi(2a),\mathsf{G}(a)}=O((\log n)^{3})
where the implicit constants in the asymptotic notation again depend on
a
. Now a straightforward computation shows that Theorems
2.10
and
2.13
yield the desired results.
∎
Another result about graphs of subexponential growth that should be mentioned here is a theorem of Gao and Jackson
[
GJ15
, Theorem 4.2]
that asserts that for every
d\geqslant 2
, the Schreier graph of the free part of the Bernoulli shift action
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\curvearrowright\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{Z}^{d}}
admits a continuous proper
4
-coloring. This fact can be alternatively derived by using Theorem
2.13
in combination with the deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for
4
-coloring grid graphs designed by Brandt et al.
[
Bra+17
, Theorem 4]
.
3.E.  A pointwise version of the Abért–Weiss theorem
So far we have described a number of results in descriptive combinatorics that can be obtained using distributed algorithms. Now we present a direct application of the
Measurable Symmetric LLL
to a question in ergodic theory.
Throughout §
3.E
,
\Gamma
shall denote a countably infinite group. We are interested in
probability measure
-
preserving
(
p.m.p.
)
actions
of
\Gamma
, i.e., actions of the form
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
, where
(X,\mu)
is a standard probability space and the measure
\mu
is
\alpha
-invariant. More generally, we consider
Borel actions
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright X
, i.e., actions of
\Gamma
on a standard Borel space
X
by Borel automorphisms. An action
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright X
is
free
if the
\alpha
-stabilizer of every point
x\in X
is trivial.
An important example of a
p.m.p.
action is the Bernoulli shift action
\sigma\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright([0,1]^{\Gamma},\lambda^{\Gamma}),
where
([0,1],\lambda)
is the unit interval equipped with the Lebesgue probability measure
\lambda
(owing to the measure isomorphism theorem
[
Kec95
, Theorem 17.41]
, any other atomless standard probability space could be used instead). For brevity, we write
\Omega\coloneqq[0,1]^{\Gamma}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\bm{\lambda}\coloneqq\lambda^{\Gamma}
(this notation will only be used in §
3.E
). Our starting point is a result of Abért and Weiss:
Theorem 3.19
(Abért–Weiss
[
AW13
]
)
.
Fix the following data:
—
a partition
\Omega=A_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup A_{k}
of
\Omega
into finitely many Borel pieces;
—
a finite set
F\in[\Gamma]^{<\infty}
; and
—
\varepsilon>0
.
For every free
p.m.p.
action
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
, there is a Borel partition
X=B_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup B_{k}
such that
\mu(B_{i}\cap\gamma\cdot B_{j})\,\approx_{\varepsilon}\,\bm{\lambda}(A_{i}\cap\gamma\cdot A_{j}),
for all
1\leqslant i
,
j\leqslant k
and all
\gamma\in F
.
Here and in what follows, we write
a\approx_{\varepsilon}b
to mean
|a-b|<\varepsilon
. Theorem
3.19
can be stated briefly as “The shift action
\sigma\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright(\Omega,\bm{\lambda})
is
weakly contained
in every free
p.m.p.
action
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright(X,\mu)
.” The relation of weak containment was introduced by Kechris in
[
Kec10
, §10(C)]
. For more details and further background on this topic, see the survey
[
BK20
]
by Burton and Kechris.
Here we strengthen Theorem
3.19
by replacing the quantities
\mu(B_{i}\cap\gamma\cdot B_{j})
with certain pointwise averages almost everywhere:
Theorem 3.20.
Fix the following data:
—
a partition
\Omega=A_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup A_{k}
of
\Omega
into finitely many Borel pieces;
—
a finite set
F\in[\Gamma]^{<\infty}
; and
—
\varepsilon>0
.
Then there is
n_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
with the following property. Fix a finite set
D\in[\Gamma]^{<\infty}
of size
|D|\geqslant n_{0}
and let
\alpha\colon\Gamma\curvearrowright X
be a free Borel action of
\Gamma
. For every probability Borel measure
\mu
on
X
, there exists a Borel partition
X=B_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup B_{k}
such that
(3.21)
\frac{|\{\delta\in D\,:\,\delta\cdot x\in B_{i}\cap\gamma\cdot B_{j}\}|}{|D|}\,\approx_{\varepsilon}\,\bm{\lambda}(A_{i}\cap\gamma\cdot A_{j}),
for all
1\leqslant i
,
j\leqslant k
, all
\gamma\in F
, and
\mu
-almost all
x\in X
.
Note that in Theorem
3.20
, the measure
\mu
is not required to be
\alpha
-invariant. If
\mu
is
\alpha
-invariant, then the partition

4.A.  Proof of Theorem

We start with the following simple observation, which will be used repeatedly throughout §§

Lemma 4.1.
If
\bm{G}
is a locally finite Borel structured graph, then the function

(

Proof
.
We need to argue that for every finite rooted structured graph

, the set

is Borel. Since

is locally finite, by the Feldman–Moore theorem
[
KM04
, Theorem 1.3]
, there exist Borel involutions
\gamma_{i}\colon V(\bm{G})\to V(\bm{G})
,
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
, such that
{\mathrm{dist}}_{\bm{G}}(x,y)<\infty
if and only if
y=\gamma_{i}(x)
for some
i\in{\mathbb{N}}

\gamma_{0}
is the identity map on

. For all

,

such that

denote the minimum

). Now, for any

if and only if there exists a partial function

such that:

\varphi
is a bijection between

and

;
—

, we have

if and only if

;
—
the mapping
\gamma_{i}(x)\mapsto\varphi(i)
is an isomorphism of the structured graphs

and

Since the set

is countable, it follows that the set (

Lemma 4.4.

5.  Proof of the
Measurable Symmetric LLL
5.A.  Proof outline
In this section we outline our strategy for proving Theorem
2.20
. Say that
\mathscr{B}
is an
(N,\varepsilon)
-CSP
if
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{N}\,\leqslant\,\varepsilon.
We shall describe here the main steps in proving that Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSPs have measurable solutions; the Baire-measurable case is similar but differs in some technical aspects.
An important role in our argument is played by the notion of a
reduction
between CSPs. Roughly speaking, a CSP
\mathscr{B}
is reducible to a CSP
\mathscr{C}
if there is a “local rule” that transforms any solution to
\mathscr{C}
into a solution to
\mathscr{B}
; thus, to solve
\mathscr{B}
, it suffices to solve
\mathscr{C}
. The precise definition of what we mean by a “local rule” here is given in Definitions
5.1
and
5.4
; it has some similarities and is closely related to the notion of a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm.
The first key ingredient in the proof of Theorem
2.20
is a “bootstrapping lemma” (Lemma
5.11
), which asserts that if
\mathscr{B}
is an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP, then
\mathscr{B}
is reducible to an
(N,\varepsilon)
-CSP
\mathscr{C}
for
any
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
. The proof of this fact involves the bootstrapping technique developed by Fischer and Ghaffari in
[
FG17
, §3.2]
, which in turn is based on the work of Chang and Pettie
[
CP19
]
on the randomized time hierarchy for the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model. A CSP
\mathscr{B}
can be interpreted as a local coloring problem on an auxiliary graph (see §
5.D
for details). Ghaffari, Harris, and Kuhn
[
GHK18
]
showed that if
\mathscr{B}
is an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP, then the randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity of the corresponding local coloring problem is
o(\log n)
(the work of Ghaffari, Harris, and Kuhn builds on the earlier breakthrough of Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
who obtained the same conclusion for
(32,e^{-32})
-CSPs). Our “bootstrapping lemma” follows by combining the Ghaffari–Harris–Kuhn result with Lemma
4.6
.
Next we need to discuss partial solutions to CSPs. Given a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
, a
partial solution
to
\mathscr{B}
is a partial map
g\colon X\rightharpoonup[m]
that can be extended to a full solution
f\colon X\to[m]
. Given a partial solution
g
to
\mathscr{B}
, the problem of extending
g
to a full solution can naturally be encoded as a CSP on
X\setminus\mathrm{dom}(g)
; we denote this CSP by
\mathscr{B}/g
(see §
5.C
for the definition). The second key ingredient in our proof is Lemma
5.12
, which says, roughly, that if
N
and
1/\varepsilon
are large enough and
\mathscr{B}
is a Borel
(N,\varepsilon)
-CSP on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
, then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel partial solution
g
such that
\mathrm{dom}(g)
has measure at least, say,
1/2
and the CSP
\mathscr{B}/g
satisfies
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B}/g)\lessapprox\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})}
. Arguments similar to Lemma
5.12
have been used by Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
and Molloy and Reed
[
MR98
]
and ultimately go back to the seminal work of Beck
[
Bec91
]
.
Now we can sketch the overall flow of the proof. We are given a Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP
\mathscr{B}
on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
. Using Lemma
5.11
, we “bootstrap”
\mathscr{B}
to an
(N,\varepsilon)
-CSP, where
N
,
1/\varepsilon\gg 1
. We then apply Lemma
5.12
to get a Borel partial solution
g_{0}
to
\mathscr{B}
such that
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{0}))\geqslant 1/2
and
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B}/g_{0})
is still very small; in particular, by making
N
and
1/\varepsilon
sufficiently large, we may arrange
\mathscr{B}/g_{0}
to be reducible to an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP (see Lemma
5.18
). We then repeat the same steps with
\mathscr{B}/g_{0}
in place of
\mathscr{B}
and obtain a Borel partial solution
g_{1}
to
\mathscr{B}/g_{0}
such that
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{1}))\geqslant(1-\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{0})))/2
and
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup g_{1})
is again reducible to an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP. After countably many such iterations, we will have constructed a sequence
g_{0}
,
g_{1}
, … of Borel functions such that
g\coloneqq g_{0}\cup g_{1}\cup\ldots
is a partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
with
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g))=1
. Since
g
is a partial solution, it can be extended to a full solution
f
, and any such
f
is
\mu
-measurable, as desired.
5.B.  Reductions between CSPs
For sets
A
and
B
, we use
[A\rightharpoonup B]
to denote the set of all partial functions
A\rightharpoonup B
. For functions
f
,
g
, the notation
g\subseteq f
means that
f
is an extension of
g
, i.e.,
\mathrm{dom}(g)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(f)
and
g={{f}|{\mathrm{dom}(g)}}
. The following definitions are crucial for our proof of Theorem
2.20
.
Definition 5.1
(Connections)
.
An
(X,Y)
-connection
, where
X
and
Y
are sets, is a mapping
\rho\colon[Y\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}]\to[X\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}]
that is monotone in the sense that if

and

, then
\rho(g)\subseteq\rho(f)
as well. We say that a set
S\subseteq Y
\rho
-determines
an element

if for all
f\colon Y\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
,
(5.2)
\rho(f)(x)\,=\,\rho({{f}|{S}})(x).
When one side of (
5.2
) is undefined, we interpret the equality to mean that the other side is undefined as well. The
width
of an
(X,Y)
-connection

is the quantity
\mathsf{w}(\rho)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{x\in X}\,\inf\{|S|\,:\,\text{$S\subseteq Y$ is a set that $\rho$-determines $x$}\}.
An
(X,Y)
-connection

is

if
\mathsf{w}(\rho)
is finite.
Lemma/Definition 5.3
(
S_{\rho}(x)
)
.
Let
\rho
be a local
(X,Y)
-connection. Then for each
x\in X
, there is a unique set
S_{\rho}(x)\subseteq Y
such that
S\subseteq Y

-determines
x
if and only if
S\supseteq S_{\rho}(x)
.
Proof
.
This is a consequence of the fact that if
S_{1}
,
S_{2}\subseteq Y
are sets that
\rho
-determine

, then
S_{1}\cap S_{2}
also
\rho
-determines
x
, which holds since for any
f\colon Y\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}

\rho(f)(x)\,=\,\rho({{f}|{S_{1}}})(x)\,=\,\rho({{({{f}|{S_{1}}})}|{S_{2}}})(x)\,=\,\rho({{f}|{(S_{1}\cap S_{2})}})(x).\qed
Definition 5.4
(Reductions)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
and
\mathscr{C}\colon Y\to^{?}[n]
be CSPs. A
reduction
from
\mathscr{B}
to
\mathscr{C}
is a local
(X,Y)
-connection
\rho
such that for all
f\colon Y\to[n]
,
\text{$f$ is a solution to $\mathscr{C}$}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\text{$\rho(f)$ is a solution to $\mathscr{B}$}.
The
degree
of a reduction
\rho
is the quantity
\mathsf{d}(\rho)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{x\in X}\,|\{C\in\mathscr{C}\,:\,\mathrm{dom}(C)\cap S_{\rho}(x)\neq\varnothing\}|.
If there is a reduction from
\mathscr{B}
to
\mathscr{C}
, then we say that
\mathscr{B}
is
reducible
to
\mathscr{C}
and write
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
to indicate that
\rho
is a reduction from
\mathscr{B}
to
\mathscr{C}
. Note that every reduction
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
satisfies
\mathsf{d}(\rho)\,\leqslant\,\mathsf{w}(\rho)\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C})+1).
If
X
and
Y
are standard Borel spaces, we say that an
(X,Y)
-connection
\rho
is
Borel
if
\rho(f)\colon X\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
is a Borel function whenever
f\colon Y\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}
is Borel. If
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{C}
are Borel CSPs and there is a Borel reduction
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
, we say that
\mathscr{B}
is
Borel
-
reducible
to
\mathscr{C}
.
It is clear that the (Borel-)reducibility relation is reflexive: the identity map
[X\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}]\to[X\rightharpoonup{\mathbb{N}}]
is a reduction from any CSP
\mathscr{B}
on
X
to itself (the width of this reduction is
1
). It is not hard to see that this relation is also transitive. Indeed, we have the following:
Lemma 5.5
(Transitivity)
.
If
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
and
\sigma\colon\mathscr{C}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{D}
are Borel reductions between Borel CSPs, then the composition
\rho\circ\sigma
is a Borel reduction from
\mathscr{B}
to
\mathscr{D}
such that
(5.6)
\mathsf{w}(\rho\circ\sigma)\,\leqslant\,\mathsf{w}(\rho)\mathsf{w}(\sigma)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{d}(\rho\circ\sigma)\,\leqslant\,\mathsf{w}(\rho)\mathsf{d}(\sigma).
Proof
.
Let the given Borel CSPs
\mathscr{B}
,
\mathscr{C}
, and
\mathscr{D}
be on spaces
X
,
Y
, and
Z
respectively. It is clear that
\rho\circ\sigma
is a Borel
(X,Z)
-connection that sends solutions to
\mathscr{D}
to solutions to
\mathscr{B}
. It remains to verify inequalities (
5.6
) (the first of which implies that
\rho\circ\sigma
is local). To this end, notice that for any
x\in X
, the union
\bigcup\{S_{\sigma}(y)\,:\,y\in S_{\rho}(x)\}
(\rho\circ\sigma)
-determines
x
, has size at most
\mathsf{w}(\rho)\mathsf{w}(\sigma)
, and intersects at most
\mathsf{w}(\rho)\mathsf{d}(\sigma)
sets of the form

for

.

5.C.  Partial solutions
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a CSP and let
g\colon X\rightharpoonup[m]
be a partial function. We say that
g
is a
partial solution
to
\mathscr{B}
if
g
can be extended to a solution
f\colon X\to[m]
. Given a partial map

and

, let

be the constraint with domain
\mathrm{dom}(B/g)\coloneqq\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(g)

B/g\,\coloneqq\,\{\varphi\colon\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus\mathrm{dom}(g)\to[m]\,:\,\varphi\cup({{g}|{\mathrm{dom}(B)}})\in B\}.
In other words,
\varphi\in B/g
if and only if
\varphi\cup g
violates the constraint
B
. Note that if
\mathrm{dom}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(g)
, then
\mathrm{dom}(B/g)=\varnothing
; specifically,
B/g=\{\varnothing\}
if
g
violates
B
, and
B/g=\varnothing
otherwise. Let
\mathscr{B}/g\,\coloneqq\,\{B/g\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\}.
We view
\mathscr{B}/g
as a CSP on

. By construction,
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}/g)\leqslant\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
.
If the CSP
\mathscr{B}
and the partial map

are Borel, then the CSP
\mathscr{B}/g
is Borel as well. The following observation is immediate:
Lemma 5.7.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a CSP and let
g\colon X\rightharpoonup[m]
be a partial function. Then
g
is a partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
if and only if
\mathscr{B}/g
has a solution.
Proof
.
If
h\colon X\setminus\mathrm{dom}(g)\to[m]
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}/g
, then
g\cup h
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending
g
. Conversely, if
f
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending
g
, then
{{f}|{(X\setminus\mathrm{dom}(g))}}
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}/g
.
∎
Next we notice that a countable union of partial solutions is also a partial solution:
Lemma 5.8.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a CSP. If
(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
is a sequence such that for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
g_{i}
is a partial solution to
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
, then
\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}g_{i}
is a partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
.
Proof
.
It is clear that each finite union
g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i}
is a partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
, so let

be a solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending
g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i}
. Consider the product space

, where the topology on

is discrete. By Tychonoff’s theorem,
[m]^{X}
is compact. The set
\mathfrak{S}
of all solutions to
\mathscr{B}
is closed in

, and, for each

, the set
\mathfrak{E}_{i}
of all

is also closed. The functions

, certify that the collection

has the finite intersection property, so there is some

. This

is a solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending

Lemma 5.9.
Let

Let the given Borel CSPs
\mathscr{B}

and

5.D.  The LLL as a distributed problem
There are several natural and essentially equivalent ways of encoding a CSP as a local coloring problem on an auxiliary graph. For our purposes, the following formalism will be most convenient. A
graph-CSP
with range
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
is a pair
(G,\mathscr{B})
, where
G
is a graph and
\mathscr{B}\colon V(G)\to^{?}[m]
is a bounded CSP on
V(G)
such that
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})<\infty
, with the following property: If
x
,
y\in V(G)
are distinct vertices such that
x
,
y\in\mathrm{dom}(B)
for some
B\in\mathscr{B}
, then
x
and
y
are adjacent in
G
. A graph-CSP
(G,\mathscr{B})
can be naturally interpreted as a structured graph, where, for each tuple of vertices
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in V(G)^{<\infty}
, the structure contains the information about all the constraints
B\in\mathscr{B}
such that
\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. Formally, we construct a structure map
\sigma
on
G
as follows. A tuple
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})\in V(G)^{<\infty}
is in the domain of
\sigma
if and only if:
—
the vertices
x_{1}
, …,
x_{k}
are pairwise distinct; and
—
there is a constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
.
To compute
\sigma(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
, let
\iota\colon\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\to[k]
be given by
\iota(x_{i})\coloneqq i
. For each
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
, let
B^{\ast}
be the set of all maps
\varphi\colon[k]\to[m]
such that
\varphi\circ\iota\in B
, and let
\sigma(\bm{x})\,\coloneqq\,\{B^{\ast}\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\text{ and }\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}\}.
Since
\mathscr{B}
is bounded,
\sigma

\tau
is a reduction from
\mathscr{C}
to
\mathscr{D}
, in the obvious way. Namely, for each
C\in\mathscr{C}
, we let
C^{\ast}
be the
(Z,2)
-constraint with
\mathrm{dom}(C^{\ast})\coloneqq\mathrm{dom}(C)\times[N]
given by
C^{\ast}\coloneqq\{\varphi\colon\mathrm{dom}(C)\times[N]\to[2]\,:\,\tau(\varphi)\in C\}
,
and set
\mathscr{D}\coloneqq\{C^{\ast}\,:\,C\in\mathscr{C}\}
. Let
\sigma\coloneqq\rho\circ\tau
. By Lemma
5.5
,
\sigma
is a Borel reduction from
\mathscr{B}
to
\mathscr{D}
. We claim that the CSP
\mathscr{D}
and the reduction
\sigma
are as desired.
The equality
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{D})=\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C})
follows since for all
C\in\mathscr{C}
,
|\mathrm{N}(C^{\ast})|=|\mathrm{N}(C)|
. To see that
\mathsf{d}(\sigma)=\mathsf{d}(\rho)
, observe that for each
x\in X
, the set
S_{\rho}(x)\times[N]
\sigma
-determines
x
and
\mathrm{dom}(C^{\ast})\cap(S_{\rho}(x)\times[N])\neq\varnothing
if and only if
\mathrm{dom}(C)\cap S_{\rho}(x)\neq\varnothing
. Finally, to bound
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{D})
, let
C\in\mathscr{C}
and consider an arbitrary function
\psi\in C
. If
\varphi\colon\mathrm{dom}(C^{\ast})\to[2]
satisfies
\tau(\varphi)=\psi
, then
(\varphi(y,1),\ldots,\varphi(y,N))\in\xi^{-1}(\psi(y))
for all
y\in\mathrm{dom}(C)
. Hence there are at most
(1+\delta)2^{N}/n
possible values for the tuple
(\varphi(y,1),\ldots,\varphi(y,N))
, and so the number of such functions
\varphi
cannot exceed
((1+\delta)2^{N}/n)^{|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}
. Therefore,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[C^{\ast}]\,=\,\frac{|C^{\ast}|}{2^{|\mathrm{dom}(C^{\ast})|}}\,
\displaystyle=\,\frac{\sum_{\psi\in C}|\{\varphi\,:\,\tau(\varphi)=\psi\}|}{2^{N|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}}\,\leqslant\,\frac{1}{2^{N|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}}\cdot|C|\cdot\left(\frac{(1+\delta)2^{N}}{n}\right)^{|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}
\displaystyle=\,(1+\delta)^{|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}\cdot\frac{|C|}{n^{|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}}\,=\,(1+\delta)^{|\mathrm{dom}(C)|}\mathbb{P}[C]\,\leqslant\,(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{P}[C],
and thus
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{D})\leqslant(1+\varepsilon)\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})
, as desired.
∎
Now we can combine Lemmas
5.11
,
5.12
, and
5.17
to obtain the following:
Lemma 5.18.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP. If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on
X
, then
\mathscr{B}
admits a Borel partial solution
g\colon X\rightharpoonup[m]
such that
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g))\geqslant 1/2
and
\mathscr{B}/g
is again a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP.
Proof
.
Using Lemmas
5.11
and
5.17
, we can find a binary Borel
(16,2^{-32})
-CSP
\mathscr{C}
and a Borel reduction
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
such that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})\mathsf{d}(\rho)^{2}\leqslant 1/4
. Let
h
be a partial Borel solution to
\mathscr{C}
satisfying the conclusion of Lemma
5.12
and define
g\coloneqq\rho(h)
. By Lemma
5.9
,
g
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{B}/g
is Borel-reducible to
\mathscr{C}/h
. Thus, it suffices to verify that
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g))\geqslant 1/2
and
\mathscr{C}/h
is an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP. By the choice of
h
, we have
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g))\,\geqslant\,1-\mathsf{d}(\rho)\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}\,\geqslant\,1-1/2\,=\,1/2,
as desired. Furthermore,
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C}/h)\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C}/h)+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2\cdot\sqrt{2^{-32}}\,=\,2^{-15}.
Thus,
\mathscr{C}/h
is indeed an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP, and we are done.
∎
With Lemma
5.18
in hand, it is easy to finish the proof of Theorem
2.20
(i)
. Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP and let
\mu
be a probability Borel measure on
X
. Repeated applications of Lemma
5.18
produce a sequence
(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
such that for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
:
—
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
is a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP;
—
g_{i}
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
; and
—
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{i}))\geqslant(1-\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{0}))-\cdots-\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g_{i-1})))/2
.
Let
g\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}g_{i}
. Then
\mu(\mathrm{dom}(g))=1
. By Lemma
5.8
,
g
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
, so let
f
be any solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending
g
. Since
f
agrees with the Borel function
g
on a
\mu
-conull set,
f
is
\mu
-measurable, and the proof is complete.

′

4.C
(ii)
In the Baire category setting, instead of Lemma
5.18
we use the following:
Lemma 5.19.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP. Then there is a finite set
\mathcal{G}
of Borel partial solutions to
\mathscr{B}
such that:
—
\bigcup\{\mathrm{dom}(g)\,:\,g\in\mathcal{G}\}=X
; and
—
for every
g\in\mathcal{G}
,
\mathscr{B}/g
is a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP.
Proof
.
Using Lemmas
5.11
and
5.17
, we can find a binary Borel
(16,2^{-32})
-CSP
\mathscr{C}
and a Borel reduction
\rho\colon\mathscr{B}\rightsquigarrow\mathscr{C}
such that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})\mathsf{d}(\rho)^{2}\leqslant 1/4
. Using the construction from the proof of Lemma
5.12
, we obtain a natural number
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and an assignment to each sequence
w\in[2]^{N}
of a Borel partial solution
h_{w}
to
\mathscr{C}
with the following properties:
(i)
for all
w\in[2]^{N}
,
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C}/h_{w})\leqslant 2\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}
(Claim
.2
); and
(ii)
for every
x\in X
,
\mathbb{P}_{w}[x\in\mathrm{dom}(\rho(h_{w}))]\geqslant 1-\mathsf{d}(\rho)\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}
, where
\mathbb{P}_{w}
denotes probability with respect to a uniformly random choice of
w\in[2]^{N}
(equation (
5.16
)).
From
(i)
it follows that each
\mathscr{C}/h_{w}
is an
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP, since
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C}/h_{w})\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C}/h_{w})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}\cdot(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{C})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2\cdot\sqrt{2^{-32}}\,=\,2^{-15}.
Furthermore, from
(ii)
it follows that for all
x\in X
,
\mathbb{P}_{w}[x\in\mathrm{dom}(\rho(h_{w}))]\,\geqslant\,1-\mathsf{d}(\rho)\sqrt{\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{C})}\,\geqslant\,1-1/2\,=\,1/2.
In particular, for each
x\in X
, there is some
w\in[2]^{N}
such that
x\in\mathrm{dom}(\rho(h_{w}))
. By Lemma
5.9
, each
\rho(h_{w})
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{B}/\rho(h_{w})
is Borel-reducible to
\mathscr{C}/h_{w}
. Therefore, the set
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq\{\rho(h_{w})\,:\,w\in[2]^{N}\}
is as desired.
∎
We are now ready to prove Theorem
′
‣
4.C
(ii)
. Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}[m]
be a Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP and let
\mathcal{T}
be a countable set of compatible Polish topologies on
X
. For each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, fix a countable base
\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
consisting of nonempty open sets, and let
(\tau_{i},U_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
be an enumeration of all pairs
(\tau,U)
with
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
and
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
. Using Lemma
5.19
, we can recursively build a sequence
(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
such that for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
:
—
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
is a potential Borel
(8,2^{-15})
-CSP;
—
g_{i}
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
; and
—
the set
(\mathrm{dom}(g_{0})\cup\ldots\cup\mathrm{dom}(g_{i}))\cap U_{i}
is
\tau_{i}
-nonmeager.
Specifically, once
g_{0}
, …,
g_{i-1}
have been constructed, we define
g_{i}
as follows. Let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite set of Borel partial solutions to
\mathscr{B}/(g_{0}\cup\ldots\cup g_{i-1})
given by Lemma
5.19
. Then
U_{i}\,\subseteq\,\mathrm{dom}(g_{0})\cup\ldots\cup\mathrm{dom}(g_{i-1})\cup\bigcup\{\mathrm{dom}(g)\,:\,g\in\mathcal{G}\}.
Since
U_{i}
is nonempty and
\tau_{i}
-open, it is
\tau_{i}
-nonmeager, so
\mathrm{dom}(g)\cap U_{i}
must be
\tau_{i}
-nonmeager for some
g\in\{g_{0},\ldots,g_{i-1}\}\cup\mathcal{G}
. If
\mathrm{dom}(g_{j})\cap U_{i}
is
\tau_{i}
-nonmeager for some
0\leqslant j\leqslant i-1
, then
we can let
g_{i}\coloneqq\varnothing
, and otherwise
we can make
g_{i}
be any
g\in\mathcal{G}
such that
\mathrm{dom}(g)\cap U_{i}
is
\tau_{i}
-nonmeager.
Once we have built a sequence
(g_{i})_{i=0}^{\infty}
as above, we let
g\coloneqq\bigcup_{i=0}^{\infty}g_{i}
. By Lemma
5.8
,
g
is a Borel partial solution to
\mathscr{B}
. Furthermore, for each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
, the set
\mathrm{dom}(g)\cap U
is
\tau
-nonmeager for every
U\in\mathcal{U}_{\tau}
, which, by
[
Kec95
, Proposition 8.26]
, implies that
\mathrm{dom}(g)
is
\tau
-comeager. Now let
f
be any solution to
\mathscr{B}
extending
g
. For each
\tau\in\mathcal{T}
,
f
agrees with the Borel function
g
on a
\tau
-comeager set, and therefore
f
is
\tau
-Baire-measurable, which finishes the proof.
