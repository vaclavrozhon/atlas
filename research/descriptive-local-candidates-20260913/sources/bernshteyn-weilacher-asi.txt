Abstract.
Asymptotic separation index is a parameter that measures how easily a Borel graph can be approximated by its subgraphs with finite components. In contrast to the more classical notion of hyperfiniteness, asymptotic separation index is well-suited for combinatorial applications in the Borel setting. The main result of this paper is a Borel version of the Lovász Local Lemma—a powerful general-purpose tool in probabilistic combinatorics—under a finite asymptotic separation index assumption. As a consequence, we show that locally checkable labeling problems that are solvable by efficient randomized distributed algorithms admit Borel solutions on bounded degree Borel graphs with finite asymptotic separation index. From this we derive a number of corollaries, for example a Borel version of Brooks’s theorem for graphs with finite asymptotic separation index.
1.  Introduction and main results
1.1.  Borel combinatorics and the asymptotic separation index
This paper is a contribution to the area of
descriptive combinatorics
, which investigates classical combinatorial notions—such as colorings, matchings, etc.—from the standpoint of descriptive set theory. Before we proceed, let us review some basic notation. We use
{\mathbb{N}}
to denote the set of all non-negative integers and identify each
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the
q
-element set
q=\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,i<q\}
. For a set

and a natural number

, we let
[X]^{q}\,\coloneqq\,\{A\subseteq X\,:\,|A|=q\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}\,\coloneqq\,\{A\subseteq X\,:\,\text{$A$ is finite}\}.
All graphs in this paper are undirected and simple. In other words, a graph

consists of a vertex set

and an edge set

. When there is no possibility of confusion, we use the standard graph-theoretic convention and write

. Given

, an

Throughout the paper, proper coloring of graphs will be used as a motivating example of a combinatorial problem.
Definition 1.1
(Colorings and the chromatic number)
.
Let

*
*
*
Although there is a rich and interesting theory concerning colorings with infinitely many colors (see, e.g.,
[

,
KM20
,
Ges11
]
), we will only focus on the case of finitely many colors in this paper.
Descriptive combinatorics applies graph-theoretic ideas to the study of Borel graphs:
Definition 1.2
(Borel graphs)
.
A

is a graph
G
whose vertex set

is a standard Borel space and whose adjacency relation
\{(u,v)\in V(G)^{2}\,:\,uv\in E(G)\}
is a Borel subset of
V(G)^{2}
.
The reader is referred to
[
Kec95
,
Tse16
]
for descriptive set theory background, such as the definition of a standard Borel space
†
†
†
The reader unfamiliar with this terminology may assume that the space is, say, the unit interval

. This typically results in no loss of generality, thanks to the Borel isomorphism theorem

, Theorem 15.6]
.
, and to
[

,

]
for an overview of Borel graph theory. A useful observation is that if

for

and

also carry natural standard Borel structures (see §

), and

is a standard Borel space and

Since every countable subset of a standard Borel space is Borel, countable graphs are (trivially) Borel, and hence Definition

is only really interesting when

is uncountable. On the other hand, a large part of descriptive combinatorics deals with the case when

is

(or even

), i.e., when every vertex of

has countably (resp. finitely) many neighbors. Indeed, all graphs considered in this paper will be locally countable. Note that

is locally countable if and only if every connected component of

is countable. As a result, locally countable graphs studied in descriptive combinatorics usually have uncountably many components (this is in contrast to classical, finite combinatorics, where it is common to only focus on connected graphs).
The fundamental question of descriptive combinatorics may be formulated as follows:
Question 1.3
.
When can a combinatorial construction (for example, a proper
q
-coloring) be performed on a Borel graph
G
in a “constructive” fashion?
The word “constructive” here can mean different things depending on the context. The most basic interpretation is to require all functions, sets, etc. appearing in the construction to be Borel. By introducing additional structure on
V(G)
, this requirement may be relaxed. For instance, one may ask for a construction that is measurable with respect to a measure
\mu
on
V(G)
or Baire-measurable with respect to a compatible Polish topology
\tau
on
V(G)
. As a concrete example, we can apply this perspective to proper colorings by modifying Definition
1.1
as follows:
Definition 1.4
(Borel, measurable, Baire-measurable chromatic numbers)
.
Let
G
be a Borel graph. A proper
q
-coloring
f\colon V(G)\to q
of
G
is
Borel
if
f^{-1}(i)
is a Borel subset of
V(G)
for all
0\leqslant i<q
. The
Borel chromatic number
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)
of

is the smallest
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G
has a Borel proper
q
-coloring; if no such
q
exists, we set
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\coloneqq\infty
.
Similarly, given a probability Borel measure
\mu
or a compatible Polish topology
\tau
on
V(G)
, we say that a proper
q
-coloring
f\colon V(G)\to q
of
G
is
\mu
-measurable
or
\tau
-Baire-measurable
if
f^{-1}(i)
is a
\mu
-measurable, resp.
\tau
-Baire-measurable subset of
V(G)
for all
0\leqslant i<q
. The
\mu
-measurable chromatic number
\chi_{\mu}(G)
and the
\tau
-Baire-measurable chromatic number
\chi_{\tau}(G)
are defined as the smallest
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G
has a
\mu
-measurable, resp.
\tau
-Baire-measurable

-coloring if such

Definition

is due to Kechris, Solecki, and Todorcevic, who initiated the systematic study of Borel graphs and their combinatorics in the seminal paper
[

]
. Among other things, they demonstrated that greedy algorithms may be implemented on locally finite Borel graphs “in a Borel way,” leading, for instance, to the following result:
Theorem 1.5
(Kechris–Solecki–Todorcevic
[

, Proposition 4.6]
)
.
If
G
is a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta+1
.
Unfortunately, later work revealed that without any additional assumptions, it is often impossible to improve on the initial results obtained in the Borel setting using greedy algorithms. The following theorem of Marks serves as a remarkable illustration of this, showing that the bound in Theorem
1.5
is optimal even if

has no cycles (graphs with no cycles are called

):
Theorem 1.6
(Marks
[
Mar16
]
)
.
For every
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a Borel forest
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
such that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\Delta+1
.
By contrast, every forest
G
satisfies
\chi(G)\leqslant 2
. Furthermore, Theorem
1.6
shows that the following classical upper bounds on the chromatic number fail in the Borel setting:
Theorem 1.7
(Brooks
[
Bro41
,
Die17
]
)
.
If
G
is a graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant 3
and without a
(\Delta+1)
-clique, then
\chi(G)\leqslant\Delta
.
Theorem 1.8
(Johansson
[
Joh96
,
MR02
]
)
.
There exist constants
C
,
\Delta_{0}>0
such that if
G
is a triangle-free graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
, then
\chi(G)\leqslant C\Delta/\log\Delta
.
Both Brooks’s and Johansson’s theorems have analogs in the measurable/Baire-measurable setting
[
CMT16
,
Ber23
]
, showing that Marks’s Theorem
1.6
is a purely Borel phenomenon.
In view of the negative results such as Theorem
1.6
, it is natural to look for additional assumptions on a Borel graph
G
that improve the behavior of its Borel combinatorics. In this regard, a helpful observation is that Borel combinatorics essentially trivialize on
component-finite
graphs, i.e., graphs in which every connected component is finite. It is well understood in the field that a combinatorial problem admits a Borel solution on a component-finite Borel graph
G
if and only if it admits any solution on
G
at all (see
[
Pik21
, §5.3]
and §
2.2
for two ways of making this statement precise). For example,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\chi(G)
for every component-finite Borel graph
G
. Therefore, one may hope that Borel combinatorics on a locally countable Borel graph
G
would become more approachable if
G
could in some sense be “approximated” by its component-finite subgraphs.
One well-studied notion that attempts to capture this intuition is hyperfiniteness. As usual, a
subgraph
of a graph
G
is a graph
H
such that
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
and
E(H)\subseteq E(G)
. We write
H\subseteq G
to indicate that
H
is a subgraph of
G
. If
G
and
H
are Borel graphs and
V(H)\subseteq V(G)
is a Borel subset equipped with the relative
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets, we say that
H
is a
Borel subgraph
of
G
. Given a set
U\subseteq V(G)
, we let
G[U]
denote the subgraph of
G
induced
by
U
, i.e., the graph with vertex set
U
and edge set
E(G[U])\coloneqq E(G)\cap[U]^{2}
.
Note that when
U\subseteq V(G)
is a Borel set,
G[U]
is a Borel subgraph of
G
.
Definition 1.9
(Hyperfinite graphs)
.
A Borel graph
G
is
hyperfinite
if there is an increasing sequence
G_{0}\subseteq G_{1}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq G
of component-finite Borel subgraphs of
G
whose union is
G
.
The systematic study of hyperfinite graphs was initiated by Weiss
[
Wei84
]
and Slaman and Steel
[
SS88
]
, with important foundational work done by Dougherty, Jackson, and Kechris
[
DJK94
]
and Jackson, Kechris, and Louveau
[
JKL02
]
, among others. For an overview of this topic, see
[
Kec21
]
. While hyperfiniteness is in general a very powerful notion, with many applications to measurable constructions (see, e.g.,
[
CM16
,
BKS21
]
), counterexamples from
[
Con+20
]
show that it is largely unhelpful for obtaining Borel solutions to combinatorial problems. For example, Marks’s Theorem
1.6
remains true under the hyperfiniteness assumption:
Theorem 1.10
(Conley–Jackson–Marks–Seward–Tucker-Drob
[
Con+20
]
)
.
For every
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, there exists a hyperfinite Borel forest
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
such that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\Delta+1
.
In particular, Theorems
1.7
and
1.8
fail in the Borel setting for hyperfinite graphs.
Nevertheless, in the breakthrough paper
[
Con+20a
]
, Conley, Jackson, Marks, Seward, and Tucker-Drob succeeded in isolating a different and more subtle notion of “approximating” a graph by its component-finite subgraphs that turns out to be extremely useful in combinatorial arguments. For a graph
G
and an integer
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
G^{R}
denotes the graph with
V(G^{R})\coloneqq V(G)
in which two distinct vertices
u

v
are adjacent if and only if they are joined by a path of at most
R
edges in
G
.
Definition 1.11
(Asymptotic separation index)
.
Let
G
be a Borel graph. The
separation index
of
G
, in symbols
\mathsf{si}(G)
, is the smallest
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
for which there exists a partition
V(G)=U_{0}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{s}
of
V(G)
into
s+1
Borel subsets such that the induced subgraphs
G[U_{0}]
, …,
G[U_{s}]
are component-finite (if no such
s\in{\mathbb{N}}
exists, we set
\mathsf{si}(G)\coloneqq\infty
). The
asymptotic separation index
of
G
, in symbols
\mathsf{asi}(G)
, is the supremum of
\mathsf{si}(G^{R})
taken over all
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
There is a growing body of literature demonstrating that various combinatorial problems admit Borel solutions on Borel graphs with finite asymptotic separation index
[
BW21
,
Con+20a
,
QW22
,
Wei21
]
. For example, it turns out that the Borel chromatic number of a Borel graph is bounded above by a function of its ordinary chromatic number and asymptotic separation index:
Theorem 1.12
(Conley–Jackson–Marks–Seward–Tucker-Drob
[
Con+20a
, Theorem 8.1]
)
.
If
G
is a Borel graph with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
and
\chi(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant(\mathsf{asi}(G)+1)\chi(G)-\mathsf{asi}(G)
.
Another example is a result of Qian and the second named author, who showed in
[
QW22
]
that a Borel graph

of maximum degree
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
and with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
has a Borel proper edge-coloring with
\Delta+1+\mathsf{asi}(G)
colors. Moreover, if

is bipartite, this bound can be improved to
\Delta+\mathsf{asi}(G)
by a result of Bowen and the second named author
[

]
. (We discuss edge-colorings in more detail in §

.)
Of course, these results are only impressive assuming there is a rich family of Borel graphs with finite asymptotic separation index. Thankfully, this is indeed the case, as many natural classes of graphs have asymptotic separation index at most

. Here is a non-exhaustive list of examples (the reader is referred to the cited papers for details and omitted definitions):
Theorem 1.13
(Conley–Jackson–Marks–Seward–Tucker-Drob
[

]
)
.
Suppose that

has at least one of the following properties:

—

.
Theorem 1.14
(AB–Yu

.
If

.
Furthermore,

(Conley–Jackson–Marks–Seward–Tucker-Drob

.
Let

\mathsf{asi}(G[U])\leqslant 1
.
And if

Theorem 1.16
(

]
)
.
Let

be a hyperfinite locally finite Borel graph. Fix a probability Borel measure

. Then there is a

Both Theorems

It is not a coincidence that in all the above examples, the asymptotic separation index is not only finite but in fact bounded by

: it is not known whether there exist any Borel graphs

with
1<\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty

Con+20a
, 15]
. The problem of whether such graphs exist is especially interesting in view of results such as Theorem

The aim of this paper is to make several additions to the list of combinatorial constructions that can be performed in a Borel way on graphs with finite asymptotic separation index. For example, we establish a Borel version of Brooks’s Theorem

for Borel graphs

with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
:
Theorem 1.17
(Borel Brooks for finite

)
.
Let
G
be a Borel graph with finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant 3
and without a
(\Delta+1)
-clique. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta
.
We also establish a Borel version of Johansson’s Theorem

for Borel graphs

with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty

]
) with Theorem

for any given
\varepsilon>0
and large enough

. In other words, this approach yields a factor in front of
\Delta/\log\Delta
that is close to
\mathsf{asi}(G)+1
. We prove a bound with a factor in front of

that is
independent of
\mathsf{asi}(G)
. Furthermore, when

has no cycles of length

and

, our bound matches the best known bound on the ordinary chromatic number of

(due to Kim

]

Theorem 1.18
(Borel colorings of graphs without short cycles and with finite

)
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
, there is

with the following property. Let

be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree

While most work in descriptive combinatorics addresses specific combinatorial problems, several recent papers attack the fundamental Question

for relatively general classes of problems

.
1.2.  The Lovász Local Lemma and its Borel versions
Typical combinatorial problems require assigning a “color” (an element of some finite set) to every member of a given structure (e.g., to every vertex of a graph) in a way that fulfills a prescribed set of constraints. This idea is formally captured in the following definition:
Definition 1.19
(Constraint satisfaction problems)
.
Let
q
be a positive integer. By a
q
-coloring
of a set
S
we mean a function
f\colon S\to q
.
Given a set
X
and a finite subset
D\subseteq X
, an
(X,q)
-constraint
(or simply a
constraint
if
X
and
q
are understood) with
domain
D
is a set
B\subseteq q^{D}
of
q
-colorings of
D
. If
B
is a constraint with domain
D
, then we write
\mathrm{dom}(B)\coloneqq D
.
We say that a
q
-coloring
f\colon X\to q
of
X
violates
a constraint
B
if
{{f}|_{\mathrm{dom}(B)}}\in B
, and
satisfies
B
otherwise.
A
constraint satisfaction problem
(a
CSP
for short)
\mathscr{B}
on
a set
X
with
range
q
, in symbols
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q,
is a set of
(X,q)
-constraints. A
solution
to a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is a
q
-coloring
f\colon X\to q
that satisfies every constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
. We say that
\mathscr{B}
is
satisfiable
if it has a solution.
In other words, each constraint in a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is interpreted as a set of finite “forbidden” (or “bad”) patterns that are not allowed to appear in a solution
f\colon X\to q
.
Example 1.20
(Proper coloring as a CSP)
.
Finding a proper
q
-coloring of a graph
G
can be represented by a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon V(G)\to^{?}q
as follows. For an edge
uv\in E(G)
, let
B_{uv}
be the constraint with domain
\{u,v\}
given by
B_{uv}\coloneqq\{(u\mapsto i,\,v\mapsto i)\,:\,0\leqslant i<q\}
. Then a coloring
f\colon V(G)\to q
satisfies
B_{uv}
precisely when
f(u)\neq f(v)
. Therefore,
f
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}\coloneqq\{B_{uv}\,:\,uv\in E(G)\}
if and only if it is a proper
q
-coloring of
G
.
The
Lovász Local Lemma
(the
LLL
for short) provides a sufficient condition guaranteeing that a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is satisfiable. To state it, we associate to
\mathscr{B}
two numerical parameters,
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
, defined as follows. For a constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
, the
probability
of
B
is the quantity
\mathbb{P}[B]\,\coloneqq\,\frac{|B|}{q^{|\mathrm{dom}(B)|}}.
In other words,
\mathbb{P}[B]
is the probability that
B
is violated by a uniformly random
q
-coloring. Let
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}\mathbb{P}[B].
The
maximum dependency degree
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
of
\mathscr{B}
is the value
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}|\{B^{\prime}\in\mathscr{B}\,:\,B\neq B^{\prime}\text{ and }\mathrm{dom}(B^{\prime})\cap\mathrm{dom}(B)\neq\varnothing\}|.
Lemma 1.21
(Lovász Local Lemma
[
EL75
,
Spe77
,
AS16
]
)
.
If
\mathscr{B}
is a CSP such that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)\,\leqslant\,1/e,
where
e=2.71\ldots
is the base of the natural logarithm, then
\mathscr{B}
has a solution.
Remarks 1.22
.
(i)
The statement of the LLL given above is somewhat less general than the more classical version usually found in the combinatorics literature. Specifically, the probabilities of the constraints in Lemma
1.21
are computed by considering a
uniformly random
q
-coloring

; that is, we view
(f(x)\,:\,x\in X)
as a collection of mutually independent discrete random variables, each distributed uniformly over a
q

variable version
of the LLL (the name is due to Kolipaka and Szegedy
[
KS11
]
). Even though this setting is not the most general, it does encompass virtually all standard applications and is often viewed as the “right one” for algorithmic considerations (see, e.g.,
[
Bec91
,
MT10
,
FG17
]
). For the statement of the LLL in abstract probability spaces, see
[
AS16
, §5.1]
.
(ii)
The LLL is usually stated and proved in the case when the CSP
\mathscr{B}
comprises only finitely many constraints. Nevertheless, a routine compactness argument shows that an infinite CSP
\mathscr{B}
is satisfiable if and only if all its finite subsets
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{B}
are satisfiable (see, e.g.,
[
AS16
, proof of Theorem 5.2.2]
), so the conclusion of Lemma
1.21
for infinite
\mathscr{B}
follows from the finite case.
The LLL is used throughout combinatorics; for a sample of its applications, see
[
AS16
,
MR02
]
.
Here we are interested in the LLL from the perspective of descriptive combinatorics. This line of research forms part of a broader program concerning versions of the LLL that are “constructive” in various senses: algorithmic
[
Bec91
,
BGR20
,
FG17
,
MT10
]
, computable
[
RS14
]
, or—as in this paper—Borel/measurable
[
Ber19
,
Ber23
,
Ber23a
,
Csó+22
]
.
Let
X
be a standard Borel space and let
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
. Then the set
\mathsf{Const}(X,q)
of all
(X,q)
-constraints also carries a natural standard Borel structure (see §

), so we can define Borel CSPs as follows:
Definition 1.23
(Borel CSPs)
.
Let
X
be a standard Borel space and let
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
. A CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is
Borel
if it is a Borel subset of
\mathsf{Const}(X,q)
.
Heuristically, a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is Borel as long as the constraints that need to be satisfied are specified “explicitly.”
Example 1.24
(Proper coloring as a Borel CSP)
.
If
G
is a Borel graph, then the CSP from Example
1.20
that encodes proper
q
-coloring problem on
G
is easily seen to be Borel.
We now ask the following natural question:
Question 1.25
.
Given a Borel CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
on a standard Borel space
X
, what LLL-style conditions guarantee that
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
?
Without any additional assumptions on
\mathscr{B}
, Question
1.25
has recently been fully resolved in a series of contributions by several authors. In the following statement and in the remainder of the paper, we say that a CSP
\mathscr{B}
is
bounded
if
\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}|\mathrm{dom}(B)|<\infty
.
‡
‡
‡
The boundedness assumption can likely be removed, but it makes the results of
[
Ber23
]
easier to apply and is satisfied in most applications.
Theorem 1.26
(Brandt–Grunau–Rozhoň
[
BGR20
]
, AB
[
Ber23
]
, Thornton
[
Tho22
]
)
.
(i)
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a bounded Borel CSP on a standard Borel space

. If
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,2^{\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})}\,<\,1,
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
.
(ii)
On the other hand, for every

, there exists a bounded Borel CSP
\mathscr{B}
on a standard Borel space such that
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})=d
and
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})=2^{-d}
, so
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,2^{\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})}\,=\,1,
yet

has no Borel solution.
Let us say a few words about how Theorem
1.26
follows from the cited sources. In
[
BGR20
]
, Brandt, Grunau, and Rozhoň designed an efficient deterministic distributed algorithm for finding solutions to CSPs under the condition
\mathsf{p}\,2^{\mathsf{d}}<1
(their work builds on earlier contributions of Brandt, Maus, and Uitto
[
BMU19
]
). In
[
Ber23
]
, the first named author established certain general results that allow using efficient distributed algorithms to derive conclusions in descriptive combinatorics. In particular Theorem
1.26
(i)
follows immediately from the Brandt–Grunau–Rozhoň algorithm via
[
Ber23
, Theorem 2.10]
. (See §
1.3
for a more detailed discussion of the connections between distributed computing and descriptive combinatorics.)
The second part of Theorem
1.26
is proved by analyzing the so-called
sinkless orientation problem
on regular graphs. Let
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
G
be a
d
-regular graph. An orientation of
G
is
sinkless
if the outdegree of every vertex is positive. A sinkless orientation of
G
can be naturally encoded as a solution to a certain CSP
\mathscr{B}_{\text{sinkless}}\colon E(G)\to^{?}2
. Here the color of each edge
e\in E(G)
indicates the direction in which
e
is oriented. For example, we may fix a function
c\colon E(G)\to V(G)
sending each edge to one of its endpoints and say that an edge
e
is oriented towards
c(e)
if and only if its color is
0
. Then we define, for each vertex
v
, a constraint
B_{v}
with domain
\mathrm{dom}(B_{v})\coloneqq\{e\in E(G)\,:\,e\ni v\}
that requires the outdegree of
v
to be positive, and let
\mathscr{B}_{\text{sinkless}}\,\coloneqq\,\{B_{v}\,:\,v\in V(G)\}.
Clearly, we have
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}_{\text{sinkless}})=d
and
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B}_{\text{sinkless}})=2^{-d}
. Furthermore, if
G
is a Borel graph, then the CSP
\mathscr{B}_{\text{sinkless}}
can be made Borel by choosing a Borel function
c\colon E(G)\to V(G)
using the Luzin–Novikov theorem
[
Kec95
, Theorem 18.10]
. Nevertheless, Thornton
[
Tho22
, Theorem 3.5]
applied the determinacy method of Marks
[
Mar16
]
to construct, for each

, a Borel

-regular forest
G
that does not admit a Borel sinkless orientation. This verifies Theorem
1.26
(ii)
.
While Theorem
1.26
provides a complete characterization of the range of values for the parameters
\mathsf{p}
and
\mathsf{d}
under which a Borel version of the LLL holds, its downside is that the inequality
\mathsf{p}\,2^{\mathsf{d}}<1
is much harder to satisfy than the usual LLL condition
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant 1/e
. In fact, we are not aware of any applications of substantial combinatorial interest where the bound
\mathsf{p}\,2^{\mathsf{d}}<1
holds and Theorem
1.26
(i)
can be invoked. This motivates the search for other Borel versions of the LLL which make extra assumptions on the structure of the CSP in question. To formulate such assumptions, it is convenient to associate to each CSP a graph:
Definition 1.27
(Graphs associated to CSPs)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a CSP. Then
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is the graph with vertex set
X
in which two vertices
x
,
y\in X
are adjacent if and only if
x\neq y
and there is some constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
x
,
y\in\mathrm{dom}(B)
.
Note that if
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
is a Borel CSP and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
is finite, then
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is a locally finite Borel graph. Moreover, if additionally
\mathscr{B}
is bounded, then the maximum degree of
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is finite.
An important result of Csóka, Grabowski, Máthé, Pikhurko, and Tyros is a Borel version of the LLL for CSPs whose associated graphs have subexponential growth. Here we say that a graph
G
is of
subexponential growth
if for every
\varepsilon>0
there is
r>0
such that for all
R\geqslant r
and all
v\in V(G)
, the
R
-ball around
v
in
G
contains at most
e^{\varepsilon R}
vertices.
Theorem 1.28
(Borel LLL for subexponential growth; Csóka–Grabowski–Máthé–Pikhurko–Tyros
[
Csó+22
, Theorem 4.5]
)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that the graph
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is of subexponential growth. If
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)\,\leqslant\,1/e,
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
.
We investigate the situation where instead of subexponential growth, the graph
G_{\mathscr{B}}
has finite asymptotic separation index. Actually, for our first main result, we only need an upper bound on the
separation index
of
G_{\mathscr{B}}
rather than its
asymptotic
separation index. (Even more precisely, we use a bound on a certain auxiliary parameter closely related to
\mathsf{si}(G_{\mathscr{B}})
, which we call the shattering number of
\mathscr{B}
; see §
3.1
for details.)
Theorem 1.29
(Borel LLL with bounded separation index)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that
\mathsf{si}(G_{\mathscr{B}})\leqslant s<\infty
. If
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{s+1}\,\leqslant\,e^{-s-1},
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
.
It is a standard observation (see, e.g.,
[
Bec91
,
Ber23
,
CP19
,
FG17
,
GHK18
]
) that for a majority of applications, instead of the usual LLL condition
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant 1/e
, it is enough to have a version of the LLL that holds under a
polynomial criterion
, i.e., with a bound of the form
\mathsf{p}\,f(\mathsf{d})\leqslant 1
for some polynomial
f
. When
s
is treated as a constant parameter, our Theorem
1.29
is precisely of this type. (This should be contrasted with the exponential bound
\mathsf{p}\,2^{\mathsf{d}}<1
in Theorem
1.26
.) For instance, if
G_{\mathscr{B}}
has asymptotic separation index at most
1
(which happens in the numerous situations discussed in §
1.1
), we can apply the trivial inequality
\mathsf{si}(G_{\mathscr{B}})\leqslant\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})
to obtain the following special case of Theorem
1.29
:
Corollary 1.30
(Borel LLL for
\mathsf{asi}\leqslant 1
)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})\leqslant 1
. If
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{2}\,\leqslant\,e^{-2},
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
.
Most of the examples listed in Theorem
1.13
have or can have exponential growth rate. In such cases, Corollary
1.30
is an advancement over both Theorems
1.28
and
1.26
(i)
. Interestingly, it is not known whether there exist any Borel graphs
G
of subexponential growth with
\mathsf{asi}(G)>1
.
Of course, the result of Corollary
1.30
holds for other finite values of
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})
as well, but with the exponent
2
replaced by
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})+1
. Our second main result is a Borel version of the LLL for bounded CSPs
\mathscr{B}
satisfying
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})<\infty
, which holds under a polynomial criterion independent of the actual value of
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})
:
Theorem 1.31
(Borel LLL for finite
\mathsf{asi}
)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a bounded Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})<\infty
. If
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2^{-15},
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution
f\colon X\to q
.
The above results add further impetus to solve the problem of whether there exist Borel graphs
G
with
1<\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, since if they do not, Corollary
1.30
would supersede Theorem

.
In addition to Borel versions of the LLL, we can also consider its measurable and Baire-measurable versions. To date, the most general result along these lines is the following:
Theorem 1.32
(AB
[
Ber23
, Theorem 2.20]
)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a bounded Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,2^{-15}.
Then the following conclusions hold:
(i)
If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on

, then
\mathscr{B}
has a
\mu
-measurable solution.
(ii)
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
X
, then
\mathscr{B}
has a
\tau
-Baire-measurable solution.
Thus, the LLL holds measurably/Baire-measurably with a polynomial criterion. However, it is still not known whether the usual criterion
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant 1/e
suffices (we conjecture that it does). By combining Corollary

with Theorem

, we immediately obtain a quantitative improvement to Theorem

Corollary 1.33

.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. Suppose that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{2}\,\leqslant\,e^{-2}.
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
X
, then
\mathscr{B}
has a
\tau

Similarly, if

is hyperfinite, Theorem

yields an improvement in the measurable setting:
Corollary 1.34
(Measurable hyperfinite LLL)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q

. Suppose that the graph

1.3.  Distributed algorithms
A recent trend in descriptive combinatorics, initiated by the first named author in
[
Ber23
]
, is to investigate the surprisingly intimate relationship between descriptive combinatorics and
distributed computing
—an area of computer science concerned with problems that can be solved efficiently by a decentralized network of processors. It turns out that the existence of an efficient distributed algorithm that solves a combinatorial problem on a class of finite graphs often implies that the same problem admits Borel, measurable, etc. solutions on certain Borel graphs
[
Ber23
]
, and sometimes the reverse implication also holds
[
Ber23a
,
Bra+21
,
GR21a
]
. Using Theorem
1.29
, we contribute a new result to this avenue of research, which allows using randomized distributed algorithms to solve combinatorial problems in a Borel way on graphs with finite asymptotic separation index.
The class of combinatorial problems we consider here are the so-called locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems, which were isolated by Naor and Stockmeyer in
[
NS95
]
(in
[
Ber23
]
the term “local coloring problems” is used instead). The definition of an LCL problem is very similar to the definition of a CSP given in Definition
1.19
; the main difference is that an LCL problem is defined on an underlying graph using the graph’s structure:
Definition 1.35
(Locally checkable labeling problems)
.
A
labeled graph
is a pair
(G,f)
, where
G
is a graph and
f\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
is a function, called a
labeling
. A
rooted labeled graph
is a triple
(G,f,v)
, where
(G,f)
is a labeled graph and
v\in V(G)
is a vertex, called a
root
. Two rooted labeled graphs
(G,f,v)
and
(G^{\prime},f^{\prime},v^{\prime})
are
isomorphic
if there is a graph isomorphism between
G
and
G^{\prime}
that sends
v
to
v^{\prime}
and preserves the labeling. Given a labeled graph
(G,f)
, a vertex
v\in V(G)
, and an integer
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we let
[G,f,v]_{R}
be the isomorphism type of the rooted labeled graph
\big(G[B_{G}(v,R)],\,{{f}|_{B_{G}(v,R)}},\,v\big),
where
B_{G}(v,R)
is the
R
-ball around
v
in
G
.
A
locally checkable labeling problem
(an
LCL problem
for short) of
radius
R
is a pair
\Pi=(R,\mathcal{P})
, where
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathcal{P}
is a map that sends isomorphism classes of finite rooted labeled graphs to
\{0,1\}
. If
\Pi=(R,\mathcal{P})
is an LCL problem and
(G,f)
is a locally finite labeled graph, then
f
solves
\Pi
on
G
, or is a
\Pi
-coloring
of
G
, if
\mathcal{P}([G,f,v]_{R})=1
for all
v\in V(G)
. A graph
G
is
\Pi
-colorable
if it has a
\Pi
-coloring
f\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
.
Informally, an LCL problem
\Pi=(R,\mathcal{P})
is a “rule” that decides whether a labeling
f
of
G
is “valid” by looking at the restrictions of
f
to
R
-balls around individual vertices.
Example 1.36
(Proper coloring as an LCL problem)
.
A typical example of an LCL problem is proper
q
-coloring, since whether a coloring of a graph
G
is proper is determined by its restrictions to
1
-balls in
G
. Explicitly, given (the isomorphism type of) a finite rooted labeled graph
(G,f,v)
, set
\mathcal{P}(G,f,v)\coloneqq 1
if and only if
f(v)<q
and
f(u)\neq f(v)
for all neighbors
u
of
v
. If we let
\Pi\coloneqq(1,\mathcal{P})
, then a
\Pi
-coloring of a locally finite graph
G
is the same as a proper
q
-coloring of
G
.
The model of distributed computation relevant to our work is called
\mathsf{LOCAL}
§
§
§
This is not an acronym.
. It was introduced by Linial in
[
Lin92
]
(although there are some earlier related results, e.g., by Alon, Babai, and Itai
[
ABI86
]
, Luby
[
Lub86
]
, and Goldberg, Plotkin, and Shannon
[
GPS88
]
). For an introduction to this subject, see the book
[
BE13
]
by Barenboim and Elkin.
The
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model is intended to quantify the difficulty of transforming local data into a global solution to a problem on a large (but finite!) decentralized communication network. Informally, we imagine each vertex of an
n
-vertex graph
G
to be occupied by a processor that may pass messages to its neighbors. The length of the messages is unrestricted, and the computational power available to each processor is unlimited (in other words, any computations carried out by individual processors take a single unit of time). Eventually, every processor must decide on its own part of the output; in the context of LCL problems, each vertex must decide on its own label. Since the processors may only communicate along the edges of
G
, in
T
time units, a processor can only collect information from its
T
-ball in
G
—this is why the model is called “
\mathsf{LOCAL}
”. In effect, a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm with running time
T
can be thought of as a function that determines the output at each vertex of
G
based only on the isomorphism type of the
T
-ball around it
[
BE13
, §4.1.2]
. Formally, in addition to the graph itself, a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm also takes as input a labeling of
G
, used to distinguish the vertices from each other.
Definition 1.37
(
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms)
.
A
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
is a function
\mathcal{A}
that sends isomorphism classes of finite rooted labeled graphs to
{\mathbb{N}}
. Given a locally finite labeled graph
(G,f)
and
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
, the
output of
\mathcal{A}
on
G
after time
T
is the function
\mathcal{A}_{T}(G,f)\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
given by
\big(\mathcal{A}_{T}(G,f)\big)(v)\,\coloneqq\,\mathcal{A}\left([G,f,v]_{T}\right)\quad\text{for all }v\in V(G).
Observe that an LCL problem, as defined in Definition
1.35
, is a pair
\Pi=(R,\mathcal{P})
such that
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathcal{P}
is a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm taking values in
\{0,1\}
, and a labeling
f\colon V(G)\to{\mathbb{N}}
is a
\Pi
-coloring of
G
exactly when
\mathcal{P}_{R}(G,f)
is the constant
1
function on
V(G)
.
There are two variations of the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, depending on the way the symmetry-breaking labeling
f
in Definition
1.37
is chosen. In the deterministic version of the model,
f
assigns a unique identifier to each vertex, and the output of the algorithm must provide a valid solution to the problem regardless of the specific assignment of the identifiers:
Definition 1.38
(Deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity of LCL problems)
.
Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem and let
\mathbb{G}
be a class of finite graphs. The
deterministic
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity
of
\Pi
on the class
\mathbb{G}
is the function
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}\colon{\mathbb{N}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
defined by letting
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)
be the smallest
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that there exists a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with the following property:
For every
n
-vertex graph
G\in\mathbb{G}
and every bijection
\mathsf{ID}\colon V(G)\to n
, the function
\mathcal{A}_{T}(G,\mathsf{ID})
is a
\Pi
-coloring of
G
.
If no such

exists, we set
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)\coloneqq\infty
.
In the randomized version of the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model, the symmetry is broken using a random labeling:
Definition 1.39
(Randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity of LCL problems)
.
Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem and let
\mathbb{G}
be a class of finite graphs. The
randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity
of
\Pi
on the class
\mathbb{G}
is the function
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}\colon{\mathbb{N}}^{+}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
defined by letting
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)
be the smallest
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that there exist
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and a
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm
\mathcal{A}
with the following property:
For every
n
-vertex graph
G\in\mathbb{G}
, if a mapping
\vartheta\colon V(G)\to\ell
is chosen uniformly at random, then
\mathbb{P}\big[\text{the function $\mathcal{A}_{T}(G,\vartheta)$ is a $\Pi$-coloring of $G$}\big]\,\geqslant\,1-\frac{1}{n}.
If no such
T\in{\mathbb{N}}
exists, we set
\mathsf{Det}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)\coloneqq\infty
.
Unsurprisingly, the randomized version of the model is more computationally powerful than the deterministic one, and there exist many instances where a problem’s randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity is significantly lower than its deterministic

complexity
[
CKP19
]
.
In
[

, the first named author showed that fast deterministic

(AB

Let

has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring.
Similarly, a fast randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm yields measurable/Baire-measurable solutions:
Theorem 1.41
(AB
[
Ber23
, Theorem 2.14]
)
.
Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem and let
\mathbb{G}
be a class of
\Pi
-colorable finite graphs such that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)=o(\log n)
. If
G
is a Borel graph of finite maximum degree all of whose finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathbb{G}
, then the following conclusions hold:
(i)
If
\mu
is a probability Borel measure on
V(G)
, then
G
has a
\mu
-measurable
\Pi
-coloring.
(ii)
If
\tau
is a compatible Polish topology on
V(G)
, then
G
has a
\tau
-Baire-measurable
\Pi
-coloring.
In other words, if we are willing to sacrifice Borelness and settle for measurable or Baire-measurable solutions, then we may use the much more powerful randomized version of the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model to find them. Here we show that if the graph
G
has finite asymptotic separation index, then no sacrifice is necessary: we can obtain
Borel
\Pi
-colorings of
G
from
randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms:
Theorem 1.42
(Borel colorings using randomized algorithms and finite
\mathsf{asi}
)
.
Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem and let
\mathbb{G}
be a class of
\Pi
-colorable finite graphs such that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi,\mathbb{G}}(n)=o(\log n)
. Let
G
be a Borel graph of finite maximum degree all of whose finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathbb{G}
. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
G
has a Borel
\Pi
-coloring.
We in fact prove somewhat stronger (and numerically explicit) versions of Theorem
1.42
, which are presented in §
3.2
. We should also note that Theorems
1.40
and
1.41
are stated in
[
Ber23
]
in the more general context of so-called structured graphs, i.e., graphs equipped with additional combinatorial data such as an orientation, a labeling, an ordering of the vertices, etc. Our results also hold for structured graphs, as we explain in §
3.2
.
Although it may at first seem unrelated, Theorem
1.42
is actually a relatively straightforward consequence of our main results concerning Borel versions of the LLL. As demonstrated in
[
Ber23
]
, randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms and the LLL are closely linked. Specifically,
[
Ber23
, Lemma 4.6]
gives a way to reduce an LCL problem
\Pi
to an application of the LLL with a polynomial criterion using a sufficiently fast randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm. As a result, Theorem
1.42
follows in a fairly routine manner from Theorem
1.29
and
[
Ber23
, Lemma 4.6]
; the details are presented in §
3.2
. Curiously, the relationship between
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms and the LLL is reciprocal: in order to prove Theorem
1.31
, we combine Theorem
1.42
with a fast randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for solving instances of the LLL due to Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
and Ghaffari, Harris, and Kuhn
[
GHK18
]
; see §
3.3
for the details.
1.4.  Combinatorial applications
1.4.1.
Bounds on the Borel chromatic number
We have already mentioned two applications of our general results to the study of Borel proper colorings of graphs with finite asymptotic separation index: Theorem
1.17
(Borel version of Brooks’s theorem) and Theorem
1.18
(Borel version of Johansson’s theorem). Both of these results are derived from Theorem
1.42
by citing relevant randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms.
Proof
of Theorem
1.17
.
Fix an integer
\Delta\geqslant 3
and let
\mathbb{B}\mathrm{rooks}_{\Delta}
be the class of all finite graphs of maximum degree at most
\Delta
with no
(\Delta+1)
-cliques. Ghaffari, Hirvonen, Kuhn, and Maus
[
Gha+18
]
developed a randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for
\Delta
-coloring
n
-vertex graphs in the class
\mathbb{B}\mathrm{rooks}_{\Delta}
with running time
O((\log\log n)^{2})=o(\log n)
. If
G
is a Borel graph of maximum degree
\Delta
and without a
(\Delta+1)
-clique, then all its finite induced subgraphs are in
\mathbb{B}\mathrm{rooks}_{\Delta}
, so if
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
G
has a Borel proper
\Delta
-coloring by Theorem
1.42
, as desired.
∎
Theorem
1.18
is proved in exactly the same way by invoking randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms due to Chung, Pettie, and Su
[
CPS17
]
; see §
4.1
for the details.
As one more illustration, we show that if the Borel chromatic number of a Borel graph
G
with finite asymptotic separation index is close to the maximum degree of
G
, then it must be equal to the ordinary chromatic number of
G
:
Theorem 1.43
(Borel colorings with
\Delta-O(\sqrt{\Delta})
colors)
.
There exists a constant
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a Borel graph with finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and let
k\coloneqq\lfloor\sqrt{\Delta+1/4}-7/2\rfloor
. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
and
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\geqslant\Delta-k
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\chi(G)
.
Proof
.
Clearly,
\chi(G)\leqslant\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)
. To show
\chi(G)\geqslant\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)
, set
q\coloneqq\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)-1
and suppose, toward a contradiction, that
\chi(G)\leqslant q
. Let
\Pi_{q}
be the LCL problem that encodes proper
q
-coloring of graphs (see Example
1.36
) and let
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ol}_{\Delta,q}
be the class of all finite graphs with maximum degree at most
\Delta
and chromatic number at most
q
. For large
\Delta
and
q\geqslant\Delta-k-1
, Bamas and Esperet
[
BE19
, Theorem 1.3]
proved that
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi_{q},\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ol}_{\Delta,q}}(n)\,\leqslant\,\exp(O(\sqrt{\log\log n}))\,=\,o(\log n).
Since all finite induced subgraphs of
G
are in
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{ol}_{\Delta,q}
, Theorem
1.42
implies that
G
has a Borel proper
q
-coloring, which contradicts the choice of
q
. ∎
When
\Delta
is sufficiently large, Theorem
1.17
is a consequence of Theorem
1.43
and Brooks’s Theorem
1.7
for the ordinary chromatic number. Similarly, we have:
Corollary 1.44
(Borel colorings with
\Delta-1
colors)
.
There exists a constant
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the following property. Let
G
be a Borel graph with finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and without a
\Delta
-clique. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta-1
.
Proof
.
For large
\Delta
, Reed
[
Ree99
]
showed that graphs with maximum degree
\Delta
and without
\Delta
-cliques have chromatic number at most
\Delta-1
. Hence, the desired result follows from Theorem
1.43
.
∎
1.4.2.
Borel edge-colorings
Alongside proper coloring, another classical concept in graph theory is proper edge-coloring, which is defined as follows:
Definition 1.45
(Edge-colorings and the (Borel) chromatic index)
.
Let
G
be a graph and let
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
. A
proper
q
-edge-coloring
of
G
is a function
f\colon E(G)\to q
such that
f(e)\neq f(e^{\prime})
for all distinct edges
e
,
e^{\prime}\in E(G)
that share a vertex. The
chromatic index
\chi^{\prime}(G)
of
G
is the smallest
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G
has a proper
q
-edge-coloring; if no such
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
exists, we set
\chi^{\prime}(G)\coloneqq\infty
. Similarly, if
G
is a Borel graph, then the
Borel chromatic index
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)
of
G
is the smallest
q\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
G
has a Borel proper
q
-edge-coloring; if no such
q
exists, we set
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\coloneqq\infty
.
A proper
q
-edge-coloring of a graph
G
is exactly the same thing as a proper
q
-coloring of the
line graph
of
G
, i.e., the graph
L(G)
with vertex set
E(G)
and edge set
\{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in[E(G)]^{2}\,:\,|e\cap e^{\prime}|=1\}
. If
G
is a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
, then the maximum degree of
L(G)
is at most
2\Delta-2
, and hence the Borel chromatic index of
G
is at most
2\Delta-1
by the Kechris–Solecki–Todorcevic Theorem
1.5
. Marks
[
Mar16
]
proved that there exist Borel forests
G
for which this bound is optimal, and Conley, Jackson, Marks, Seward, and Tucker-Drob
[
Con+20
]
further showed that such
G
can be taken to be hyperfinite. The following theorem summarizes these facts:
Theorem 1.46
(Kechris–Solecki–Todorcevic
[
KST99
, 15]
, Marks
[
Mar16
, Theorem 1.4]
, Conley–Jackson–Marks–Seward–Tucker-Drob
[
Con+20
, Theorem 1.4(2)]
)
.
Fix an integer
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
(i)
Every Borel graph
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
satisfies
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant 2\Delta-1
.
(ii)
There exists a hyperfinite Borel forest
G
of maximum degree
\Delta
with
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=2\Delta-1
.
By contrast, a celebrated result of Vizing asserts that the ordinary chromatic index of a graph of maximum degree
\Delta
cannot exceed
\Delta+1
:
Theorem 1.47
(Vizing
[
Viz64
]
)
.
If
G
is a graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
, then
\chi^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\Delta+1
.
See
[
Sti+12
, §A.1]
for an English translation of Vizing’s paper and
[
BM08
,
Die17
]
for modern textbook presentations.
Theorem
1.46
precludes a Borel version of Vizing’s theorem when
\Delta\geqslant 3
. On the other hand, a measurable version of Vizing’s theorem was recently proved by Grebík
[
Gre23
]
(building on the earlier breakthrough work of Grebík and Pikhurko
[
GP20
]
). Naturally, we would like to know what additional assumptions on
G
bring the Borel chromatic index of
G
closer to
\chi^{\prime}(G)
. For example, Dhawan and the first named author
[
BD23
]
showed that
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta+1
if
G
is of subexponential growth. In this direction, the asymptotic separation index turns out to be an extremely helpful parameter, as demonstrated by Qian and the second named author
[
QW22
]
:
Theorem 1.48
(Qian–FW
[
QW22
, Theorem 3.7]
)
.
If
G
is a Borel graph with finite maximum degree
\Delta
and with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\chi^{\prime}(G)+\mathsf{asi}(G)
, and hence
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\Delta+1+\mathsf{asi}(G)
.
In the case when
G
has no odd cycles, Theorem
1.48
was established earlier by Bowen and the second named author
[
BW21
]
. Note that for graphs
G
with
\mathsf{asi}(G)\leqslant 1
, examples of which are given in §
1.1
, Theorem
1.48
yields the bound
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\Delta+2
. It is still an open problem whether this can be reduced to
\Delta+1
. It is also not known whether a bound of the form
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(G)\leqslant\Delta+c
for some fixed
c
(perhaps even
c=1
) holds for all graphs
G
with
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
regardless of the value of
\mathsf{asi}(G)
. As a consequence of our main results, we obtain an upper bound on
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)
for graphs
G
with finite asymptotic separation index that is asymptotic to
\Delta
and independent of the value of
\mathsf{asi}(G)
:
Theorem 1.49
(Borel edge-colorings with
\approx\Delta
colors)
.
There is
\Delta_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that if
G
is a Borel graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
and
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\Delta+\sqrt{\Delta}\log^{3}\Delta.
Theorem
1.49
is proved by appealing to a randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for edge-coloring due to Chang, He, Li, Pettie, and Uitto
[
Cha+19
]
; see §
4.2
for the details. In view of Theorem
1.48
, Theorem
1.49
is only interesting for graphs
G
whose asymptotic separation index is non-negligible compared to their maximum degree. This again highlights the importance of determining whether there exist Borel graphs
G
with
1<\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
.
1.4.3.
Borel edge-colorings of Schreier graphs
Some of the most well-studied examples in descriptive combinatorics are the so-called Schreier graphs
[
KM20
, §6.5]
. These graphs are associated to group actions and arise naturally in measurable and topological dynamics. For the remainder of §
1.4.3
, we fix a finitely generated group
\Gamma
with a finite symmetric generating set
F\subseteq\Gamma\setminus\{\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}\}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}
is the identity element of
\Gamma
(a subset of a group is
symmetric
if it is closed under taking inverses). Recall that a group action
\bm{a}\colon\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is
free
if
\gamma\cdot_{\bm{a}}x\neq x
for all
x\in X
and
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}\}
, i.e., if the stabilizer of every point in
X
is trivial.
Definition 1.50
(Schreier graphs)
.
The
Schreier graph
of a free action
\bm{a}\colon\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is the graph
\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F)
with vertex set
X
and edge set
\{\{x,\sigma\cdot_{\bm{a}}x\}\,:\,x\in X,\ \sigma\in F\}
.
Note that if
X
is a standard Borel space and
\bm{a}\colon\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is a free Borel action (meaning that the map
x\mapsto\gamma\cdot_{\bm{a}}x
is Borel for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
), then the corresponding Schreier graph
\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F)
is a Borel graph of maximum degree
|F|
. In contrast to general Borel graphs, the bound in Theorem
1.46
(i)
is not sharp when
G
is a Schreier graph, as it can be easily seen
[
KM20
, 67]
that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F))\,\leqslant\,|\{\sigma\in F\,:\,\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)\text{ is even}\}|\,+\,\frac{3}{2}|\{\sigma\in F\,:\,\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)\text{ is odd or infinite}\}|\,\leqslant\,\frac{3}{2}|F|.
It is an open problem to determine the optimal upper bound on
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}^{\prime}(\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F))
in terms of
|F|
.
The investigation of Borel edge-colorings of Schreier graphs with finite asymptotic separation index was initiated by the second named author in
[
Wei21
]
. There it is shown that a Borel version of Vizing’s theorem holds for Schreier graphs with asymptotic separation index at most
1
under the assumption that no element of
F
is of odd order (this is also a consequence of Theorem
1.48
, which was proved later). Here we generalize this result by extending it to the case of arbitrary finite asymptotic separation index and allowing
F
to contain elements of sufficiently large odd order:
Theorem 1.51
(Borel edge-colorings of Schreier graphs)
.
Suppose that
|F|\geqslant 2
and for each
\sigma\in F
, the order of
\sigma
is either even or at least
2^{30}\,|F|\log|F|
. Let
\bm{a}\colon\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be a free Borel action of
\Gamma
on a standard Borel space
X
and let
G\coloneqq\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F)
be the corresponding Schreier graph. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant|F|+1
.
When
|F|\leqslant 1
, the corresponding Schreier graphs
G
trivially satisfy
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant|F|
, so there is no harm in only considering the
|F|\geqslant 2
case. The assumption that
|F|\geqslant 2
in Theorem
1.51
ensures that
\log|F|>0
. We made no attempt to optimize the constant factor
2^{30}
. Note that Theorem
1.51
applies to some groups of exponential growth thanks to Theorem
1.13
(a Borel version of Vizing’s theorem for graphs of subexponential growth is already known
[
BD23
]
).
Theorem
1.51
cannot be easily derived using Theorem
1.42
, because no
o(\log n)
-time randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for Vizing’s theorem is known (although the existence of such an algorithm has not been ruled out), with the most efficient currently available algorithm running in time
O(\log^{2}n)
[
BD23a
]
. Instead, we prove Theorem
1.51
by invoking our Borel LLL (i.e., Theorem
1.31
) directly. More precisely, we apply Theorem
1.31
to prove a certain general result about Borel independent complete sections, which is described in §
1.4.4
, and then use it to deduce Theorem
1.51
.
1.4.4.
Borel independent complete sections
For a graph
G
, a set
I\subseteq V(G)
is
G
-independent
if no two vertices in
I
are adjacent. If
\mathcal{F}
is a family of pairwise disjoint sets, then a
complete section
for
\mathcal{F}
is a set
S
that includes at least one element from every set in
\mathcal{F}
. The following classical fact is proved by a simple application of the LLL:
Theorem 1.52
(Alon
[
Alo88
,
Alo94
,
AS16
]
)
.
Let
G
be a graph of finite maximum degree
\Delta
and let
\mathcal{F}
be a family of pairwise disjoint subsets of
V(G)
. If
|S|\geqslant 2e\Delta
for all
S\in\mathcal{F}
, then there is a
G
-independent complete section for
\mathcal{F}
.
Theorem
1.52
, and independent complete sections in general, have been a subject of considerable interest, not least because of their many applications to other combinatorial problems.
∥
∥
∥
A related notion considered in the literature is that of an independent transversal—i.e., a set that meets each member of
\mathcal{F}
in
exactly
one point. For our purposes, working with complete sections will be more natural. Note that the existence of a
G
-independent complete section for
\mathcal{F}
is equivalent to the existence of a
G
-independent transversal (although this equivalence may fail in the Borel setting).
The required lower bound on the sizes of the sets in
\mathcal{F}
has been subsequently improved from
2e\Delta
to
2\Delta
by Haxell
[
Hax01
]
, which is best possible
[
BES75
]
.
We prove a Borel version of Theorem
1.52
under a finite asymptotic separation index assumption. To simplify the statement of our result, we say that a set
S\subseteq V(G)
is a
complete section
for a graph
G
if it meets every connected component of
G
. In other words,
S
is a complete section for
G
if and only if it is a complete section for the following family of sets:
\mathcal{F}\,\coloneqq\,\{V(C)\,:\,\text{$C$ is a connected component of $G$}\}.
A subgraph
G^{\prime}
of
G
is
spanning
if
V(G^{\prime})=V(G)
.
Theorem 1.53
(Borel independent complete sections)
.
Let
H
be a Borel graph with
\mathsf{asi}(H)<\infty
and let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be two spanning Borel subgraphs of
H
. Suppose that the maximum degree of
G_{1}
is at most
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\Delta\geqslant 2
. If every component of
G_{2}
contains at least
2^{25}\,\Delta\log\Delta
vertices, then there is a Borel
G_{1}
-independent complete section for
G_{2}
.
The assumption that
\mathsf{asi}(H)<\infty
in Theorem
1.53
cannot be removed. Indeed, Marks
[
Mar16
, Theorem 1.6]
gave an example of a Borel graph
H
and two spanning Borel subgraphs
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
such that both
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
have maximum degree
2
, all their components are infinite, and every Borel set
A\subseteq V(H)
either includes an entire component of
G_{1}
(and hence it is definitely not
G_{1}
-independent), or misses a component of
G_{2}
(i.e., it is not a complete section for
G_{2}
).
The usual proof of Theorem
1.52
is one of the rare applications of the LLL for which the bound
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant 1/e
is sharp and a polynomial criterion is insufficient. As a result, we cannot establish Theorem
1.53
by simply following the standard proof of Theorem
1.52
and substituting Theorem
1.29
or Theorem
1.31
in place of the LLL. Nevertheless, we are able to prove Theorem
1.53
using a different approach, which unfortunately results in a superlinear lower bound on the size of the components of
G_{2}
. However, we suspect that the conclusion of Theorem
1.53
should hold even if the components of
G_{2}
have size bounded below by some linear function of
\Delta
:
Conjecture 1.54
.
There exist constants
C
,
\Delta_{0}>0
with the following property: Let
H
be a Borel graph with
\mathsf{asi}(H)<\infty
and let
G_{1}
,
G_{2}
be two spanning Borel subgraphs of
H
. Suppose that the maximum degree of
G_{1}
is at most
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\Delta\geqslant\Delta_{0}
. If every component of
G_{2}
contains at least
C\Delta
vertices, then there is a Borel
G_{1}
-independent complete section for
G_{2}
.
One way to settle Conjecture
1.54
would be to prove a version of Theorem
1.31
under the usual LLL assumption
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)\leqslant 1/e
. Since we suspect that
\Delta\log\Delta
is not the optimal order of magnitude in Theorem
1.53
, we made no attempt to optimize the constant factor
2^{25}
. We prove Theorem
1.53
in §
4.3
and then use it to derive Theorem
1.51
in §
4.4
. If Conjecture
1.54
is true, then the same argument would improve Theorem
1.51
by replacing the bound
2^{30}\,|F|\log|F|
by a linear function of
|F|
. We expect that this can also be achieved by following a “nibbling” approach similar to the one in the paper
[
RS02
]
by Reed and Sudakov, but the resulting argument would be quite technical, and since we do not see how to generalize it to prove Conjecture
1.54
in full, we do not pursue it in this paper.
2.  Preliminaries
2.1.  The spaces of finite sets and constraints
In this subsection, given a standard Borel space
X
and an integer
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, we describe a way of endowing the set
\mathsf{Const}(X,q)
of all
(X,q)
-constraints with the structure of a standard Borel space (this is necessary for Definition
1.23
to make sense). The material in this subsection is entirely standard; nevertheless, we decided to include it here for completeness.
Let
X
be a standard Borel space. We begin by equipping
[X]^{k}
for
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
with the structure of a standard Borel space. To this end, let
(X)^{k}
be the set of all
k
-tuples
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1})\in X^{k}
of distinct points, and let
\sim_{k}
be the equivalence relation on
(X)^{k}
given by
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1})\,\sim_{k}\,(y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1})\quad\vcentcolon\Longleftrightarrow\quad\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{k-1}\}\,=\,\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{k-1}\}.
By
[
KM04
, Example 6.1 and Proposition 6.3]
, the quotient space
[X]^{k}=(X)^{k}/\sim_{k}
is standard Borel. It follows that the disjoint union
{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}=[X]^{0}\sqcup[X]^{1}\sqcup[X]^{2}\sqcup\ldots
is standard Borel as well
[
Tse16
, Proposition 1.4]
.
Next, given
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
, we let
{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
be the set of all partial functions
\varphi\colon X\dashrightarrow q
such that
\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)
is a finite subset of
X
. Each
\varphi\in{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
can be identified with a finite subset of
X\times q
, namely, with its graph
\{(x,\varphi(x))\,:\,x\in\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)\}
. Using this identification, it is routine to check that
{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
is a Borel subset of
{[{{X\times q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
.
Finally, since an
(X,q)
-constraint is a finite subset of
{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
, we may define
\mathsf{Const}(X,q)\,\coloneqq\,\big\{B\in{[{{{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}}}]^{{<\infty}}}\,:\,\text{all functions in $B$ have the same domain}\big\}.
It can be easily verified that
\mathsf{Const}(X,q)
is a Borel subset of the standard Borel space
{[{{{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}}}]^{{<\infty}}}
, and therefore, equipped with the relative
\sigma
-algebra of Borel sets, it is also standard Borel.
2.2.  Borel combinatorics on component-finite graphs
As mentioned in the introduction, the reason we expect graphs with finite asymptotic separation index to have well-behaved Borel combinatorics is that Borel combinatorics “trivialize” on component-finite graphs. The following statement makes this intuition rigorous:
Proposition 2.1
(Borel combinatorics on component-finite graphs)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
. If the graph
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is component-finite and
\mathscr{B}
has a solution, then it has a Borel solution as well.
The proof of Proposition
2.1
relies on the following deep and classical result (which we shall use throughout the paper without mention):
Theorem 2.2
(Luzin–Novikov uniformization
[
Kec95
, Theorem 18.10]
)
.
Let
X
,
Y
be standard Borel spaces and let
R\subseteq X\times Y
be a Borel subset. If for every
x\in X
, the set
R_{x}\,\coloneqq\,\{y\in Y\,:\,(x,y)\in R\}
is countable, then there exists a sequence
(f_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of Borel partial functions
f_{n}\colon X\dashrightarrow Y
defined on Borel subsets of
X
such that for all
x\in X
,
R_{x}=\{f_{n}(x)\,:\,n\in{\mathbb{N}},\ x\in\mathrm{dom}(f_{n})\}
.
If additionally each set
R_{x}
is nonempty, then the mapping
f\colon X\to Y
given by
f(x)\,\coloneqq\,f_{n(x)}(x),\text{ where }n(x)\,\coloneqq\,\min\{n\in{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,x\in\mathrm{dom}(f_{n})\},
is a Borel function such that
f(x)\in R_{x}
for all
x\in X
.
Proof
of Proposition
2.1
.
This is a standard argument in descriptive combinatorics, so we only provide a proof sketch. Roughly, since on each component of
G_{\mathscr{B}}
the problem can be solved in only finitely many ways, the Luzin–Novikov theorem yields a Borel choice of one solution on every component; putting the chosen solutions together solves the problem on the whole space.
In a little more detail, let
\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\{V(C)\,:\,\text{$C$ is a connected component of $G_{\mathscr{B}}$}\}
. This is a Borel subset of
{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}
. Say that a
finite partial solution
to
\mathscr{B}
is a map
\varphi\in{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
such that
\varphi
satisfies every constraint
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
\mathrm{dom}(B)\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)
. Then the set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{[{{X}\dashrightarrow{q}}]^{{<\infty}}}
of all finite partial solutions to
\mathscr{B}
is also Borel. Since
\mathscr{B}
has a solution, for each finite set
U\subseteq X
, there is some
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}
with
\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)=U
. Let
R\,\coloneqq\,\{(U,\varphi)\in\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{S}\,:\,\mathrm{dom}(\varphi)=U\}.
Then
R
is a Borel subset of
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{S}
, and for each
U\in\mathcal{C}
, the set
R_{U}\coloneqq\{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}\,:\,(U,\varphi)\in R\}
is finite and nonempty. Therefore, by the Luzin–Novikov theorem (Theorem
2.2
), there exists a Borel function
\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{S}\colon U\mapsto\varphi_{U}
such that
\mathrm{dom}(\varphi_{U})=U
for all
U
. For each
x\in X
, let
[x]\in\mathcal{C}
be the vertex set of the component containing
x
. Then the map
f\colon X\to q
given by
f(x)\,\coloneqq\,\varphi_{[x]}(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in X,
or, equivalently,
f\coloneqq\bigsqcup_{U\in\mathcal{C}}\varphi_{U}
, is a Borel solution to
\mathscr{B}
, as desired.
∎
Corollary 2.3.
If
G
is a component-finite Borel graph and
\chi(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\chi(G)
.
Proof
.
Clearly,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\geqslant\chi(G)
. To see that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant\chi(G)
, recall from Example
1.24
that the proper
\chi(G)
-coloring problem for
G
can be encoded via a Borel CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon V(G)\to^{?}\chi(G)
given by
\mathscr{B}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\{(u\mapsto i,\,v\mapsto i)\,:\,0\leqslant i<\chi(G)\}\,:\,uv\in E(G)\}.
Since
G_{\mathscr{B}}=G
is component-finite, it has a Borel proper
\chi(G)
-coloring by Proposition
2.1
.
∎
Another useful consequence of the Luzin–Novikov theorem is the fact that if
f\colon X\to Y
is a countable-to-one Borel function between standard Borel spaces, then its image
f(X)
is a Borel subset of
Y
[
Kec95
, Exercise 18.14]
.

3.1.  Proof of Theorem

As stated in §

Definition 3.1
(Shattering number)

Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q

of
\mathscr{B}

\mathcal{P}\subseteq{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}

\mathcal{P}
is a partition of

and for each
B\in\mathscr{B}

\mathrm{dom}(B)
intersects at most
s

\mathcal{P}
(if no such

Some readers may be more familiar with describing a partition of a standard Borel space

with finite classes. For their benefit, we remark that a partition
\mathcal{P}
is a Borel subset of
{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}
if and only if the equivalence relation whose classes are the members of
\mathcal{P}
is a Borel subset of
X^{2}
[
BY23

.
Let us observe two easy upper bounds on the shattering number of a Borel CSP.
Lemma 3.2.
If
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space

(i)
\mathsf{sh}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant\mathsf{si}(G_{\mathscr{B}})+1

(ii)
\mathsf{sh}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}|\mathrm{dom}(B)|

Proof
.
For
(i)
, suppose that
X=U_{0}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{s}
is a partition witnessing
\mathsf{si}(G_{\mathscr{B}})=s

\mathcal{P}\,\coloneqq\,\{V(C)\,:\,\text{$C$ is a connected component of $G[U_{i}]$ for some $0\leqslant i\leqslant s$}\}
is a family of finite sets witnessing
\mathsf{sh}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant s+1
. For
(ii)
, take
\mathcal{P}=\{\{x\}\,:\,x\in X\}
.
∎
We can now state the strengthening of Theorem
1.29
using the shattering number:
Theorem 3.3
(Borel LLL with bounded shattering number)
.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X

\mathsf{sh}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant s
for some
s\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}

(3.1)
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{s}\,\leqslant\,e^{-s},
then
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution

Theorem
1.29
is a direct consequence of Theorem
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
(i)
. On the other hand, combining Theorem
3.3
with Lemma
3.2
(ii)
gives a conclusion reminiscent of, though slightly weaker than,
[
Ber23a
, Corollary 1.8]
.
The technique we employ to prove Theorem
3.3
is known as the
method of conditional probabilities
in computer science, where it is commonly used for derandomization
[
AS16
,
MR95
]
. This method has already been applied to obtain “constructive” versions of the LLL, for instance by Beck
[
Bec91
]
, Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
, and the first named author
[
Ber23a
]

[

by Fischer and Ghaffari, which uses so-called network decompositions to design efficient distributed algorithms in a way that is rather analogous to our use of the shattering number.
Our proof of Theorem
3.3
is inductive: we shall construct the desired
q
-coloring of
X
that solves
\mathscr{B}
by iteratively coloring subsets of
X
. Throughout the iterations, the domains of the constraints will be shrinking until they become empty. Note that there are precisely two constraints with the empty domain:
\varnothing
and
\{\varnothing\}
. The constraint
\{\varnothing\}
has probability
1/q^{0}=1
and is violated by every coloring, while the constraint
\varnothing
has probability
0
and is always satisfied. The distinction between these two cases will play a decisive role in the last stage of our proof.
To facilitate the analysis of the iterative process, the following notation will be useful. Fix a set
X
and an integer
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
and let
f\colon U\to q
be a
q
-coloring of a subset
U\subseteq X
. For an
(X,q)
-constraint
B
, we write
B/f
to denote the constraint with
\mathrm{dom}(B/f)\coloneqq\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus U
given by
B/f\,\coloneqq\,\{\varphi\colon(\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus U)\to q\,:\,{{f}|_{\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U}}\,\sqcup\,\varphi\,\in\,B\}.
In other words,
\varphi\in B/f
if and only if the coloring
f\sqcup\varphi
violates
B
. We emphasize that it is possible to have
\mathrm{dom}(B)\subseteq U
, in which case
\mathrm{dom}(B/f)=\varnothing
. More specifically, if
\mathrm{dom}(B)\subseteq U
, then
B/f\,=\,\begin{cases}\{\varnothing\}&\text{if $f$ violates $B$};\\
\varnothing&\text{if $f$ satisfies $B$}.\end{cases}
Observe that if
f^{\prime}\colon U^{\prime}\to q
is another
q
-coloring of some set
U^{\prime}\subseteq X\setminus U
, then
(B/f)/f^{\prime}\,=\,B/(f\sqcup f^{\prime}).
Given a CSP
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
, we define a CSP
\mathscr{B}/f\colon(X\setminus U)\to^{?}q
by
\mathscr{B}/f\,\coloneqq\,\{B/f\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}\}.
By construction,
f^{\prime}\colon(X\setminus U)\to q
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}/f
if and only if
f\sqcup f^{\prime}
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}
. Note that we always have
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}/f)\leqslant\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
, but there is no
a priori
relation between
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B}/f)
and
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
.
The following lemma describes the inductive step in the proof of Theorem
3.3
:
Lemma 3.4.
Let
\mathscr{B}\colon X\to^{?}q
be a Borel CSP on a standard Borel space
X
and let
s\colon\mathscr{B}\to{\mathbb{N}}
be a Borel function such that for all
B\in\mathscr{B}
,
(3.2)
\mathbb{P}[B]\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{s(B)}\,<\,e^{-s(B)}.
Let
U\subseteq X
be a Borel set such that the graph
G_{\mathscr{B}}[U]
is component-finite. For each
B\in\mathscr{B}
, define
\eta(B)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}1&\text{if $\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U\neq\varnothing$};\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
Then there exists a Borel function
f\colon U\to q
such that for all
B\in\mathscr{B}
,
(3.3)
\mathbb{P}[B/f]\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}/f)+1)^{s(B)-\eta(B)}\,<\,e^{-s(B)+\eta(B)}.
Proof
.
Note that if
B\in\mathscr{B}
is a constraint such that
\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U=\varnothing
, then (
3.3
) holds for this
B
regardless of the choice of
f\colon U\to q
, since
B/f=B
,
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}/f)\leqslant\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
, and
\eta(B)=0
. Therefore, we only need to focus on the constraints
B\in\mathscr{B}
with
\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U\neq\varnothing
. Consider any such constraint
B

\psi\colon(\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U)\to q

probability of
B
conditioned on
\psi
be
\mathbb{P}[B\,|\,\psi]\,\coloneqq\,\frac{|\{\varphi\in B\,:\,{{\varphi}|_{\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U}}=\psi\}|}{q^{|\mathrm{dom}(B)\setminus U|}}.
In other words,
\mathbb{P}[B\,|\,\psi]
is the probability that a uniformly random
q
-coloring of
\mathrm{dom}(B)
violates
B
given that it agrees with
\psi
on
\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U
. Observe that for any
f\colon U\to q
, we have
(3.4)
\mathbb{P}[B/f]\,=\,\mathbb{P}[B\,|\,{{f}|_{\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U}}].
Let
B^{\ast}
be the constraint with
\mathrm{dom}(B^{\ast})\coloneqq\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U
given by
B^{\ast}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\psi\colon(\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U)\to q\ :\ \mathbb{P}[B\,|\,\psi]\,\geqslant\,(e(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1))^{-s(B)+1}\big\}.
Simple double counting shows that
\displaystyle(e(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1))^{-s(B)}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\eqref{eq:key_lemma_assumption}}}}{{>}}\,\mathbb{P}[B]\,=\,q^{-|\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U|}\sum_{\psi\colon(\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U)\to q}\mathbb{P}[B\,|\,\psi]
\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\geqslant\,q^{-|\mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U|}\,|B^{\ast}|\,(e(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1))^{-s(B)+1}\,=\,\mathbb{P}[B^{\ast}]\,(e(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1))^{-s(B)+1}.
(This is essentially Markov’s inequality from probability theory.) We conclude that
(3.5)
\mathbb{P}[B^{\ast}]\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)\,<\,1/e.
Now let
\mathscr{B}^{\ast}\colon U\to^{?}q
be the Borel CSP defined by
\mathscr{B}^{\ast}\,\coloneqq\,\{B^{\ast}\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B},\ \mathrm{dom}(B)\cap U\neq\varnothing\}.
Since
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B}^{\ast})\leqslant\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
by construction, it follows from (

) that
\mathscr{B}
satisfies the assumptions of the ordinary LLL, i.e., of Lemma
1.21
. Therefore,
\mathscr{B}^{\ast}
has a solution. Note that
G_{\mathscr{B}^{\ast}}=G_{\mathscr{B}}[U]
, which is component-finite by hypothesis. Thus,
\mathscr{B}^{\ast}
has a Borel solution

by Proposition
2.1
. We conclude from (

), the definition of

, and the inequality

that

is as desired.
∎

.

\mathcal{P}\subseteq[X]^{<\infty}

3.2.  From the LLL to
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms
As briefly mentioned in the introduction, LCL problems and
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms can be defined in the situation when the underlying graph is equipped with additional combinatorial structure, such as an orientation, a labeling, etc. Since we will need this general set-up for the proof of Theorem
1.31
, we recall the necessary definitions here. Our presentation follows that in
[
Ber23
, §2.A]
.
For a set
X
, let
X^{<\infty}\coloneqq X^{0}\sqcup X^{1}\sqcup X^{2}\sqcup\ldots
be the set of all finite tuples of elements of
X
.
Definition 3.5
(Structured graphs)
.
Let
G
be a graph. A
structure map
on
G
is a partial function
\sigma\colon V(G)^{<\infty}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{N}}
such that for some
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, every tuple
\bm{x}\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
is of length at most
\ell
. A
structured graph
is a pair
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
, where
G
is a graph and
\sigma
is a structure map on
G
.
Examples 3.6
.
(i)
A labeled graph
(G,f)
in the sense of Definition
1.35
can be viewed as a structured graph by identifying
V(G)
with
V(G)^{1}
in the obvious way.
(ii)
A directed graph
G
can be viewed as a structured graph by encoding the directions of the arcs of
G
using the function
\sigma\colon V(G)^{2}\to 2
that sends a pair
(u,v)
to
1
if and only if there is an arc from
u
to
v
in
G
.
(iii)
An ordered graph is a pair
(G,\preccurlyeq)
, where
G
is a graph and
\preccurlyeq
is a linear order on
V(G)
. These objects are of interest in extremal combinatorics
[
Tar19
]
. An ordered graph can be represented by a structured graph using the map
\sigma\colon V(G)^{2}\to 2
, where
\sigma(u,v)=1
if and only if
u\preccurlyeq v
.
A structured graph
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
is
Borel
if
G
is a Borel graph and
\sigma
is a Borel function (that is,
\sigma^{-1}(i)
is a Borel subset of
V(G)^{<\infty}
for all
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
).
We naturally extend standard graph-theoretic notation and terminology to structured graphs. For example, given a structured graph
\bm{G}=(G,\sigma)
, we write
V(\bm{G})\coloneqq V(G)
and
E(\bm{G})\coloneqq E(G)
. Similarly, given
v\in V(\bm{G})
and
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we let
B_{\bm{G}}(v,R)\,\coloneqq\,B_{G}(v,R)\,\subseteq\,V(\bm{G}).
For a subset
U\subseteq V(\bm{G})
, the subgraph of
\bm{G}
induced
by
U
is the structured graph
\bm{G}[U]
given by
\bm{G}[U]\,\coloneqq\,\big(G[U],{{\sigma}|_{U^{<\infty}}}\big).
Isomorphisms between structured graphs are required to preserve the values of the structure maps. We also let
\mathsf{asi}(\bm{G})\coloneqq\mathsf{asi}(G)
.
With this we can define labelings of structured graphs, rooted (labeled) structured graphs, LCL problems and their solutions,
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms, and deterministic and randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
complexity of LCL problems on structured graphs by simply repeating Definitions
1.35
,
1.37
,
1.38
, and
1.39
with every occurrence of the word “graph” replaced by “structured graph.” Theorems
1.40
and
1.41
remain true in this more general context
[
Ber23
, Theorems 2.10 and 2.14]
, and the same is true for our Theorem
1.42
:
Theorem 3.7
(Theorem
1.42
for structured graphs)
.
Let
\Pi
be an LCL problem and let
\mathbb{G}
be a class of
\Pi
-colorable finite structured graphs such that

3.3.  From
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms to the LLL
In this section we verify the only remaining implication from the diagram in §
3.2
, namely (C): Theorem
3.7
\Longrightarrow
Theorem
1.31
. Our argument follows the strategy outlined in
[
Ber23
, §5.D]
. The key point is that there exist efficient randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms that solve CSPs satisfying a polynomial LLL criterion, and combining such an algorithm with Theorem
3.7
yields Borel solutions to such CSPs when the associated graph has finite asymptotic separation index.
To make the above idea precise, we need to be able to encode a given CSP as an LCL problem on an auxiliary structured graph. There are several natural ways of doing so; here we follow the approach described in
[
Ber23
, §5.D]
.
Definition 3.11
(Graph-CSPs)
.
A
graph-CSP
with range
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
is a pair
(G,\mathscr{B})
, where
G
is a graph and
\mathscr{B}\colon V(G)\to^{?}q
is a bounded CSP such that
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})<\infty
and
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is a subgraph of
G
.
A graph-CSP
(G,\mathscr{B})
can be viewed as a structured graph in the following way. For each tuple of vertices
\bm{v}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1})\in V(G)^{<\infty}
, the structure map
\sigma
will contain the information about the constraints
B\in\mathscr{B}
such that
\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1}\}
. Formally, let
(V(G))^{<\infty}
be the set of all finite tuples of distinct vertices and for each
\bm{v}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1})\in(V(G))^{<\infty}
, define
\mathscr{B}_{\bm{v}}\,\coloneqq\,\{B\in\mathscr{B}\,:\,\mathrm{dom}(B)=\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1}\}\}.
The domain of
\sigma
is the set of all
\bm{v}\in(V(G))^{<\infty}
with
\mathscr{B}_{\bm{v}}\neq\varnothing
. Note that since
\mathscr{B}
is bounded,
\sigma
is defined on tuples of bounded length. For
\bm{v}=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1})\in\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
, let
\iota_{\bm{v}}\colon\{v_{0},\ldots,v_{k-1}\}\to k
be the bijection given by
\iota_{\bm{v}}(x_{i})\coloneqq i
, and for each
B\in\mathscr{B}_{\bm{v}}
, define
B_{\bm{v}}^{\ast}\,\coloneqq\,\{\varphi\colon k\to q\,:\,\varphi\circ\iota_{\bm{v}}\in B\}.
Note that the mapping
B^{\ast}_{\bm{v}}\to B\colon\varphi\mapsto\varphi\circ\iota_{\bm{v}}
is a bijection. Now let the
type
of
\bm{v}
be
\mathsf{type}(\bm{v})\,\coloneqq\,\{B^{\ast}_{\bm{v}}\,:\,B\in\mathscr{B}_{\bm{v}}\}.
Observe that the set
\mathsf{Types}
of all possible types of tuples in
\mathrm{dom}(\sigma)
is countable (indeed, it is a subset of
{[{{{[{{{[{{{\mathbb{N}}}\dashrightarrow{{\mathbb{N}}}}]^{{<\infty}}}}}]^{{<\infty}}}}}]^{{<\infty}}}
), so we can fix an arbitrary injection
\mathsf{code}\colon\mathsf{Types}\to{\mathbb{N}}
and let
\sigma(\bm{v})\,\coloneqq\,\mathsf{code}(\mathsf{type}(\bm{v})).
The pair
(G,\sigma)
now represents exactly the same information as
(G,\mathscr{B})
, and since
\sigma
is defined on tuples of bounded length,
(G,\sigma)
is a structured graph (as in Definition
3.5
). Moreover, it is routine to check that if
G
is a Borel graph and
\mathscr{B}
is a Borel CSP, then
\sigma
is a Borel function, and thus
(G,\sigma)
is a Borel structured graph.
Let
(G,\mathscr{B})
be a graph-CSP. The requirement that
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is a subgraph of
G
means that the
1
-ball around every vertex of
G
includes the domains of all constraints that involve that vertex. That is, the problem of solving a graph-CSP is an LCL problem of radius
1
, which we denote by
\Pi_{\mathrm{CSP},q}
(here
q\in{\mathbb{N}}^{+}
is the range of
\mathscr{B}
). Given parameters
q
,
k
,
p
, and
d
, let
\mathbb{CSP}(q,k,p,d)
be the class of all finite graph-CSPs
(G,\mathscr{B})
(viewed as structured graphs) such that:
—
the range of
\mathscr{B}
is
q
;
—
\sup_{B\in\mathscr{B}}|\mathrm{dom}(B)|\leqslant k
;
—
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant p
and
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant d
.
After these technical preliminaries, we can rigorously formulate a result regarding
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithms for the LLL that we shall use:
Theorem 3.12
(Ghaffari–Harris–Kuhn
[
GHK18
]
)
.
If
q
,
k
,
p
, and
d
satisfy
p(d+1)^{8}\leqslant 2^{-15}
, then
\mathsf{Rand}_{\Pi_{\mathrm{CSP},q},\,\mathbb{CSP}(q,k,p,d)}(n)\,=\,\exp(O(\sqrt{\log\log n}))\,=\,o(\log n).
The first
o(\log n)
-time randomized
\mathsf{LOCAL}
algorithm for the LLL with a polynomial criterion is due to Fischer and Ghaffari
[
FG17
]
. We are using the Ghaffari–Harris–Kuhn version instead because it improves the criterion from
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)^{32}\leqslant e^{-32}
to
\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{d}+1)^{8}\leqslant 2^{-15}
. In subsequent work, considerable effort has been put into making the running time of the algorithm even lower
[
RG20
,
Dav23
]
. On the other hand, we are not aware of any improvements to the required bound on
\mathsf{p}
as a function of
\mathsf{d}
, and it remains an open problem whether the exponent

can be reduced, ideally all the way down to
1
.
With Theorem
3.12
in hand, we are ready to deduce Theorem
1.31
from Theorem
3.7
:
Proof
of implication
(C)

Let

In this subsection, we prove Theorem

and

\sigma(v)\coloneqq 0
and
\sigma(e)\coloneqq 1
. This defines a function

\bm{G^{\prime}}\coloneqq(G^{\prime},\sigma)

G
, distinguished from each other via the map

. Proper
q
-edge-coloring of

can be encoded as an LCL problem on
\bm{G^{\prime}}
of radius
2
, which requires every vertex
x
of
\bm{G^{\prime}}
with

to receive a label in

by paths of length
2
.
Fix
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}

.
Combining a result of Chang, He, Li, Pettie, and Uitto
[

,
G_{1}^{\prime}\coloneqq G_{1}[U]
, and let
G_{2}^{\prime}
be the spanning subgraph of
G_{2}[U]
obtained by only retaining the edges of the graphs
G_{2}[R]
with
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
(in other words,
G_{2}^{\prime}
is the graph whose connected components are precisely the graphs
G_{2}[R]
with
R\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
). Then
H^{\prime}
,
G_{1}^{\prime}
, and
G_{2}^{\prime}
are Borel graphs satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
4.3
. Furthermore, by construction, every component of
G_{2}^{\prime}
contains exactly
k
vertices. Suppose we can find a Borel
G_{1}^{\prime}
-independent complete section
S
for
G_{2}^{\prime}
. Then
S
is
G_{1}
-independent (because
G_{1}^{\prime}
is an induced subgraph of
G_{1}
) and a complete section for
G_{2}
(because every component of
G_{2}
includes a component of
G_{2}^{\prime}
). Therefore, proving the theorem under the assumption that every component of
G_{2}
contains exactly
k
vertices implies the theorem in general.
Let
G\coloneqq G_{1}
and
\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\{V(C)\,:\,\text{$C$ is a connected component of $G_{2}$}\}
. Then
\mathcal{F}
is a Borel subset of
[V]^{k}
, and our goal is to find a Borel
G
-independent complete section for
\mathcal{F}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that each set
F\in\mathcal{F}
is
G
-independent. To see this, let
G^{\prime}
be the spanning subgraph of
G
in which two vertices are adjacent if and only if they are adjacent in
G
and do not belong to the same set in
\mathcal{F}
(so each set
F\in\mathcal{F}
is
G^{\prime}
-independent by construction). Suppose we can find a Borel
G^{\prime}
-independent complete section
S
for
\mathcal{F}
. Using the Luzin–Novikov theorem (Theorem
2.2
), we can choose, for each
F\in\mathcal{F}
, a single vertex
v_{F}\in S\cap F
so that the mapping
\mathcal{F}\to S\colon F\mapsto v_{F}
is Borel. Then
S^{\prime}\coloneqq\{v_{F}\,:\,F\in\mathcal{F}\}
is a Borel subset of
S
that meets every set in
\mathcal{F}
in exactly one vertex. It follows that
S^{\prime}
is
G
-independent, so we have found a Borel
G
-independent complete section for
\mathcal{F}
.
With these preparations, we may start the main part of the proof. Given a
\Delta
-coloring
f\colon V\to\Delta
, we define a set
S_{f}\subseteq V
by
S_{f}\,\coloneqq\,\{v\in V\,:\,f(v)=0\text{ and }f(u)\neq 0\text{ for all }uv\in E(G)\}.
By construction,
S_{f}
is a
G
-independent set, and if
f
is a Borel function, then
S_{f}
is Borel. Our aim is to find a Borel function
f\colon V\to\Delta
such that
S_{f}
meets every set in
\mathcal{F}
.
The membership of a vertex
v
in
S_{f}
is determined by the values of
f
at
v
and the neighbors of
v
. Thus, whether
S_{f}
meets a set
F\in\mathcal{F}
only depends on the restriction of
f
to
B_{G}(F,1)
, i.e., the set of all vertices that are in
F
or have a
G
-neighbor in
F
. Hence, we can define a
(V,\Delta)
-constraint
B_{F}
with domain
B_{G}(F,1)
such that
f\colon V\to\Delta
satisfies
B_{F}
if and only if
S_{f}
meets
F
. Explicitly,
B_{F}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\varphi\colon B_{G}(F,1)\to\Delta\ :\ \text{for all $v\in F$, $\varphi(v)\neq 0$ or $\varphi(u)=0$ for some $uv\in E(G)$}\big\}.
Let
\mathscr{B}\coloneqq\{B_{F}\,:\,F\in\mathcal{F}\}
. This is a bounded Borel CSP such that if
f\colon V\to\Delta
is a solution to
\mathscr{B}
, then
S_{f}
is a Borel
G
-independent complete section for
\mathcal{F}
. It remains to argue that
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution, to which end we shall employ Theorem
1.31
.
To begin with, we observe that
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})<\infty
. Indeed, if
u
and
v
are neighbors in
G_{\mathscr{B}}
, then there is some
F\in\mathcal{F}
such that
u
,
v\in B_{G}(F,1)
. Since
H[F]
is a connected graph with at most
k
vertices, it follows that
u
and
v
are joined by a path of at most
k+1
edges in
H
. Thus,
G_{\mathscr{B}}
is a subgraph of
H^{k+1}
, and hence
\mathsf{asi}(G_{\mathscr{B}})\leqslant\mathsf{asi}(H)<\infty
. We will not use the graph
H
again, so all the references to neighbors, adjacency, edges, etc. in the sequel are in relation to the graph
G
.
Next we need to bound the parameters
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
and
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
. We start with
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})
, which is the easier one. Take
F\in\mathcal{F}
and suppose that
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}\setminus\{F\}
satisfies
B_{G}(F^{\prime},1)\cap B_{G}(F,1)\neq\varnothing
. Consider any vertex
v\in B_{G}(F,1)\cap B_{G}(F^{\prime},1)
. Since
v\in B_{G}(F,1)
, there are at most
k+\Delta k
choices for
v
. As
v\in B_{G}(F^{\prime},1)
, either
v
itself or a neighbor of
v
is in
F^{\prime}
. Since the sets in
\mathcal{F}
are disjoint, it follows that, given
v
, there are at most
1+\Delta
choices for
F^{\prime}
. In total, for fixed
F
, there are at most
(k+\Delta k)(1+\Delta)-1
options for
F^{\prime}
(we are subtracting
1
since
F
itself should not be counted), so
\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})\,\leqslant\,(k+\Delta k)(1+\Delta)-1\,\leqslant\,4\Delta^{2}k-1\,\leqslant\,2^{27}\Delta^{5}-1.
Now we turn our attention to
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})
. Consider any set
F\in\mathcal{F}
. We need to bound
\mathbb{P}[B_{F}]
, i.e., the probability that a uniformly random
\Delta
-coloring
\varphi\colon B_{G}(F,1)\to\Delta
belongs to the set
B_{F}
. Let
N
be the set of all neighbors of the vertices in
F
, and let
\psi\colon N\to\Delta
and
\xi\colon F\to\Delta
be the restrictions of
\varphi
to
N
and
F
respectively. By our assumption, the set
F
is
G
-independent, so,
B_{G}(F,1)=F\sqcup N
, which means that
\psi
and
\xi
are chosen independently and uniformly at random from the sets of all
\Delta
-colorings of
N
and
F
respectively. Let
(4.2)
F^{\ast}\,\coloneqq\,\{v\in F\,:\,\psi(u)\neq 0\text{ for all neighbors }u\text{ of }v\}.
Note that
\varphi\in B_{F}
if and only if
\xi(v)\neq 0
for all
v\in F^{\ast}
. Therefore,
(4.3)
\mathbb{P}[B_{F}]\,=\,\mathbb{P}[\xi(v)\neq 0\text{ for all }v\in F^{\ast}].
The key observation is that
|F^{\ast}|
is unlikely to be too small:
Claim 4.1.
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[|F^{\ast}|<k/8]\,\leqslant\,4\Delta^{-500}.
Proof.
Observe that, since
(1-1/x)^{x}\geqslant(1-1/2)^{2}=1/4
for all
x\geqslant 2
, we have
\mathbb{E}[|F^{\ast}|]\,\geqslant\,|F|\left(1-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)^{\Delta}\,\geqslant\,\frac{k}{4}.
Therefore, we need to find an upper bound on the probability that
|F^{\ast}|<\mathbb{E}[|F^{\ast}|]-k/8
. To this end, we shall apply a concentration of measure result that is often used in probabilistic combinatorics and is a consequence of Talagrand’s isoperimetric inequality
[
Tal95
]
. There are several variants of this inequality in the literature
[
MR14
,
BJ18
,
KP20
]
, and designing new and improved versions of it is an active area of research. We shall use a (simplified form of) the recent formulation due to Delcourt and Postle
[
DP22
]
. Given two finite strings
{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
and
{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})
, and a vector
{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we let
\mathsf{dist}_{{c}}({x},{y})\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{i\,:\,x_{i}\neq y_{i}}|c_{i}|.
Theorem 4.2
(Talagrand’s inequality; Delcourt–Postle
[
DP22
, Theorem 4.4]
)
.
Let
(\Omega_{1},\mu_{1})
, …,
(\Omega_{n},\mu_{n})
be discrete probability spaces and let
(\Omega,\mu)\coloneqq\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\Omega_{i},\mu_{i})
be their product space. Suppose that
X\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-negative random variable satisfying the following for some
r
,
d>0
:
(T)
For each
{\omega}\in\Omega
, there exists a vector
{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|{c}\|_{1}\leqslant rX(\omega)
,
\|{c}\|_{\infty}\leqslant d
, and for all
{\omega}^{\prime}\in\Omega
, we have
X({\omega}^{\prime})\geqslant X(\omega)-\mathsf{dist}_{{c}}({\omega}^{\prime},{\omega})
.
Then for any
t>96\sqrt{rd\mathbb{E}[X]}+128rd
,
\mathbb{P}\left[|X-\mathbb{E}[X]|>t\right]\,\leqslant\,4\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{8rd(4\mathbb{E}[X]+t)}\right).
To apply this in our setting, we view
\psi\colon N\to\Delta
as a point in the product of
|N|
copies of the space
(\Delta,\nu)
, where
\nu
is the uniform probability measure on the set
\Delta=\{0,1,\ldots,\Delta-1\}
. Let
X\,\coloneqq\,|F|-|F^{\ast}|,
where
F^{*}(\psi)
is constructed according to (
4.2
) using
\psi
.
We claim that condition
(T)
of Theorem
4.2
holds for the random variable
X
with
r=1
and
d=\Delta
.
For each vertex
v\in F\setminus F^{\ast}(\psi)
, we arbitrarily pick a neighbor
u_{v}\in N
such that
\psi(u_{v})=0
(which exists by the definition of
F^{\ast}(\psi)
)
and define a function
c\colon N\to{\mathbb{N}}
as follows:
c(u)\,\coloneqq\,|\{v\in F\setminus F^{\ast}(\psi)\,:\,u=u_{v}\}|.
We have
\|c\|_{1}=\sum_{u\in N}c(u)=X(\psi)
and
\|c\|_{\infty}\leqslant\Delta
, since each
u\in N
has at least
c(u)
neighbors in
F\setminus F^{\ast}(\psi)
. Now take any
\psi^{\prime}\colon N\to\Delta
and note that if
v\in F^{\ast}(\psi^{\prime})\setminus F^{\ast}(\psi)
, then
\psi^{\prime}(u_{v})\neq 0
, so
X(\psi^{\prime})\,\geqslant\,X(\psi)-|F^{\ast}(\psi^{\prime})\setminus F^{\ast}(\psi)|\,\geqslant\,X(\psi)-\sum_{u\,:\,\psi^{\prime}(u)\neq 0}c(u)\,=\,X(\psi^{\prime})-\mathsf{dist}_{c}(\psi,\psi^{\prime}),
as desired.
Since
\mathbb{E}[X]\leqslant k
and
k/8>96\sqrt{\Delta k}+128\Delta
, we may now apply Theorem
4.2
with
r=1
,
d=\Delta
, and
t=k/8
to conclude that
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[|F^{\ast}|<\mathbb{E}[|F^{\ast}|]-k/8\big]\,
\displaystyle=\,\mathbb{P}[X-\mathbb{E}[X]>k/8]
\displaystyle\leqslant\,4\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}/64}{8\Delta(4k+k/8)}\right)\,=\,4\exp\left(-\frac{k}{2112\Delta}\right)\,\leqslant\,4\Delta^{-500},
where the last inequality uses the bound
k\geqslant 2^{25}\,\Delta\log\Delta
.
∎
Note that the definition of the set
F^{\ast}
depends on
\psi
but not on
\xi
. Since the functions
\psi\colon N\to\Delta
and
\xi\colon F\to\Delta
are chosen independently, conditioned on the choice of
\psi
, we can write
\mathbb{P}[\xi(v)\neq 0\text{ for all }v\in F^{\ast}\,|\,\psi]\,=\,\left(1-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)^{|F^{\ast}|}.
Therefore, thanks to Claim
4.1
, we have
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\xi(v)\neq 0\text{ for all }v\in F^{\ast}]\,
\displaystyle\leqslant\,4\Delta^{-500}\,+\,\left(1-\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)^{k/8}
\displaystyle\big(\text{since }1-x\leqslant e^{-x}\text{ for all }x\in\mathbb{R}\big)\qquad\qquad
\displaystyle\leqslant\,4\Delta^{-500}\,+\,\exp\left(-\frac{k}{8\Delta}\right)\,\leqslant\,5\Delta^{-500},
where the last inequality uses the bound
k\geqslant 2^{25}\Delta\log\Delta
. Remembering (
4.3
), we conclude that
\mathbb{P}[B_{F}]\,\leqslant\,5\Delta^{-500}.
Since this bound holds for all
F\in\mathcal{F}
, it follows that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\leqslant 5\Delta^{-500}
.
Putting everything together, we see that
\mathsf{p}(\mathscr{B})\,(\mathsf{d}(\mathscr{B})+1)^{8}\,\leqslant\,5\Delta^{-500}\cdot 2^{216}\Delta^{40}\,=\,5\cdot 2^{216}\cdot\Delta^{-460}\,\leqslant\,5\cdot 2^{-244}\,<\,2^{-15},
and thus
\mathscr{B}
has a Borel solution by Theorem
1.31
, as desired.
∎
4.4.  Edge-colorings of Schreier graphs
In this subsection, we derive Theorem
1.51
from Theorem
1.53
. The main idea of the proof of Theorem
1.51
is to decompose the line graph
L(G)
of
G
into certain induced subgraphs with a simple structure and then combine colorings of those subgraphs into a coloring of
L(G)
. We begin with a couple lemmas that facilitate this approach. The following observation is almost trivial:
Lemma 4.3.
Let
G
be a Borel graph and let
V(G)=U_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{n}
be a partition of the vertex set of
G
into Borel subsets such that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}])<\infty
for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
. Then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\,\leqslant\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}]).
Proof
.
Let
q_{i}\coloneqq\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}])
and let
f_{i}\colon U_{i}\to q_{i}
be a Borel proper
q_{i}
-coloring of
G[U_{i}]
. Define
f_{i}^{\prime}(v)\,\coloneqq\,f_{i}(v)+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}q_{j}\quad\text{for all }v\in U_{i}.
Then
f\coloneqq f_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup f_{n}^{\prime}
is a Borel proper
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}\right)
-coloring of
G
.
∎
For a graph
G
, let
\Delta(G)
denote the maximum degree of
G
. If in the setting of Lemma
4.3
each graph
G[U_{i}]
has finite maximum degree, then we can use Theorem
1.5
to write
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\,\leqslant\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}])\,\leqslant\,\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\Delta(G[U_{i}])+1)\,=\,n+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(G[U_{i}]).
The next lemma, which we deduce from Theorem
1.53
, allows us to improve this bound when
G
has finite asymptotic separation index and the graphs
G[U_{i}]
do not have small components:
Lemma 4.4.
Let
G
be a Borel graph of maximum degree at most
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\Delta\geqslant 2
, and let
V(G)=U_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{n}
be a partition of the vertex set of
G
into Borel subsets. Suppose that for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, every component of
G[U_{i}]
contains at least
2^{25}\,\Delta\log\Delta
vertices. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\,\leqslant\,1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(G[U_{i}]).
Proof
.
Let
G^{\prime}
be the spanning subgraph of
G
with
E(G^{\prime})\coloneqq E(G[U_{1}])\sqcup\ldots\sqcup E(G[U_{n}])
. Applying Theorem
1.53
with
H=G_{1}=G
and
G_{2}=G^{\prime}
yields a Borel
G
-independent complete section
S
for
G^{\prime}
. For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, let
U_{i}^{\prime}\coloneqq U_{i}\setminus S
. Clearly,
\Delta(G[U_{i}^{\prime}])\leqslant\Delta(G[U_{i}])
. Moreover, since
S
meets every connected component of
G[U_{i}]
, every component of
G[U_{i}^{\prime}]
includes a vertex that is adjacent to
S
in
G[U_{i}]
and hence has degree at most
\Delta(G[U_{i}])-1
in
G[U_{i}^{\prime}]
. Therefore, by a result of Conley, Marks, and Tucker-Drob
[
CMT16
, p. 16, proof of Theorem 1.2]
,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}^{\prime}])\leqslant\Delta(G[U_{i}])
.
To finish the argument, we apply Lemma
4.3
to the partition
V(G)=S\sqcup U_{1}^{\prime}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{n}^{\prime}
to get
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\,\leqslant\,\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[S])+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(G[U_{i}^{\prime}])\,\leqslant\,1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Delta(G[U_{i}]).\qed
For the remainder of §
4.4
, we fix a group
\Gamma
generated by a finite symmetric set
F\subseteq\Gamma\setminus\{\mathbf{1}_{\Gamma}\}
. For ease of reference, let us restate Theorem
1.51
here:
Theorem
1.51
.
Suppose that
|F|\geqslant 2
and for each
\sigma\in F
, the order of
\sigma
is either even or at least
2^{30}\,|F|\log|F|
. Let
\bm{a}\colon\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be a free Borel action of
\Gamma
on a standard Borel space
X
and let
G\coloneqq\mathsf{Sch}(\bm{a},F)
be the corresponding Schreier graph. If
\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
, then
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)\leqslant|F|+1
.
Proof
.
Recall that the line graph of
G
is the graph
L(G)
with vertex set
E(G)
and edge set
\{\{e,e^{\prime}\}\in[E(G)]^{2}\,:\,|e\cap e^{\prime}|=1\}
. Note that we have
\chi^{\prime}_{\mathsf{B}}(G)=\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L(G))
. To bound
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L(G))
, we shall apply Lemmas
4.3
and
4.4
to certain partitions of
E(G)
.
To begin with, we show that
\mathsf{asi}(L(G))\leqslant\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
. Set
s\coloneqq\mathsf{asi}(G)<\infty
. By the Luzin–Novikov theorem (Theorem
2.2
), there is a Borel function
c\colon E(G)\to X
such that
c(e)\in e
for all
e\in E(G)
. Take any
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
X=U_{0}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup U_{s}
be a partition witnessing that
\mathsf{si}(G^{R+1})\leqslant s
. Define a partition
E(G)=E_{0}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup E_{s}
by
E_{i}\coloneqq c^{-1}(U_{i})
. If
e
,
e^{\prime}\in E(G)
are joined by a path of length at most
R
in
L(G)
, then
c(e)
and
c(e^{\prime})
are joined by a path of length at most
R+1
in
G
. It follows that the graphs
L(G)^{R}[E_{i}]
are component-finite, and hence
\mathsf{si}(L(G)^{R})\leqslant s
, as desired.
Let
F_{2}
be the set of all generators
\sigma\in F
of order
2
and let
F_{>2}\subseteq F\setminus F_{2}
be a subset formed by picking one member from each pair
\{\sigma,\sigma^{-1}\}\subseteq F\setminus F_{2}
. Then we have
|F|\,=\,|F_{2}|+2|F_{>2}|.
We split the set
F_{>2}
as
F_{>2}=F_{\mathsf{even}}\sqcup F_{\mathsf{odd}}\sqcup F_{\infty}
, where
F_{\mathsf{even}}
,
F_{\mathsf{odd}}
, and
F_{\infty}
comprise the elements
\sigma\in F_{>2}
whose order is even, odd, or infinite respectively. For
\sigma\in F_{2}\cup F_{>2}
, define
E_{\sigma}\,\coloneqq\,\{\{x,\sigma\cdot_{\bm{a}}x\}\,:\,x\in X\}.
Note that the sets
(E_{\sigma}\,:\,\sigma\in F_{2}\cup F_{>2})
form a partition of
E(G)
.
Let
L_{\sigma}\coloneqq L(G)[E_{\sigma}]
. If
\sigma\in F_{2}
, then
E_{\sigma}
is an
L(G)
-independent set. For
\sigma\in F_{\mathsf{even}}\cup F_{\mathsf{odd}}
, every component of
L_{\sigma}
is a cycle of length
\mathrm{ord}(\sigma)
, and for
\sigma\in F_{\infty}
, every component of
L_{\sigma}
is an infinite path. If
\sigma\not\in F_{\infty}
, then
L_{\sigma}
is component-finite and hence, by Corollary
2.3
,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{\sigma})=\chi(L_{\sigma})
.
Let
L_{0}
and
L_{1}
be the subgraphs of
L(G)
induced by
\bigcup_{\sigma\in F_{2}\cup F_{\mathsf{even}}}E_{\sigma}
and
\bigcup_{\sigma\in F_{\mathsf{odd}}\cup F_{\infty}}E_{\sigma}
respectively. By Lemma
4.3
,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{0})\,\leqslant\,\sum_{\sigma\in F_{2}\cup F_{\mathsf{even}}}\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{\sigma})\,=\,|F_{2}|+2|F_{\mathsf{even}}|.
Note that
\Delta(L(G))<2|F|
. Since every component of
L_{\sigma}
for
\sigma\in F_{\mathsf{odd}}\cup F_{\infty}
contains at least
2^{30}\,|F|\log|F|\,\geqslant\,2^{25}\cdot 2|F|\log(2|F|)
vertices, we may apply Lemma
4.4
to conclude that
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{1})\,\leqslant\,1+\sum_{\sigma\in F_{\mathsf{odd}}\cup F_{\infty}}\Delta(L_{\sigma})\,=\,1+2|F_{\mathsf{odd}}|+2|F_{\mathsf{\infty}}|.
By Lemma
4.3
again,
\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L(G))\,\leqslant\,\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{0})\,+\,\chi_{\mathsf{B}}(L_{1})\,\leqslant\,|F_{2}|+2|F_{\mathsf{even}}|+1+2|F_{\mathsf{odd}}|+2|F_{\mathsf{\infty}}|\,=\,|F|+1,
and the proof is complete.
∎
