Abstract.
The Lovász Local Lemma (the LLL for short) is a powerful tool in probabilistic combinatorics that is used to verify the existence of combinatorial objects with desirable properties. Recent years saw the development of various “constructive” versions of the LLL. A major success of this research direction is the Borel version of the LLL due to Csóka, Grabowski, Máthé, Pikhurko, and Tyros, which holds under a subexponential growth assumption.
A drawback of their approach is that it only applies when the underlying random variables take values in a finite set. We present an alternative proof of a Borel version of the LLL that holds even if the underlying random variables are continuous and applies to dependency graphs of limited exponential growth.

[

. Recent years saw an explosion of “constructive” versions of the LLL, for various notions of “constructiveness”

[

.
There exist several essentially equivalent frameworks for stating the LLL in the Borel context
[
6
,
8
,
17
]
. We shall find the following formalism convenient:
Definition 1.1
(Constraint satisfaction problems)
.
A
constraint satisfaction problem
(a
CSP
for short) is a tuple
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
, where:
—
V
,
\Lambda
, and
\mathfrak{C}
are sets whose elements are called
variables
,
labels
, and
constraints
respectively,
—
\mathsf{dom}
assigns to each constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
a finite set
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\subseteq V
called the
domain
of
\mathfrak{c}
,
—
\mathcal{B}
assigns to each constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
a set
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})\subseteq\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}
of
bad labelings
\varphi\colon\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\to\Lambda
.
A function
f\colon V\to\Lambda
is called a
labeling
of
V
. Such a labeling
f
violates
a constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
if its restriction to
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
is in
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
; otherwise,
f
satisfies
\mathfrak{c}
. A
solution
to
\Pi
is a labeling
f\colon V\to\Lambda
that satisfies every constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
.
Let us illustrate this definition with a couple of simple examples showing how natural combinatorial problems can be represented by CSPs.
Example 1.2
(Proper coloring)
.
Given a graph
G
and a natural number
1
1
1
In this paper,
0
is a natural number, and a natural number
k
is identified with the
k
-element set
\{i\in{\mathbb{N}}\,:\,i<k\}
.
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, a
proper
k
-coloring
of
G
is a function
f\colon V(G)\to k
such that
f(u)\neq f(v)
whenever
\{u,v\}\in E(G)
. It is clear that a proper
k
-coloring of
G
is the same as a solution to the CSP
\Pi_{k}(G)\,\coloneqq\,\big(V(G),\,k,\,E(G),\,\mathsf{id},\,\mathcal{B}\big),
where for each edge
e\in E(G)
,
\mathsf{id}(e)\coloneqq e
(i.e., the domain of the corresponding constraint is the set containing the two endpoints of
e
), while
\mathcal{B}(e)
is the set of all constant mappings
\varphi\colon e\to k
.
Example 1.3
(Sinkless orientation)
.
Let
G
be a locally finite graph. An orientation of
G
is
sinkless
if it has no
sinks
, i.e., no vertices with outdegree
0
. The problem of finding a sinkless orientation of
G
can be encoded by a CSP
\Pi_{\bullet\to}(G)
as follows. Fix an arbitrary orientation
\vec{G}_{0}
of
G
. We can then identify any orientation
\vec{G}
of
G
with a labeling
f\colon E(G)\to\{{+},{-}\}
, where
f(e)={+}
if and only if
e
is oriented the same in
\vec{G}
and
\vec{G}_{0}
. Now we let
\Pi_{\bullet\to}(G)\,\coloneqq\,\big(E(G),\,\{{+},{-}\},\,V(G),\,\partial_{G},\,\mathcal{B}\big),
where for each vertex
v\in V(G)
,
\partial_{G}(v)
is the set of all edges of
G
incident to
v
and
\mathcal{B}(v)
contains the unique mapping
\varphi\colon\partial_{G}(v)\to\{{+},{-}\}
that makes
v
a sink.
The list of examples can be continued almost indefinitely and includes such classical problems as perfect matching, maximal independent set, edge coloring, hypergraph coloring, graph homomorphism, and so on. We remark that in most combinatorial applications, the set of labels
\Lambda
is finite; however, that is not a requirement in Definition
1.1
.
In view of the versatility of Definition
1.1
, it is desirable to develop flexible general conditions that guarantee the existence of a solution to a given CSP
\Pi
. The LLL gives just such a condition. Roughly, the LLL says that
\Pi
has a solution provided that:
(
a
)
the constraints interact with each other “in a limited way,” and
(
b
)
each individual constraint is “unlikely” to be violated.
The interaction between the constraints is captured by the
dependency graph
of
\Pi
:
Definition 1.4
(Dependency graph)
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a CSP. The
dependency graph
of
\Pi
, denoted by
D_{\Pi}
, is the graph with vertex set
\mathfrak{C}
and edge set
\{\{\mathfrak{c},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\}\,:\,\mathfrak{c}\neq\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},\,\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\cap\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})\neq\varnothing\}.
Throughout, we shall assume the dependency graph
D_{\Pi}
has finite maximum degree. Intuitively, the smaller the maximum degree of
D_{\Pi}
is, the less the constraints interact with each other.
To measure how likely each constraint is to be violated, we equip the label set
\Lambda
with a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
. To unclutter the notation, we shall, when there is no possibility of confusion, use the symbol “
\mathbb{P}
” not only for the given measure on
\Lambda
but also for product measures of the form
\mathbb{P}^{S}
on
\Lambda^{S}
for some set
S
. Now, for a constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, we can randomly generate a labeling
\varphi\colon\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\to\Lambda
(i.e., a point in
\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}
) by drawing the labels
\varphi(v)
for each
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
independently from
(\Lambda,\mathbb{P})
. The likelihood of violating
\mathfrak{c}
is simply the probability the resulting labeling is bad, i.e.,
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]
.
With these preliminaries, we are ready to state the Lovász Local Lemma:
Theorem 1.5
(Lovász Local Lemma
[
18
,
43
,
1
]
)
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a CSP and let
\mathbb{P}
be a probability measure on
\Lambda
making
(\Lambda,\mathbb{P})
a standard probability space. If there exist
p\in[0,1)
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that:
—
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
is
\mathbb{P}
-measurable and
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
—
the maximum degree of the dependency graph
D_{\Pi}
is at most
d
, and
—
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<1
(
where
\mathsf{e}=2.71\ldots
is the base of the natural logarithm
)
,
then
\Pi
has a solution
f\colon V\to\Lambda
.
Remarks 1.6
.
(i)
The above statement of the LLL is somewhat restricted compared to the more standard version typically found in the combinatorics literature, in that we compute the probability
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]
with respect to specifically the
product
measure on
\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}
. This set-up is sometimes called the
variable version
of the LLL. Even though this setting is not the most general, it does encompass virtually all standard applications
[
36
, 41]
and is often viewed as the “right one” for algorithmic considerations (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
37
,
19
]
). That being said, it is possible to formulate the LLL without assuming a product structure on the underlying probability space; see
[
1
, §5.1]
for the statement of the LLL in abstract probability spaces and
[
33
,
14
,
27
]
for comparisons between the abstract and variable versions of the LLL.
(ii)
Although the LLL is usually stated and proved in the case when the set of constraints
\mathfrak{C}
is finite, Theorem
1.5
remains valid even if
\mathfrak{C}
is infinite. Indeed, we may without loss of generality assume that
\Lambda
is a compact metric space and
\mathbb{P}
is a Borel probability measure
[
32
, Thm. 17.41]
. Pick any
p^{\prime}>p
such that
\mathsf{e}\,p^{\prime}\,(d+1)<1
and replace each
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
by an
open
set
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{c})\supseteq\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
of measure at most
p^{\prime}
, which is possible since
\mathbb{P}
is regular
[
32
, Thm. 17.10]
. The existence of a solution to
\Pi
now follows by applying the LLL to finite subsets of
\mathfrak{C}
and using the compactness of the space
\Lambda^{V}
; see, e.g.,
[
1
, proof of Thm. 5.2.2]
for details.
In this paper we are interested in the following general question:
Question 1.7
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a CSP such that
V
and
\Lambda
are standard Borel spaces. When can the LLL be invoked to conclude that there exists a
Borel
solution
f\colon V\to\Lambda
to
\Pi
?
Recall that a
standard Borel space
is a set
X
equipped with a
\sigma
-algebra
\mathfrak{B}(X)
generated by a complete separable metric on
X
. The members of
\mathfrak{B}(X)
are called the
Borel subsets
of
X
. By the Borel Isomorphism Theorem
[
32
, Thm. 15.6]
, all uncountable standard Borel spaces are isomorphic to each other, so, as a rule, no generality is lost by replacing
X
with some familiar space, such as the real line
\mathbb{R}
. A function
f\colon X\to Y
between standard Borel spaces is
Borel
if
f
-preimages of Borel subsets of
Y
are Borel in
X
, or, equivalently, if the graph of
f
is a Borel subset of
X\times Y
[
32
, Thm. 14.12]
. Borel sets and functions have a variety of useful regularity properties and are generally taken to represent the “gold standard” of well-behavedness in descriptive set theory.
Unfortunately, as the next example illustrates, even under very favorable circumstances, the solutions produced by the LLL may fail to be Borel.
Example 1.8
(Borel sinkless orientation—a negative result)
.
Fix
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
and let
G
be a
d
-regular graph (meaning that every vertex of
G
has exactly
d
neighbors). Recall the sinkless orientation problem
\Pi_{\bullet\to}(G)
from Example
1.3
. It is easy to see that the dependency graph of
\Pi_{\bullet\to}(G)
is
G
itself, hence its maximum degree is
d
. Letting
\mathbb{P}
be the uniform probability distribution on
\{+,-\}
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(v)]=2^{-d}\eqqcolon p
for each
v\in V(G)
, because exactly one out of the
2^{d}
ways to orient the edges incident to
v
is bad. The inequality
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)\,=\,\mathsf{e}\,2^{-d}\,(d+1)\,<\,1
is satisfied for all
d\geqslant 4
; moreover, its left-hand side rapidly approaches
0
as
d
goes to infinity. Nevertheless, no matter how large
d
is,
G
may fail to have a Borel sinkless orientation, since Thornton
[
44
, Thm. 3.5]
, using the determinacy method of Marks
[
35
]
, constructed for each
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
a Borel
d
-regular graph
G
with no Borel sinkless orientation.
While the above example shows the LLL may fail to produce a Borel solution in general, there has been a recent string of positive “LLL-like” results in descriptive set theory
[
6
,
8
,
17
,
4
,
10
]
. Most of these results yield weaker conclusions (e.g., measurable in place of Borel) or require stronger bounds than
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<1
(often both). A notable exception is the Borel version of the LLL due to Csóka, Grabowski, Máthé, Pikhurko, and Tyros
[
17
]
. Their result yields a Borel solution to a given CSP
\Pi
under the usual LLL condition
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<1
; to achieve this, they add some extra assumptions on
\Pi
, the main one being that the dependency graph
D_{\Pi}
ought to be
of subexponential growth
.
Definition 1.9
(Growth of graphs)
.
The
growth function
\gamma_{G}\colon{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
of a locally finite graph
G
is given by
\gamma_{G}(R)\coloneqq\sup_{v\in V(G)}|B_{G}(v,R)|
, where
B_{G}(v,R)
is the
R
-ball around
v
in
G
, i.e., the set of all vertices joined to
v
by a path of at most
R
edges.
The quantity
\mathsf{egr}(G)\,\coloneqq\,\lim_{R\to\infty}\sqrt[R]{\gamma_{G}(R)}\,=\,\inf_{R\geqslant 1}\sqrt[R]{\gamma_{G}(R)}
is called the
exponential growth rate
of
G
.
(The limit exists and is equal to the infimum by Fekete’s lemma
[
42
, Lem. A.4.2]
.) We say that
G
is
of subexponential growth
if
\mathsf{egr}(G)=1
.
The theorem of Csóka
et al.
and all the results of this paper apply to
Borel CSPs
, i.e., CSPs of the form
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
in which
V
,
\Lambda
, and
\mathfrak{C}
are standard Borel spaces and the assignments
\mathsf{dom}
and
\mathcal{B}
are Borel in a certain natural sense; see §
3.1
for the formal definition. Csóka
et al.
showed that a Borel CSP
\Pi
that fulfills the assumptions of the LLL has a Borel solution, provided that its dependency graph is of subexponential growth and, furthermore, the label set
\Lambda
and
the
order
of
\Pi
, defined below, are finite.
Definition 1.10
(Order and maximum variable degree)
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a CSP. For
v\in V
, let
\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}\,:\,v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\}
. The
order
and the
maximum variable degree
of
\Pi
are
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}}|\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})|\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathsf{vdeg}(\Pi)\,\coloneqq\,\sup_{v\in V}|\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)|.
Theorem 1.11
(Csóka–Grabowski–Máthé–Pikhurko–Tyros
[
17
]
)
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a Borel CSP and let
\mathbb{P}
be a Borel probability measure on
\Lambda
. If there are
p\in[0,1)
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that:
(i)
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the maximum degree of
D_{\Pi}
is at most
d
, and
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<1
,
(ii)
D_{\Pi}
is of subexponential growth, and
(iii)
\Lambda
is a finite set and
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)<\infty
,
then
\Pi
has a Borel solution
f\colon V\to\Lambda
.
In Theorem
1.11
,
(i)
is just the standard LLL assumption (as in Theorem
1.5
). Example
1.3
shows that some extra conditions are necessary to guarantee the existence of a Borel solution, and the subexponential growth of
D_{\Pi}
is a natural requirement to add. Indeed, Theorem
1.11
belongs to a growing body of research showing that various combinatorial problems can be solved in a Borel way on graphs of subexponential growth
[
16
,
44
,
3
]
. For example, Thornton proved that if
d\geqslant 3
, then every
d
-regular Borel graph of subexponential growth has a Borel sinkless orientation
[
44
, Thm. 1.5]
. For
d\geqslant 4
, this fact is a consequence of Theorem
1.11
. Other applications of Theorem
1.11
can be found in
[
5
,
9
]
.
On the other hand, condition
(iii)
in Theorem
1.11
may seem somewhat puzzling. While it is usually satisfied in combinatorial applications, it is unclear whether its presence is necessary, given that the classical LLL (i.e., Theorem
1.5
) is valid even if
(\Lambda,\mathbb{P})
is a
continuous
probability space. As we explain below, the proof of Theorem
1.11
in
[
17
]
uses the finiteness of both
\Lambda
and
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)
in an essential and apparently unavoidable way. Nevertheless, in this paper we present an alternative proof strategy that completely eliminates assumption
(iii)
:
Theorem 1.12.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a Borel CSP and let
\mathbb{P}
be a Borel probability measure on
\Lambda
. Suppose there exist
p\in[0,1)
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that:
(i)
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the maximum degree of
D_{\Pi}
is at most
d
, and
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<1
,
(ii)
D_{\Pi}
is of subexponential growth.
Then
\Pi
has a Borel solution
f\colon V\to\Lambda
.
Theorem
1.11
is a corollary of the following more general statement established in
[
17
]
, which explicitly invokes the finite values of
|\Lambda|
and
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)
:
Theorem 1.13
(
[
17
, Thm. 4.5]
)
.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a Borel CSP such that
\Lambda
is a nonempty finite set, and let
\mathbb{P}
be the uniform probability distribution on
\Lambda
. Suppose there exist parameters
p\in[0,1)
,
d
,
\Delta\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\varepsilon>0
such that:
(i)
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and the maximum degree of
D_{\Pi}
is at most
d
,
(ii)
both
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)
and
\mathsf{vdeg}(\Pi)
are at most
\Delta
,
(iii)
\mathsf{egr}(D_{\Pi})<(1+\varepsilon)^{2/3}
, and
(iv)
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)\,|\Lambda|^{\varepsilon\Delta}<1
.
Then
\Pi
has a Borel solution
f\colon V\to\Lambda
.
Remarks 1.14
.
(i)
Since our notation is somewhat different from that in
[
17
]
, let us briefly comment on how Theorem
1.13
follows from
[
17
, Thm. 4.5]
. Let
G
be the bipartite graph with a bipartition
(V,\mathfrak{C})
such that
v\in V
is adjacent to
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
if and only if
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
(this is the construction in
[
17
, Rmk. 1.3]
). Condition
(ii)
of Theorem
1.13
then says that the maximum degree of
G
is at most
\Delta
. It is not hard to see that if
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)<\infty
, then
\mathsf{egr}(D_{\Pi})=\mathsf{egr}(G)^{2}
, so
(iii)
is equivalent to
\mathsf{egr}(G)<(1+\varepsilon)^{1/3}
. This implies that for large enough
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\gamma_{G}(3R)<(1+\varepsilon)^{R}
. In the notation of
[
17
]
, this means that
G\in\mathrm{SubExp}(R,\varepsilon,\Delta)
, and hence
[
17
, Thm. 4.5]
may be applied to obtain a Borel solution to
\Pi
.
(ii)
Let us also sketch how Theorem
1.13
yields Theorem
1.11
. Suppose we are in the setting of Theorem
1.11
. To begin with, we may replace
\Pi
by an equivalent problem (possibly with a larger set of labels) to arrange that
\mathbb{P}
is the uniform probability measure on
\Lambda
[
5
, Rmk. 2.4]
. Next we note that
\Delta\coloneqq\max\big\{\mathsf{vdeg}(\Pi),\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)\big\}
must be finite, because
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)<\infty
by assumption and
\mathsf{vdeg}(\Pi)\leqslant d+1
. Hence, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
so small that
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)<|\Lambda|^{-\varepsilon\Delta}
. Since
D_{\Pi}
is of subexponential growth,
\mathsf{egr}(D_{\Pi})=1<(1+\varepsilon)^{2/3}
, so
\Pi
has a Borel solution by Theorem
1.13
.
Note that Theorem
1.13
holds even if the dependency graph
D_{\Pi}
is
of exponential growth
, as long as its exponential growth rate is sufficiently small. Unfortunately, how small the growth rate has to be significantly depends on
|\Lambda|
and
\Delta
; that is why both
\Lambda
and
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)
must be finite for this result to apply. In particular, notice that Theorem
1.13
can only be used when
\mathsf{egr}(D_{\Pi})<2^{2/3}\approx 1.59
. Indeed, in the setting of Theorem
1.13
, every constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
must satisfy either
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})=\varnothing
or
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{|\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})|}}\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)}}\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{\Delta}}.
Therefore, except for the trivial case
p=0
, we have
p\geqslant|\Lambda|^{-\Delta}
. Hence, the bound
\mathsf{e}\,p\,(d+1)\,|\Lambda|^{\varepsilon\Delta}<1
can only be satisfied if
\varepsilon<1
, and thus Theorem
1.13
requires
\mathsf{egr}(D_{\Pi})<2^{2/3}
.
In our main result we remedy the above shortcomings of Theorem
1.13
; that is, we generalize it to arbitrary
\Lambda
, remove the bound on
\mathsf{ord}(\Pi)
, and make it applicable to dependency graphs whose exponential growth rate may be arbitrarily large (although it must still be bounded in terms of the relationship between
p
and
d
):
Theorem 1.15.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})

. When

Example 1.16
(Borel sinkless orientation—a positive result)
.
Consider again the sinkless orientation problem
\Pi_{\bullet\to}(G)
for a

from Examples

has a Borel orientation in which every vertex has outdegree at least

[

. Theorem

yields an extension of this result to graphs whose exponential growth rate is near-linear in

In addition to establishing a more general result, our proof of Theorem

is also somewhat simpler than the proof of Theorem
1.13
given in
[
17
]
, although the two arguments have several common ingredients. The starting point of both approaches is the so-called
Moser–Tardos Algorithm
: a randomized procedure for solving CSPs developed and analyzed by Moser and Tardos in their landmark paper
[
37
]
. We give an overview of the Moser–Tardos Algorithm in §

. The difficulty in the Borel setting, roughly, is that the Moser–Tardos Algorithm needs a large set of mutually independent random inputs, which cannot be generated in a Borel way. The authors of
[
17
]
deal with this challenge via

: namely, they reuse the same random input multiple times. With a careful analysis of the Moser–Tardos Algorithm, they are able to show that it still succeeds when randomness is conserved in this way. This step in the proof involves combinatorial counting arguments that are ultimately responsible for the dependence on
|\Lambda|
and
\Delta
in Theorem

. Our strategy is to use

instead: thanks to the bound on the exponential growth rate of

, we are able to reduce the given CSP

to a different CSP

whose corresponding value of

is

smaller. This reduction is explained in §

, and the desired properties of

are established in §§

,
2.4
, and

. We then find a Borel solution to

—and hence to

Lemma 1.17.
Let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a Borel CSP and let

be a Borel probability measure on

. Suppose there exist parameters

Lemma

is proved via the

, a standard derandomization technique in computer science
[

38

. This method has already been applied to obtain “constructive” versions of the LLL in various contexts

,
19
,
4
,

. Indeed, the proof of Lemma

is almost exactly the same as that of

are stated for finite

.
2.  The Moser–Tardos Algorithm and its local analysis
In this section we present the combinatorial heart of our argument. Throughout §
2
, we fix a CSP
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
and a probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Lambda
making
(\Lambda,\mathbb{P})
a standard probability space. We also let
D\coloneqq D_{\Pi}
be the dependency graph of
\Pi
and assume that
D
is locally finite. For each
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, we write
N[\mathfrak{c}]\coloneqq B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},1)
and
N(\mathfrak{c})\coloneqq N[\mathfrak{c}]\setminus\{\mathfrak{c}\}
for the
closed
, resp.
open
neighborhood
of
\mathfrak{c}
in the graph
D
. Note that since

is locally finite by assumption,

and

are finite sets.
2.1.  The Moser–Tardos Algorithm
As mentioned in the introduction, an important tool in the study of “constructive” aspects of the LLL is the
Moser–Tardos Algorithm
, or
\mathsf{MTA}
for short (see Algorithm
1
).
It was introduced by Moser and Tardos in
[
37
]
in order to prove an algorithmic version of the LLL.
\mathsf{MTA}
takes as input a CSP
\Pi
and a map
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
, called a
table
(we picture it as a matrix with rows indexed by
{\mathbb{N}}
, columns indexed by
V
, and entries from
\Lambda
). For convenience, given
v\in V
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we write
\tau(v,n)
for the
n
-th entry of the sequence
\tau(v)\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
. The algorithm attempts to solve
\Pi
by building a sequence of labelings
f_{0}
,
f_{1}
, …
\colon V\to\Lambda
as follows. We start by setting
f_{0}(v)\coloneqq\tau(v,0)
for all
v\in V
. At the
n
-th iteration, we let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
be the set of all constraints violated by
f_{n}
. We then pick some
D
-independent
2
2
2
Recall that a set of vertices in a graph is
independent
if no two of its members are adjacent.
subset
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{n}
and, for each constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}_{n}
and every variable
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
, update
f_{n}(v)
to be the next value in the sequence
\tau(v)
(we use a function
\ell_{n}\colon V\to{\mathbb{N}}
to keep track of the current position in the sequence). This produces the labeling
f_{n+1}
. Taking a natural limit yields a (possibly partial) labeling
f
. The details are presented in Algorithm
1
.
Algorithm 1
Moser–Tardos Algorithm (
\mathsf{MTA}
)
Input:
A CSP
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
and a table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
Initialize
\ell_{0}(v)\coloneqq 0
for all
v\in V
.
for

,
1
,
2
, …
do
Define

for all
v\in V
.
Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n}\coloneqq\{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}\,:\,\text{$f_{n}$ violates $\mathfrak{c}$}\}
and pick a
D
-independent subset
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{n}
.
for
v\in V
do
if

for some

\ell_{n+1}(v)\coloneqq\ell_{n}(v)+1

\ell_{n+1}(v)\coloneqq\ell_{n}(v)

Let
\displaystyle\ell(v)\coloneqq\lim_{n\to\infty}\ell_{n}(v)\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
and define

whenever

.
Note that Algorithm

does not specify how the

Let

\ell_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)\,\coloneqq\,\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\,\in\,\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)}|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)\,\coloneqq\,\tau(v,\ell_{n}(v)).

.
If we run
\mathsf{MTA}
on input

with

on step

violates every constraint in
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
. ∎
The
Maximal Moser–Tardos Algorithm
, or
\mathsf{MMTA}
for short, is a variant of
\mathsf{MTA}
with the additional requirement that each set
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
be an
(
inclusion-
)

-independent subset of

(this is
[
37

\mathsf{MTA}(\Pi,\tau)
(resp.
\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)

that can be generated by
\mathsf{MTA}
(resp.
\mathsf{MMTA}
) on input

. By definition,
\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)\subseteq\mathsf{MTA}(\Pi,\tau)
.
Lemma 2.3.
Suppose
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]<1
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and let
\tau
be a table. If a labeling
f\in\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)
is defined on all of

, then
f
is a solution to
\Pi
.
Proof
.
Note that if the domain of a constraint
\mathfrak{c}
is empty, then there are two options for
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
, namely
\varnothing
and
\{\varnothing\}
, and we have
\mathbb{P}[\varnothing]=0
and
\mathbb{P}[\{\varnothing\}]=1
. Since
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]<1
by assumption, we conclude that
\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})=\varnothing
. In other words, such a constraint
\mathfrak{c}
is satisfied by every labeling.
Now suppose
f\in\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)
is defined everywhere and violates some constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
. It follows that
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\neq\varnothing
. Let
\ell_{n}
and
\ell
be as in Algorithm
1
, and let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
be such that
\ell(v)=\ell_{n}(v)
for all
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
, which exists because
\ell
is finite everywhere. Then
f
agrees with
f_{n}
on
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
, so
f_{n}
also violates
\mathfrak{c}
. This implies that
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, and, by the maximality of
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
and since
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\neq\varnothing
, there must be some
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{I}_{n}
with
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in N[\mathfrak{c}]
. But then for any
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\cap\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})
, we have
\ell_{n+1}(v)=\ell_{n}(v)+1
, contradicting the choice of
n
.
∎
2.2.  Good and locally good tables
It follows from Lemma
2.3
that to solve
\Pi
, it is enough to find a table
\tau
such that some labeling
f\in\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)
is defined everywhere. We shall seek a table with the following stronger property:
Definition 2.4
(Good tables)
.
A table
\tau
is
good
if every
f\in\mathsf{MTA}(\Pi,\tau)
is defined on all of
V
.
The following is the main result of
[
37
]
:
Theorem 2.5
(Moser–Tardos
[
37
, Thm. 1.2]
)
.
Let
p
and
d
be as in Theorem
1.5
(
i.e., the LLL
)
.
Sample a random table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
from the product space
(\Lambda^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}},\mathbb{P}^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}})
. Then for each
v\in V
,
\mathbb{P}[\text{every $f\in\mathsf{MTA}(\Pi,\tau)$ is defined on $v$}]\,=\,1.
It follows that if
V
is finite (or countable), then a random table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is almost surely good; in particular, a good table exists. As explained in Remark
1.6
(ii)
, Theorem
1.5
for infinite
V
then follows via a compactness argument. Moreover, although the proof of Theorem
2.5
given in
[
37
]
assumes
V
is finite, it works just as well in the infinite case; see
[
6
, §3]
for a detailed presentation of the argument for arbitrary
V
. (This was also observed in
[
41
,
34
]
.)
Unfortunately, even though Theorem
2.5
holds for infinite
V
, it cannot be applied directly to find a Borel solution to
\Pi
, for two reasons. First, if
V
is uncountable, we can no longer conclude that a random table is good with probability
1
(or even with positive probability). Second—and more importantly—a random table would typically not be a Borel function. As mentioned in the introduction, Csóka
et al.
[
17
]
handled this issue by using the same random values for multiple variables. Namely, they partitioned
V
into finitely many “sparse” Borel subsets as
V=V_{1}\cup\ldots\cup V_{k}
and generated a table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
by independently picking
k
random sequences
\tau_{1}
, …,
\tau_{k}\in\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
and setting
\tau(v)\coloneqq\tau_{i}
for all
v\in V_{i}
. They then were able to establish a suitable analog of Theorem
2.5
for this construction under the assumptions of Theorem
1.13
.
Our approach instead is to treat the problem of finding a good table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
as
a CSP in its own right
. The idea is to “localize” the notion of goodness by “zooming in” on
r
-balls around individual constraints in the graph
D
. To this end, given
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we define the
(\mathfrak{c},r)
-local CSP
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}\coloneqq(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{c},r})
by letting, for all
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
\mathcal{B}_{\mathfrak{c},r}(\mathfrak{a})\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r),\\
\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{a})}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
That is, from the point of view of
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
, the constraints at distance more than
r
from
\mathfrak{c}
in
D
are always violated. In particular, to check whether an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
, we only need to consider the constraints in
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
:
Lemma 2.6.
Let
\tau
be a table and let
\mathfrak{I}
be an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence. For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
v\in V
, define
\ell_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)
and
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)
as in (
2.2
). Then, for any
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
if and only if for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\cdot)
violates every constraint in
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
.
Proof
.
Follows immediately by applying Lemma
2.1
with
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
in place of
\Pi
.
∎
It is clear that, unless
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)=\mathfrak{C}
, the CSP
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
has no solutions, because the constraints in
\mathfrak{C}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
are, by definition, always violated. In particular, the Moser–Tardos Algorithm must fail to generate a solution to
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
. However, we may ask whether the constraints in
\mathfrak{C}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
are in some sense the “main reason” for this failure. Namely, if
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
, must it be that “many” constraints in the sets
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
come from outside
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
?
In order to quantify the word “many” in the preceding sentence, we restrict our attention to
finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequences, i.e.,
\mathsf{MT}
-sequences
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{n}|<\infty
. In other words,
\mathfrak{I}
is finite if each
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
is a finite set and
\mathfrak{I}_{n}=\varnothing
for all large enough
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Now, if
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
, we can consider the sets
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathfrak{I}_{n}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and we want the former ones to not be much larger than the latter. Formally, we say a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner
for
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
r
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
if
\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)|\,\geqslant\,N\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{n}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)|\,<\,\varepsilon\sum_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{n}|.
Definition 2.7
(Locally good tables)
.
Fix
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. A table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good
if for all
0\leqslant r<R
, there is no
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
. A table
\tau
is
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good
if it is
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
.
The following lemma, proved in §
2.4
, is the only place where we use a bound on
\mathsf{egr}(D)
:
Lemma 2.8.
Let

\tau_{1}
agree on
\mathsf{dom}_{r}(\mathfrak{c})
, Lemma
2.6
shows that
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau_{1})
as well. Hence,
\tau_{1}
is also not
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good, as claimed.
∎
It follows from Lemma
2.9
that a table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is a solution to
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
if and only if
\tau
is
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good, as intended.
Next we proceed to bound the maximum degree of the dependency graph of
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
:
Lemma 2.10.
For any
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon>0
, the maximum degree of the dependency graph of
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
is at most
\gamma_{D}(2R+1)-1
.
Proof
.
Distinct constraints
\mathfrak{c}
,
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{C}
are adjacent in the dependency graph of
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
if and only if
\mathsf{dom}_{R}(\mathfrak{c})\cap\mathsf{dom}_{R}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
, which means that for some
\mathfrak{a}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)
and
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime},R)
, we have
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{a})\cap\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{a}^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
, i.e.,
\mathfrak{a}^{\prime}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{a},1)
. The latter condition implies that
\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},2R+1)\setminus\{\mathfrak{c}\}
. Since
|B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},2R+1)|\leqslant\gamma_{D}(2R+1)
, the result follows.
∎
The only consequence of Lemma
2.10
we need is that the maximum degree of the dependency graph of
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
can be bounded by a function of
R
independent of
N
. By contrast, we will show that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{R,N,\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{c})]
is bounded above by a quantity that
goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
(in fact, it goes to
0
exponentially quickly, but that is not important for our purposes):
Lemma 2.11.
Suppose there exist
p\in[0,1)
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
s>1
, and
\varepsilon
,
\eta\in(0,1)
such that:
(i)
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the maximum degree of
D
is at most
d
, and
p\,(\mathsf{e}\,(d+1))^{s}<1
,
(ii)
\displaystyle\varepsilon+\frac{1}{s}<1
and
\displaystyle p^{1-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{s}}\leqslant\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}
.
Then for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{R,N,\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant(1+\eta)^{-N}\,F(d,\eta,R)
, where the quantity
F(d,\eta,R)
is independent of
N
.
We prove Lemma
2.11
in §
2.5
.
Using Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
, for any fixed
R
, we are able to pick
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
so large that
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
1.17
. As a result, we can find a Borel
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good table
\tau
—which must be good by Lemma
2.8
—and use it to finish the proof of Theorem
1.15
. The details of this part of the argument, as well as the proof of Lemma
1.17
, are presented in §
3
.
2.3.  Preliminary facts about tables and
\mathsf{MT}
-sequences
The following observation will be useful:
Lemma 2.12.
Fix
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
for some table
\tau
. Then, for any
R>r
,
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
.
Proof
.
Recall for all
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the notation
L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{a})\,\coloneqq\,\{i<n\,:\,\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{I}_{i}\}\qquad\text{and}\qquad L_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},\mathfrak{a})\,\coloneqq\,\{i<n\,:\,\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{\prime}\}.
For each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
v\in V
, let
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)
and
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},v)
be defined according to (
2.2
). By Lemma
2.6
, we need to show that for all
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)=\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
, the function
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},\cdot)
violates
\mathfrak{a}
. To this end, note that if
v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{a})
, then every constraint
\mathfrak{b}\in\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)
belongs to
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r+1)\subseteq B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)
, and hence
L_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},\mathfrak{b})=L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{b})
. It follows that
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},v)=f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},v)
. Since
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
,
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\cdot)
violates
\mathfrak{a}
by Lemma
2.6
, which implies that
f_{n}(\mathfrak{I}^{\prime},\cdot)
violates
\mathfrak{a}
as well, as desired.
∎
A consequence of Lemma
2.12
is that the property of being
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good for a table
\tau
is “localized” in the graph
D
, in the sense that it only involves the constraints in
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)
. To make this statement precise, we say that an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
(\mathfrak{c},R)
-bounded
for some
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
if
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\subseteq B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Lemma 2.13.
Fix
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. A table
\tau
is
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good if and only if for all
r<R
, no
(\mathfrak{c},R)
-bounded
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
.
Proof
.
If
\tau
is
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good, then such an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}
does not exist by definition. Now suppose
\tau
is not
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good and let
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
. Then the
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}^{\prime}\coloneqq(\mathfrak{I}_{n}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is also consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
by Lemma
2.12
, and it is
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner and
(\mathfrak{c},R)
-bounded, as desired.
∎
2.4.  Locally good implies good: Proof of Lemma
2.8
Let
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be such that
\gamma_{D}(R)<(1-\varepsilon)^{-R}
and let
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
be an
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good table for some
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Our goal is to show that
\tau
is good.
Assume, toward a contradiction, that there is a labeling
f\in\mathsf{MTA}(\Pi,\tau)
whose domain is not all of
V
and let
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence consistent with
(\Pi,\tau)
used to generate
f
. Recall for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
the notation
L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})\,\coloneqq\,\{i<n\,:\,\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}_{i}\}
. If
\ell_{n}
and
\ell
are as in Algorithm
1
, then
\ell_{n}(v)\,=\,\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\,\in\,\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)}|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|.
Since
\ell(v)=\infty
for some
v\in V
and
|\mathsf{dom}^{-1}(v)|\leqslant d+1
for all
v\in V
, there must be
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
such that
|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|>N
.
We fix any such
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and choose
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
to maximize
|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|
.
For each integer
-1\leqslant r<R
, we define a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}^{r}=(\mathfrak{I}^{r}_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
via
\mathfrak{I}^{r}_{i}\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\mathfrak{I}_{i}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r+1)&\text{if }i<n,\\
\varnothing&\text{if }i\geqslant n.\end{cases}
Consider any
0\leqslant r<R
. Since
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi,\tau)
and hence also with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
, Lemma
2.12
shows that
\mathfrak{I}^{r}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
. Furthermore, note that
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)|\,=\,\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r-1}|\,\geqslant\,\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}^{-1}_{i}|\,=\,|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|\,>\,N.
Since
\tau
is
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good,
\mathfrak{I}^{r}
cannot be
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner, and hence
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)|\,\geqslant\,\varepsilon\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r}|.
This implies that, for
0\leqslant r<R
,
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r}|\,\geqslant\,\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\,\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r}\cap B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)|\,=\,\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\,\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{r-1}|.
Applying this inequality iteratively, we obtain
(2.14)
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{R-1}|\,\geqslant\,\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{R}\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}^{-1}_{i}|\,=\,\left(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\right)^{R}|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|.
On the other hand,
(2.15)
\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}|\mathfrak{I}_{i}^{R-1}|\,=\,\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)}|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{a})|\,\leqslant\,|B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)|\,|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|\,\leqslant\,\gamma_{D}(R)\,|L_{n}(\mathfrak{I},\mathfrak{c})|
by the choice of
\mathfrak{c}
. Combining (
2.14
) and (
2.15
), we get
\gamma_{D}(R)\geqslant(1-\varepsilon)^{-R}
, which is a contradiction. The proof of Lemma
2.8
is complete.
2.5.  Witness digraphs and the proof of Lemma
2.11
Our proof of Lemma
2.11
relies, in a black box manner, on certain basic results originating in the work of Moser and Tardos
[
37
]
. The central idea of Moser–Tardos theory is to analyze
\mathsf{MTA}
by means of certain
witness structures
that keep track of
\mathsf{MTA}
’s execution process. One can find several types of such structures in the literature, e.g., the
witness trees
from the original paper
[
37
]
,
stable set sequences
[
33
]
,
neat piles
[
6
]
, and
witness digraphs
[
23
]
. The latter will be especially convenient for our purposes, although it must be stressed that all these different structures are essentially equivalent and can be straightforwardly converted into each other.
The definition of a witness digraph appeared implicitly in the paper
[
33
]
by Kolipaka and Szegedy and was formalized in
[
23
]
by Haeupler and Harris. See
[
25
,
24
,
26
,
28
]
for a selection of other works that rely on this notion.
In the sequel, a
digraph
means a simple directed graph. A
decorated digraph
is a digraph
\Gamma
equipped with a mapping
\updelta\colon V(\Gamma)\to\mathfrak{C}
, called a
decoration
. We shall always use the symbol
\updelta
to denote the decoration on a decorated digraph. Isomorphisms between decorated digraphs are required to preserve the decoration.
To each finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence we associate a decorated digraph called its
full witness digraph
:
Definition 2.16
(Full witness digraph)
.
Let
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence. The
full witness digraph
of
\mathfrak{I}
is the finite decorated digraph
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
defined as follows. The vertex set of
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
is
V(\Gamma({\mathfrak{I}}))\,\coloneqq\,\big\{(n,\mathfrak{c})\,:\,n\in{\mathbb{N}},\,\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}_{n}\big\}.
There is a directed edge from
(n,\mathfrak{c})
to
(n^{\prime},\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})
in
\Gamma({\mathfrak{I}})
if and only if
n<n^{\prime}
and
\mathfrak{c}\in N[\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}]
. The decoration
\updelta
of
\Gamma({\mathfrak{I}})
is given by
\updelta(n,\mathfrak{c})\coloneqq\mathfrak{c}
for all vertices
(n,\mathfrak{c})
.
Lemma 2.17.
Let
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence and let
\Gamma\coloneqq\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
. Then:
(i)
\Gamma
has no directed cycles,
(ii)
for all
x
,
y\in V(\Gamma)
,
(x,y)\in E(\Gamma)
or
(y,x)\in E(\Gamma)
if and only if
x\neq y
and
\updelta(x)\in N[\updelta(y)]
.
Proof
.
If
(x,y)\in E(\Gamma)
, then the first coordinate of
x
is strictly less than the first coordinate of
y
, so no directed cycle is possible. Item
(ii)
is immediate from the way the edge set of
\Gamma
is defined.
∎
We call a finite decorated digraph
\Gamma
satisfying conditions
(i)
and
(ii)
of Lemma
2.17
a
witness digraph
. Thus, Lemma
2.17
says that the full witness digraph of a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence is a witness digraph. Now we relate witness digraphs and tables:
Definition 2.18
(Compatible witness digraphs)
.
A witness digraph
\Gamma
is
compatible
with
(\Pi,\tau)
, where
\tau
is a table, if there exists an
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}
consistent with
(\Pi,\tau)
such that
\Gamma\cong\Gamma(\mathfrak{I})
.
The next result, which we call the
First Fundamental Theorem
of Moser–Tardos theory, computes the probability that a given witness digraph is compatible with
(\Pi,\tau)
for random
\tau
:
Theorem 2.19
(First Fundamental Theorem
[
23
, Prop. 2.5]
)
.
Sample a random table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
from the space
(\Lambda^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}},\mathbb{P}^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}})
. Then, for every witness digraph
\Gamma
, we have
\mathbb{P}[\Gamma\text{ is compatible with }(\Pi,\tau)]\,=\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\updelta(x))].
Given a constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, we let
\mathsf{Sink}(\mathfrak{c})
be the class of all witness digraphs
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma
has a single sink
\sigma\in V(\Gamma)
and
\updelta(\sigma)=\mathfrak{c}
.
We also let
\mathsf{Sink}^{\ast}(\mathfrak{c})
be a set containing exactly one representative from each isomorphism class of witness digraphs in
\mathsf{Sink}(\mathfrak{c})
. The
Second Fundamental Theorem
of Moser–Tardos theory is a purely algebraic statement that allows one to bound the quantities appearing in Theorem
2.19
for
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Sink}^{*}(\mathfrak{c})
:
Theorem 2.20
(Second Fundamental Theorem
[
23
, Prop. 2.10]
)
.
Suppose
\alpha
,
\beta\colon\mathfrak{C}\to[0,1)
are functions such that for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
\alpha(\mathfrak{c})\,\leqslant\,\beta(\mathfrak{c})\prod_{\mathfrak{c}^{\prime}\in N(\mathfrak{c})}(1-\beta(\mathfrak{c}^{\prime})).
Then for each
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathsf{Sink}^{\ast}(\mathfrak{c})}\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\alpha(\updelta(x))\,\leqslant\,\frac{\beta(\mathfrak{c})}{1-\beta(\mathfrak{c})}.
Theorems
2.19
and
2.20
are essentially present in the seminal paper
[
37
]
, cf.
[
37
, Lem. 2.1 and 3.1]
; the formulations using witness digraphs were given in
[
23
]
.
We now have all the tools needed to prove Lemma
2.11
.
Proof
of Lemma
2.11
.
Fix
p\in[0,1)
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
s>1
, and
\varepsilon
,
\eta\in(0,1)
as in the statement of Lemma
2.11
. Let
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Our goal is to bound
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{L}_{R,N,\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{c})]
, i.e., the probability that a random table
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is not
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good. We shall achieve this by applying Theorem
2.20
to certain auxiliary CSPs
\Sigma_{r}
in place of
\Pi
. Specifically, for each
r<R
, we define
\Sigma_{r}
by adding to
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
a new constraint
\mathfrak{u}
with
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{u})\coloneqq\mathsf{dom}_{R-1}(\mathfrak{c})
that is always violated.
Claim 2.21.
If a table
\tau
is not
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good, then for some
r<R
, there is a witness digraph
\Gamma\in\mathsf{Sink}^{*}(\mathfrak{u})
compatible with
(\Sigma_{r},\tau)
such that
—
|\{x\in V(\Gamma)\,:\,\updelta(x)=\mathfrak{u}\}|=1
,
—
|\{x\in V(\Gamma)\,:\,\updelta(x)\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)\}|\geqslant N
, and
—
|\{x\in V(\Gamma)\,:\,\updelta(x)\in\mathfrak{C}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)\}|<\varepsilon(|V(\Gamma)|-1)
.
Proof.
By Lemma
2.13
, there exist
r<R
and a
(\mathfrak{c},R)
-bounded
(\mathfrak{c},r,N,\varepsilon)
-Følner
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}=(\mathfrak{I}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
. Fix
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\mathfrak{I}_{n}=\varnothing
for all
n\geqslant k
and define
\mathfrak{I}^{*}_{n}\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\mathfrak{I}_{n}&\text{if }n<k,\\
\{\mathfrak{u}\}&\text{if }n=k,\\
\varnothing&\text{if }n>k.\end{cases}
This gives a finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequence
\mathfrak{I}^{*}\coloneqq(\mathfrak{I}^{*}_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for
\Sigma_{r}
. Observe that
\mathfrak{I}^{*}
is consistent with
(\Sigma_{r},\tau)
, because
\mathfrak{I}
is consistent with
(\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r},\tau)
and
\mathfrak{u}
is always violated. Now we take
\Gamma\cong\Gamma(\mathfrak{I}^{*})
, noting that the vertex
(k,\mathfrak{u})
is the unique sink in
\Gamma(\mathfrak{I}^{*})
. ∎
Let
\mathbb{G}_{r}\subseteq\mathsf{Sink}^{*}(\mathfrak{u})
be the set of all witness digraphs
\Gamma
satisfying the properties in Claim
2.21
. It follows that, for a random table
\tau
sampled from
(\Lambda^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}},\mathbb{P}^{V\times{\mathbb{N}}})
,
\mathbb{P}[\text{$\tau$ is not $(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)$-locally good}]\,\leqslant\,\sum_{r<R}\,\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}}\mathbb{P}[\text{$\Gamma$ is compatible with $(\Sigma_{r},\tau)$}].
Recall that, by the definition of
\Pi_{\mathfrak{c},r}
, the constraints in
\mathfrak{C}\setminus B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
are always violated, and the same goes for
\mathfrak{u}
. Since
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{a}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)
, Theorem
2.19
shows that for each
\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}[\text{$\Gamma$ is compatible with $(\Sigma_{r},\tau)$}]\,
\displaystyle=\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)\,:\,\updelta(x)\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)}\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\updelta(x))]\,\leqslant\,p^{|\{x\in V(\Gamma)\,:\,\updelta(x)\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},r)\}|}.
By the definition of
\mathbb{G}_{r}
, the last quantity is less than
p^{(1-\varepsilon)(|V(\Gamma)|-1)}
. It remains to bound
\sum_{r<R}\,\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}}p^{(1-\varepsilon)(|V(\Gamma)|-1)}.
To this end, we shall invoke Theorem
2.20
.
Define functions
\alpha_{r}
,
\beta_{r}\colon\mathfrak{C}\cup\{\mathfrak{u}\}\to[0,1)
as follows. Note that there is
\zeta=\zeta(d)\in(0,1)
such that
\zeta\,(1-\zeta)^{d}\,\geqslant\,(\mathsf{e}\,(d+1))^{-1},
namely
\zeta=1/\mathsf{e}
for
d=0
and
\zeta=1/(d+1)
for
d>0
. We set
\xi\,\coloneqq\,\eta\,\left(1-\zeta\right)^{\gamma_{D}(R)},
and define
\alpha_{r}(\mathfrak{a})\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}(1+\eta)\,p^{1-\varepsilon}&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C},\\
\xi&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{u}\end{cases}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{a})\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\zeta&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C},\\
\eta&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}=\mathfrak{u}.\end{cases}
Using that
|V(\Gamma)|\geqslant N+1
for all
\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}
, we can write
\displaystyle\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}}p^{(1-\varepsilon)(|V(\Gamma)|-1)}\,
\displaystyle=\,\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}}\frac{1}{\xi\,(1+\eta)^{|V(\Gamma)|-1}}\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\alpha_{r}(\updelta(x))
\displaystyle\leqslant\,\frac{1}{\xi\,(1+\eta)^{N}}\,\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathsf{Sink}^{*}(\mathfrak{u})}\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\alpha_{r}(\updelta(x)).
Now we verify the assumptions of Theorem
2.20
. In the dependency graph of
\Sigma_{r}
, the neighborhood of each
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
is either
N(\mathfrak{a})
or
N(\mathfrak{a})\cup\{\mathfrak{u}\}
, and
\displaystyle\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{a})\left(\prod_{\mathfrak{b}\in N(\mathfrak{a})}(1-\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{b}))\right)(1-\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{u}))\,\geqslant\,\zeta\left(1-\zeta\right)^{d}(1-\eta)
\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\geqslant\,\frac{1-\eta}{\mathsf{e}\,(d+1)}\,>\,(1-\eta)p^{1/s}\,\geqslant\,(1+\eta)p^{1-\varepsilon}\,=\,\alpha_{r}(\mathfrak{a}),
where we use the bounds
p\,(\mathsf{e}\,(d+1))^{s}<1
and
p^{1-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{s}}\leqslant(1-\eta)/(1+\eta)
.
On the other hand, the neighborhood of
\mathfrak{u}
is
B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)
, and
\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{u})\prod_{\mathfrak{a}\in B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)}(1-\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{a}))\,=\,\eta\,\left(1-\zeta\right)^{|B_{D}(\mathfrak{c},R)|}\,\geqslant\,\xi\,=\,\alpha_{r}(\mathfrak{u}).
Therefore, we can apply Theorem
2.20
to conclude that
\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathsf{Sink}^{*}(\mathfrak{u})}\,\prod_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\alpha_{r}(\updelta(x))\,\leqslant\,\frac{\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{u})}{1-\beta_{r}(\mathfrak{u})}\,=\,\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}.
Putting everything together,
\displaystyle\sum_{r<R}\,\sum_{\Gamma\in\mathbb{G}_{r}}p^{(1-\varepsilon)(|V(\Gamma)|-1)}\,
\displaystyle\leqslant\,R\cdot\frac{1}{\xi\,(1+\eta)^{N}}\cdot\frac{\eta}{1-\eta}\,=\,(1+\eta)^{-N}\,F(d,\eta,R),
where
F(d,\eta,R)
depends only on
d
,
\eta
, and
R
, and not on
N
, as desired.
∎
3.  Solving Borel CSPs
3.1.  Preliminaries
In §
3
we assume some basic familiarity with descriptive set theory. As mentioned in the introduction, the reader is directed to
[
32
,
45
]
for the necessary background. In particular, we shall make significant use of the material in
[
32
, Chap. 17 and 18]
.
In this subsection we give the formal definition of a Borel CSP. To begin with, for a standard Borel space
X
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we let
[X]^{n}
(resp.
[X]^{<\infty}
) be the set of all
n
-element (resp. finite) subsets of
X
. Both
[X]^{n}
and
[X]^{<\infty}
are naturally endowed with standard Borel structures. Namely, we let
(X)^{n}\subseteq X^{n}
be the Borel set of all ordered
n
-tuples of pairwise distinct points and define an equivalence relation
\sim_{n}
on
(X)^{n}
by
(x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1})\,\sim_{n}\,(y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n-1}\}\,=\,\{y_{0},\ldots,y_{n-1}\}.
By
[
30
, Ex. 6.1 and Prop. 6.3]
, the quotient space
(X)^{n}/{\sim_{n}}\cong[X]^{n}
is standard Borel. Hence, the disjoint union
[X]^{<\infty}=[X]^{0}\cup[X]^{1}\cup[X]^{2}\cup\ldots
is standard Borel as well
[
45
, Prop. 1.4]
.
Next, given standard Borel spaces
X
and
Y
, we let
[{X}\dashrightarrow{Y}]^{<\infty}
be the set of all partial maps
\varphi\colon X\dashrightarrow Y
with finite domains. Identifying each such map
\varphi
with its graph, i.e., with the set
\{(x,\varphi(x))\,:\,x\in\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)\}\,\subseteq\,X\times Y,
makes
[{X}\dashrightarrow{Y}]^{<\infty}
a Borel subset of
[X\times Y]^{<\infty}
and hence a standard Borel space in its own right.
Now we can formally define Borel CSPs:
Definition 3.1
(Borel CSPs)
.
A CSP
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
is
Borel
if:
—
V
,
\Lambda
, and
\mathfrak{C}
are standard Borel spaces,
—
the function
\mathsf{dom}\colon\mathfrak{C}\to[V]^{<\infty}
is Borel,
—
the set
\{(\mathfrak{c},\varphi)\in\mathfrak{C}\times[{V}\dashrightarrow{\Lambda}]^{<\infty}\,:\,\varphi\in\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})\}
is Borel.
A graph
G
is
Borel
if
V(G)
is a standard Borel space and
E(G)
is a Borel subset of
[V(G)]^{2}
. It is routine to check that if
\Pi
is a Borel CSP, then its dependency graph
D_{\Pi}
is Borel.
For the remainder of §
3
, we let
\Pi=(V,\Lambda,\mathfrak{C},\mathsf{dom},\mathcal{B})
be a Borel CSP and fix a Borel probability measure
\mathbb{P}
on
\Lambda
. As in §
2
, we write
D\coloneqq D_{\Pi}
and let
N[\mathfrak{c}]
be the closed neighborhood of a constraint
\mathfrak{c}
in
D
. Given a set
\mathfrak{U}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
, we let
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{U})\coloneqq\bigcup_{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{U}}\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
and
N[\mathfrak{U}]\coloneqq\bigcup_{\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{U}}N[\mathfrak{c}]
. Note that if
D
is locally finite and
\mathfrak{U}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
is Borel, then the sets
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{U})
and
N[\mathfrak{U}]
are also Borel by the Luzin–Novikov theorem
[
32
, Thm. 18.10]
.
3.2.  Proof of Lemma
1.17
We start with a simple observation, which is a consequence of a standard “large section” uniformization result in descriptive set theory
[
32
, §18.B]
:
Lemma 3.2.
If
D
has no edges and
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]<1
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, then
\Pi
has a Borel solution.
Proof
.
Since
\mathbb{P}[\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}\setminus\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]>0
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, by
[
32
, Corl. 18.7]
, there is a Borel function
F\colon\mathfrak{C}\to[{V}\dashrightarrow{\Lambda}]^{<\infty}
such that
F(\mathfrak{c})\in\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}\setminus\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})
for every constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
. Since
D
has no edges (i.e., the constraints in
\mathfrak{C}
have disjoint domains), we can pick an arbitrary value
\lambda\in\Lambda
and define, for each
v\in V
,
f(v)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\big(F(\mathfrak{c})\big)(v)&\text{if }v\in\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\text{ for some {(}unique{)} }\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C},\\
\lambda&\text{if }v\notin\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{C}).\end{cases}
Then
f\colon V\to\Lambda
is a Borel solution to
\Pi
, as desired.
∎
As mentioned in the introduction, the proof technique we employ to establish Lemma
1.17
is the method of conditional probabilities, which is essentially based on a greedy algorithm. We will construct a Borel solution to the given CSP
\Pi
in a finite number of stages. At the start of each stage, some of the variables will already have their labels determined, and our goal will be to extend this partial labeling to a larger set of variables, yielding a solution to
\Pi
in the end. We will achieve this by repeatedly applying Lemma
3.2
to certain auxiliary CSPs.
To help with the analysis of this inductive construction, we introduce the following notation. Given
U\subseteq V
and
f\colon U\to\Lambda
, the
quotient CSP
\Pi/f
encodes the problem of extending
f
to a full solution to
\Pi
. That is, we define
\Pi/f\,\coloneqq\,\big(V\setminus U,\,\Lambda,\,\mathfrak{C},\,\mathsf{dom}/f,\,\mathcal{B}/f\big),
where, for
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
(\mathsf{dom}/f)(\mathfrak{c})\coloneqq\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\setminus U
and
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{c})
is the set of all labelings
\varphi\in\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\setminus U}
such that extending
f
by
\varphi
would violate
\mathfrak{c}
. In other words, for all
\varphi\colon(\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\setminus U)\to\Lambda
, we have
\varphi\,\in\,(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{c})\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad{{f}|_{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\cap U}}\,\cup\,\varphi\,\in\,\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c}).
By definition,
f^{\prime}\colon(V\setminus U)\to\Lambda
is a solution to
\Pi/f
if and only if
f\cup f^{\prime}
is a solution to
\Pi
. Let us now make a few observations:
(i)
It is possible that
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\subseteq U
for some
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
. In this case,
(\mathsf{dom}/f)(\mathfrak{c})=\varnothing
and
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{c})
is equal to
\varnothing
if
f
satisfies
\mathfrak{c}
and
\{\varnothing\}
if
f
violates
\mathfrak{c}
(in the CSP
\Pi
).
(ii)
Since
(\mathsf{dom}/f)(\mathfrak{c})\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the dependency graph of
\Pi/f
is a subgraph of
D
. In particular, its maximum degree is at most that of
\Pi
.
(iii)
If
U
is a Borel subset of
V
and
f
is a Borel function, then
\Pi/f
is a Borel CSP.
We can now describe the inductive step in the proof of Lemma
1.17
. For a graph
G
and
r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
G^{r}
be the graph with
V(G^{r})\coloneqq V(G)
and
E(G^{r})\coloneqq\{\{x,y\}\in[V(G)]^{2}\,:\,y\in B_{G}(x,r)\}
.
Lemma 3.3.
Suppose the maximum degree of
D
is at most
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
. If
\mathfrak{I}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}
is a Borel
D^{2}
-independent set, then there is a Borel function
f\colon\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I})\to\Lambda
such that for all
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
,
(3.4)
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})]\,\leqslant\,\begin{cases}(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{I}],\\
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\notin N[\mathfrak{I}].\end{cases}
Proof
.
Form an auxiliary CSP
\Pi^{\prime}
as follows:
\Pi^{\prime}\,\coloneqq\,\big(\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I}),\,\Lambda,\,\mathfrak{I},\,{{\mathsf{dom}}|_{\mathfrak{I}}},\,\mathcal{A}\big),
where for each
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}
,
\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{c})
is the set of all mappings
\varphi\colon\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})\to\Lambda
such that
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/\varphi)(\mathfrak{a})]\,>\,(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\quad\text{for some }\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{c}].
Note that the set
Q\,\coloneqq\,\big\{(\mathfrak{c},\varphi,\mathfrak{a})\in\mathfrak{I}\times[{V}\dashrightarrow{\Lambda}]^{<\infty}\times\mathfrak{C}\,:\,\varphi\in\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}\text{ and }\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/\varphi)(\mathfrak{a})]>(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\big\}
is Borel by
[
32
, Thm. 17.25]
. Therefore, the set
\displaystyle\big\{(\mathfrak{c},\varphi)\in\mathfrak{I}\times[{V}\dashrightarrow{\Lambda}]^{<\infty}\,:\,\varphi\in\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{c})\big\}\,=\,\big\{(\mathfrak{c},\varphi)\,:\,\exists\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{c}]\,\big((\mathfrak{c},\varphi,\mathfrak{a})\in Q\big)\big\}
is also Borel by the Luzin–Novikov theorem
[
32
, Thm. 18.10]
. It follows that
\Pi^{\prime}
is a Borel CSP.
Since
\mathfrak{I}
is
D^{2}
-independent, the constraints in
\mathfrak{I}
have disjoint domains, so the dependency graph of
\Pi^{\prime}
has no edges. Next we argue that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{c})]<1
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}
.
For
\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{c}]
and
\varphi\in\Lambda^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}
, let
X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)\,\coloneqq\,\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/\varphi)(\mathfrak{a})].
By Fubini’s theorem, the expectation of the random variable
X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)
with respect to a random choice of
\varphi\sim\mathbb{P}^{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}
is
\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)]=\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]
.
If
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]=0
, then
X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)=0
almost surely. On the other hand, if
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]>0
, then, by Markov’s inequality,
\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\big[X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)>(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\big]\,<\,\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)]}{(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]}\,=\,\frac{1}{d+1}.
Therefore, regardless of the value of
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]
, the union bound gives
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{c})]\,\leqslant\,\sum_{\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{c}]}\mathbb{P}\big[X_{\mathfrak{a}}(\varphi)>(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\big]\,<\,\frac{1}{d+1}\cdot|N[\mathfrak{c}]|\,\leqslant\,1.
It follows that
\Pi^{\prime}
has a Borel solution
f\colon\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I})\to\Lambda
by Lemma
3.2
. We claim that
f
is as desired. Indeed, take any
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
. We verify (
3.4
) for
\mathfrak{a}
. If
\mathfrak{a}\notin N[\mathfrak{I}]
, then
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})=\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})
and we are done. On the other hand, if
\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{I}]
, then there is a constraint
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{I}
with
\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{c}]
, and such
\mathfrak{c}
is unique because
\mathfrak{I}
is
D^{2}
-independent. Let
\varphi\coloneqq{{f}|_{\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{c})}}
. Then
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})=(\mathcal{B}/\varphi)(\mathfrak{a})
. Since
f
is a solution to
\Pi^{\prime}
, we have
\varphi\notin\mathcal{A}(\mathfrak{c})
, which yields
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/\varphi)(\mathfrak{a})]\leqslant(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]
, as claimed.
∎
Proof
of Lemma
1.17
.
Suppose
p\in[0,1)
and
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
are such that
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
, the maximum degree of
D
is at most
d
, and
p\,(d+1)^{d+1}<1
. We seek a Borel solution to
\Pi
.
Note that the maximum degree of
D^{2}
is at most
d^{2}
. Hence, by
[
31
, Prop. 4.6]
, there is a partition
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{I}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\mathfrak{I}_{n}
of
\mathfrak{C}
into
n\leqslant d^{2}+1
Borel
D^{2}
-independent sets. Let
U_{i}\,\coloneqq\,\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I}_{i})\setminus(\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I}_{1})\cup\ldots\cup\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I}_{i-1})).
Applying Lemma
3.3
iteratively, we obtain a sequence of Borel labelings
f_{i}\colon U_{i}\to\Lambda
such that for all
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
,
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/(f_{1}\cup\ldots\cup f_{i}))(\mathfrak{a})]\,\leqslant\,\begin{cases}(d+1)\,\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/(f_{1}\cup\ldots\cup f_{i-1}))(\mathfrak{a})]&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{I}_{i}],\\
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/(f_{1}\cup\ldots\cup f_{i-1}))(\mathfrak{a})]&\text{if }\mathfrak{a}\notin N[\mathfrak{I}_{i}].\end{cases}
Fix any
\lambda\in\Lambda
and define
f\colon V\to\Lambda
by
f(v)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}f_{i}(v)&\text{if }v\in U_{i}\text{ for some $1\leqslant i\leqslant n$},\\
\lambda&\text{if }v\notin\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{C}).\end{cases}
Since for each constraint
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{C}
, there are
|N[\mathfrak{a}]|
indices
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
with
\mathfrak{a}\in N[\mathfrak{I}_{i}]
, we have
\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})]\,=\,\mathbb{P}[(\mathcal{B}/(f_{1}\cup\ldots\cup f_{n}))(\mathfrak{a})]\,\leqslant\,(d+1)^{|N[\mathfrak{a}]|}\,\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{a})]\,\leqslant\,(d+1)^{d+1}\,p\,<\,1.
But
f
is defined on all of
V
, so
(\mathsf{dom}/f)(\mathfrak{a})=\varnothing
, i.e.,
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})
is either
\varnothing
or
\{\varnothing\}
. The latter case is impossible as
\mathbb{P}[\{\varnothing\}]=1
, hence
(\mathcal{B}/f)(\mathfrak{a})=\varnothing
. This means that
f
satisfies
\mathfrak{a}
. Therefore,
f
is a Borel solution to
\Pi
, and the proof is complete.
∎
3.3.  Proof of Theorem
1.15
Recall the set-up of Theorem
1.15
: We have parameters
p\in[0,1)
,
d\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
s>1
such that:
(i)
\mathbb{P}[\mathcal{B}(\mathfrak{c})]\leqslant p
for all
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and the maximum degree of
D
is at most
d
,
(ii)
\mathsf{egr}(D)<s
, and
(iii)
p\,(\mathsf{e}\,(d+1))^{s}<1
.
Our goal is to find a Borel solution to
\Pi
.
We start by observing that the Moser–Tardos Algorithm can be used in the Borel setting:
Claim 3.5.
If
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
is a Borel good table, then
\Pi
has a Borel solution
f\in\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)
.
Proof.
It is enough to argue that there is a Borel function
f\in\mathsf{MMTA}(\Pi,\tau)
, since such
f
would be defined on all of
V
because
\tau
is good, and hence be a solution to
\Pi
by Lemma
2.3
. To this end, we observe that if
\Pi
and
\tau
are Borel, then the Maximal Moser–Tardos Algorithm can be carried out on input
(\Pi,\tau)
so that all of
\ell_{n}
,
f_{n}
,
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
,
\mathfrak{I}_{n}
,
\ell
,
f
are Borel. Indeed,
\ell_{0}
is Borel because so is
\tau
. If
\ell_{n}
is Borel, then so is
f_{n}
, and
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
is Borel as well because
\Pi
is Borel. We can then choose a Borel maximal
D
-independent subset
\mathfrak{I}_{n}\subseteq\mathfrak{C}_{n}
using
[
31
, Prop. 4.2]
. The set
\mathsf{dom}(\mathfrak{I}_{n})
is also Borel, and thus
\ell_{n+1}
is Borel as well. Finally, if
\ell_{n}
is Borel for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, then
\ell
and
f
are also Borel.
∎
Next we show that the CSP
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
defined in §
2.2
(specifically in Lemma
2.9
) is Borel:
Claim 3.6.
For each
R
,
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the CSP
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
is Borel.
Proof.
The function
\mathsf{dom}_{R}\colon\mathfrak{C}\to[V]^{<\infty}
is Borel by the Luzin–Novikov theorem
[
32
, Thm. 18.10]
, see
[
39
, Corl. 5.2]
for details. To see that the set
Q\coloneqq\{(\mathfrak{c},\varphi)\in\mathfrak{C}\times[{V}\dashrightarrow{\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}}]^{<\infty}\,:\,\varphi\in\mathcal{L}_{R,N,\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{c})\}
is Borel, we use the characterization of
(\mathfrak{c},R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good tables from Lemma
2.13
, which shows that, as there are only countably many
(\mathfrak{c},R)
-bounded finite
\mathsf{MT}
-sequences for each
\mathfrak{c}\in\mathfrak{C}
and
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
Q
is also Borel by the Luzin–Novikov theorem
[
32
, Thm. 18.10]
.
∎
Take
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that
\mathsf{egr}(D)<(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}<s
. By the definition of the exponential growth rate, there is
R\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\gamma_{D}(R)<(1-\varepsilon)^{-R}
. On the other hand, the inequality
(1-\varepsilon)^{-1}<s
is equivalent to
\varepsilon+1/s<1
, and we can take
\eta\in(0,1)
so small that
\frac{1-\eta}{1+\eta}\,>\,p^{1-\varepsilon-\frac{1}{s}}.
Finally, we take
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
so large that
(1+\eta)^{N}\,>\,F(d,\eta,R)\,\gamma_{D}(2R+1)^{\gamma_{D}(2R+1)},
where
F(d,\eta,R)
is the function from Lemma
2.11
. Claim
3.6
and Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
imply that the CSP
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
1.17
. Therefore,
\mathsf{LG}(R,N,\varepsilon)
has a Borel solution
\tau\colon V\to\Lambda^{\mathbb{N}}
. In other words,
\tau
is a Borel
(R,N,\varepsilon)
-locally good table. By Lemma
2.8
,
\tau
is good, so, by Claim
3.5
, there exists a Borel solution to
\Pi
, and we are done.
