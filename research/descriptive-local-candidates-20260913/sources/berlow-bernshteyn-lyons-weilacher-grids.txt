Abstract.
We study the complexity of locally checkable labeling (LCL) problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
from the point of view of descriptive set theory, computability theory, and factors of i.i.d. Our results separate various complexity classes that were not previously known to be distinct and serve as counterexamples to a number of natural conjectures in the field.
1.  Introduction
1.1.  Descriptive complexity of LCL problems
A common theme throughout mathematics and computer science
is the desire to understand, in rigorous terms, the complexity inherent in various problems of interest. Depending on the subject area, different notions of complexity can be proposed, resulting in different complexity hierarchies. In this paper we investigate the complexity of so-called locally checkable labeling problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
from the perspective of descriptive set theory, probability theory, and computability theory. Our contributions separate certain complexity classes that were not previously known to be distinct and serve as counterexamples to an array of natural conjectures. Further results along these lines will be presented in the forthcoming paper
[
Ber+
]
.
To start, let us identify the class of problems we will be concerned with. In the definition given below, the underlying structure is provided by a group
\Gamma
(typically assumed to be finitely generated); in our main results, we will have
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geqslant 2
. This definition can also be phrased, somewhat more generally, in graph-theoretic terms, but we will focus on groups for convenience. Extensions of our results beyond the group-theoretic framework are discussed in §
6.1
.
Definition 1.1
(LCL problems on a group)
.
A
locally checkable labeling
(
LCL
for short)
problem
on a group
\Gamma
is a triple
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
, where:
—
W\subseteq\Gamma
is a finite set, called a
window
,
—
\Lambda
is a finite set whose elements are called
labels
, and
—
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\Lambda^{W}
is a family of mappings
W\to\Lambda
, called
allowed configurations
.
A
solution
to
\Pi
, or a
\Pi
-labeling
of
\Gamma
, is a function
f\colon\Gamma\to\Lambda
such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
, the mapping
W\to\Lambda\ \colon\ w\mapsto f(w\gamma)
is in
\mathcal{A}
. The set of all
\Pi
-labelings of
\Gamma
is called a
subshift of finite type
(an
SFT
for short).
The term “locally checkable labeling problem” was introduced by Naor and Stockmeyer
[
NS95
]
in a computer science context. At the same time, subshifts of finite type are studied in topological and symbolic dynamics, where they form an important class of dynamical systems
[
LM95
,
CC10
]
.
Example 1.2
(Proper
k
-colorings of Cayley graphs)
.
Let
\Gamma=\langle S\rangle
be a group generated by a finite set
S
with
\bm{1}\notin S
. The
Cayley graph
\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
has vertex set
\Gamma
and edge set
E(\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S))\,\coloneqq\,\{\{\gamma,\sigma\gamma\}\,:\,\gamma\in\Gamma,\,\sigma\in S\}.
Following a standard set-theoretic convention, we identify each natural number
k\in\mathbb{N}
with the
k
-element set
\{0,1,\ldots,k-1\}
. Given
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a
proper
k
-coloring
of
\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
is a function
f\colon\Gamma\to k
such that
f(\gamma)\neq f(\delta)
whenever
\gamma
and
\delta
are adjacent in
\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
. Note that
f
is a proper
k
-coloring of
\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
if and only if
f
is a solution to the LCL problem
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
, where
W\,\coloneqq\,\{\bm{1}\}\cup S,\qquad\Lambda\,\coloneqq\,k,\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{A}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\varphi\in\Lambda^{W}\,:\,\varphi(\bm{1})\neq\varphi(\sigma)\text{ for all }\sigma\in S\big\}.
In particular, the set of all proper
k
-colorings of
\mathsf{Cay}(\Gamma,S)
is an SFT.
How difficult is it to solve a given LCL problem
\Pi
? This question can be interpreted in a number of ways. For instance, it can be studied from the standpoint of
algorithmic decidability
; that is, one may seek an algorithm that, given an LCL problem
\Pi
, determines whether
\Pi
has a solution. Such an algorithm exists for LCL problems on
\mathbb{Z}
and, more generally, on finitely generated free groups
[
ABJ18
, Prop. 9.3.35]
. On the other hand, refuting a conjecture of Wang
[
Wan61
]
, Berger showed that there is no such algorithm for LCL problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
[
Ber66
]
; see also
[
Rob71
]
for a simplified proof due to Robinson. Considering other finitely generated groups gives rise to a rich theory and an active research area; see the survey
[
ABJ18
]
by Aubrun, Barbieri, and Jeandel for further references.
Another approach to the complexity of LCL problems is provided by
distributed computing
, the area of computer science concerned with decentralized computational networks. For an introduction to this field, see the book
[
BE13
]
by Barenboim and Elkin. Roughly speaking, a distributed algorithm for an LCL problem
\Pi
is a communication protocol that allows autonomous processes placed at the nodes of an underlying graph
G
to compute their corresponding labels forming a solution to
\Pi
(in our setting
G
is the Cayley graph of
\Gamma
). Starting with the seminal work of Naor and Stockmeyer
[
NS95
]
, there has been a concerted effort to obtain a classification of LCL problems according to their distributed complexity. In particular, a series of contributions by a large number of researchers has culminated in a complete characterization of possible complexities of LCL problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
[
NS95
,
CKP19
,
CP19
,
Bra+17
,
GRB22
]
and the free groups
\mathbb{F}_{n}
[
NS95
,
Bra+16
,
CKP19
,
CP19
,
Cha+18
,
Bal+21
,
Cha20
,
GRB22
]
in the so-called
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model of distributed computation.
In this paper we study the complexity theory for LCL problems developed using concepts from
descriptive set theory
. The idea of employing descriptive set-theoretic notions in a combinatorial context dates back to the pioneering work of Kechris, Solecki, and Todorcevic
[
KST99
]
. By now it has grown into a fruitful field—called
descriptive combinatorics
—with many connections to other areas; see
[
KM20
]
for a survey by Kechris and Marks,
[
Pik21
]
for an introductory article by Pikhurko, and
[
Ber22a
]
for an overview of some recent developments. We also direct the reader to
[
Kec95
,
Tse22
]
for general descriptive set theory background. In descriptive combinatorics, instead of simply
solving a given LCL problem
\Pi
on a group
\Gamma
, we work with an action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
of
\Gamma
, and our goal is to solve
\Pi
on every orbit of this action in a “uniform” fashion.
Definition 1.3
(Solutions to LCL problems on group actions)
.
Let
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be an action of a group
\Gamma
. For an LCL problem
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
on
\Gamma
, a
solution
to
\Pi
on
X
, or a
\Pi
-labeling
of
X
, is a function
f\colon X\to\Lambda
such that for all
x\in X
, the mapping
W\to\Lambda\colon w\mapsto f(w\cdot x)
is in
\mathcal{A}
.
Given an action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
, any map
f\colon X\to\Lambda
gives rise to a family of labelings of
\Gamma
parameterized by the points of
X
. Namely, for each point
x\in X
, we have the corresponding mapping
f_{x}\colon\Gamma\to\Lambda
defined by
f_{x}(\gamma)\coloneqq f(\gamma\cdot x)
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
. It is then clear that
f\colon X\to\Lambda
is a solution to an LCL problem
\Pi
on
X
if and only if for all
x\in X
,
f_{x}\colon\Gamma\to\Lambda
is a solution to
\Pi
on
\Gamma
.
Recall that a group action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is
free
if
\gamma\cdot x\neq x
for all
x\in X
and
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\bm{1}\}
. It is not hard to see that if
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is a free action, then any LCL problem
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
with a solution on
\Gamma
also has a solution on
X
. Indeed, given a
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon\Gamma\to\Lambda
of
\Gamma
, we can obtain a
\Pi
-labeling
f^{*}\colon X\to\Lambda
of
X
as follows: Form a set
X^{*}\subseteq X
consisting of one point from each
\Gamma
-orbit and then define
f^{*}(\gamma\cdot x)\coloneqq f(\gamma)
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and
x\in X^{*}
. Note, however, that this construction invokes the Axiom of Choice to establish the existence of the set
X^{*}
. In particular, there is no reason to expect the function
f^{*}
to have any desirable topological or measure-theoretic
regularity properties.
Example 1.4
(Proper
2
-coloring on
\mathbb{Z}
)
.
Consider the group
\mathbb{Z}
. The Cayley graph
G\coloneqq\mathsf{Cay}(\mathbb{Z},\{1\})
is a path which is infinite in both directions. Let
\Pi
be the proper
2
-coloring problem for
G
(as described in Example
1.2
). It is then easy to see that
\mathbb{Z}
has a
\Pi
-labeling, namely the map
\mathbb{Z}\to 2
sending each
k\in\mathbb{Z}
to
0
if
k
is even and
1
if
k
is odd. (The only other
\Pi
-labeling of
\mathbb{Z}
sends
k
to
0
if
k
is odd and
1
if
k
is even.) It follows that every free action
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
admits a
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon X\to 2
. However, such a
\Pi
-labeling
f
often must exhibit various pathological properties.
For example, suppose that
T\colon X\to X
is an aperiodic invertible measure-preserving transformation on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
such that
T^{2}
is ergodic. (There are many examples of such transformations, for instance the rotation of a circle by the angle
\alpha\pi
for fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathbb{Q}
.) Iterating
T
gives rise to a free action
\mathbb{Z}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}(X,\mu)
defined by
k\cdot x\coloneqq T^{k}(x)
for all
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in X
.
We claim that, although
X
does have a
\Pi
-labeling, no
\Pi
-labeling of
X
can be
\mu
-measurable.
Indeed, if
f\colon X\to 2
were a
\mu
-measurable
\Pi
-labeling of
X
, then
A\coloneqq f^{-1}(0)
and
B\coloneqq f^{-1}(1)
would be
\mu
-measurable sets that partition
X
and satisfy
T(A)=B
, hence
\mu(A)=\mu(B)=1/2
. At the same time,
T^{2}(A)=A
, so by the ergodicity of
T^{2}
we must have
\mu(A)\in\{0,1\}
, a contradiction.
By adding extra regularity constraints, we arrive at the following definition, which introduces the “big four” complexity classes studied in descriptive combinatorics:
Definition 1.5
(Descriptive complexity classes)
.
Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and let
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
be an LCL problem on
\Gamma
. We equip the set
\Lambda
with the discrete topology. The classes
\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)
,
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\Gamma)
, and
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)
are defined as follows:
\Pi\in\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)
if and only if every free Borel action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a standard Borel space
X
has a Borel
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon X\to\Lambda
.
\Pi\in\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)
if and only if every free Borel action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}(X,\mu)
on a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
has a
\mu
-
measurable
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon X\to\Lambda
.
\Pi\in\mathtt{BAIRE}(\Gamma)
if and only if every free Borel action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a Polish space
X
has a Baire-measurable
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon X\to\Lambda
.
\Pi\in\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)
if and only if every free continuous action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a zero
-
dimensional Polish space
X
has a continuous
\Pi
-labeling
f\colon X\to\Lambda
.
Remarks 1.6
.
(i)
Since the topology on
\Lambda
is discrete, a labeling
f\colon X\to\Lambda
is Borel,
\mu
-
measurable, Baire-measurable, or continuous if and only if for every label
\lambda\in\Lambda
, its preimage
f^{-1}(\lambda)
is a Borel,
\mu
-measurable, Baire-measurable, or clopen subset of
X
respectively.
(ii)
In the definition of
\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)
,
X
can be assumed to be any particular uncountable standard Borel space, for example, the unit interval
[0,1]
, the real line
\mathbb{R}
, or the Cantor space
2^{\mathbb{N}}
. This results in no loss of generality due to the Borel isomorphism theorem
[
Kec95
, Thm. 15.6]
.
(iii)
We stress that in the definition of
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)
, the action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}(X,\mu)
need not be measure
-
preserving. (However, see Question
6.10
.)
(iv)
Recall that a topological space is
zero-dimensional
if it has a base consisting of clopen sets. The assumption that
X
is zero-dimensional in the definition of
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)
guarantees that the family of continuous functions
X\to\Lambda
is sufficiently rich to make the definition nontrivial.
The following relations between these classes hold for every finitely generated group
\Gamma
:
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)\,\subseteq\,\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)\,\subseteq\,\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)\cap\mathtt{BAIRE}(\Gamma).
The inclusion
\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)\cap\mathtt{BAIRE}(\Gamma)
is immediate since every Borel function on a standard probability space (resp. on a Polish space) is measurable (resp. Baire
-
measurable). The inclusion
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)
follows from standard results in descriptive set theory
[
Kec95
, §13]
, which imply that if
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is a Borel action on a standard Borel space
X
, then there exists a compatible zero-dimensional Polish topology
\tau
on
X
making the action continuous.
More is known for specific groups
\Gamma
. For example, when
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
the four classes coincide:
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{Z})\,=\,\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z})\,=\,\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z})\,=\,\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}).
See
[
GR21
]
by Grebík and Rozhoň for a thorough study of this case. On the other hand, for a free group
\mathbb{F}_{n}
of rank
n\geqslant 2
, the four classes are distinct and linearly ordered by inclusion:
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{F}_{n})\,\subsetneq\,\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{F}_{n})\,\subsetneq\,\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})\,\subsetneq\,\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{F}_{n}).
This chain of strict inclusions is established in
[
Bra+22
]
by Brandt, Chang, Grebík, Grunau, Rozhoň, and Vidnyánszky,

Technically, the results in
[
Bra+22
]
are stated for LCL problems on unlabeled regular trees, but virtually the same arguments apply in the setting of
\mathbb{F}_{n}
-actions.
building on and synthesizing numerous earlier results in descriptive combinatorics, distributed computing, ergodic theory, and probability theory
[
Ber23
,
Ber23a
,
Mar16
,
CP19
,
BGH19
,
CK13
,
CMT16
,
CM16
]
. For sufficiently large
n
, the three classes
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
, and
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
are distinguished by the proper
k
-coloring problem (see Example
1.2
), since the smallest
k
such that the proper
k
-coloring problem for the standard Cayley graph of
\mathbb{F}_{n}
belongs to a given complexity class is
2n+1
for the class
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
[
KST99
,
Mar16
]
,
(2+o(1))n/\log n
for the class
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
[
Ber22
,
Ber23
,
RV17
]
, and
3
for the class
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
[
CM16
]
.
In this paper we are interested in another natural class of groups, namely
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for
n\geqslant 2
. In contrast to the
\mathbb{Z}
case, for
n\geqslant 2
we have the following strict inclusion:
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\,\subsetneq\,\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n}),
because the proper
3
-coloring problem for the standard Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
belongs to the class
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
but not
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, as shown by Gao, Jackson, Krohne, and Seward
[
Gao+24
,
Gao+18
]
. In general, the class
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is relatively well understood, thanks to the work of Gao and Jackson
[
GJ15
]
, Gao, Jackson, Krohne, and Seward
[
Gao+18
]
, Grebík and Rozhoň
[
GR23
]
, and the second named author
[
Ber23a
]
. For example, it is known that
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is precisely the class of LCL problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
solvable by a sublogarithmic-time algorithm in the
\mathsf{LOCAL}
model of distributed computation. This is a general result that holds for arbitrary countable groups
[
Ber23a
, Thm. 1.15]
; for a detailed discussion of the
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
case, see
[
GR23
, §7.1]
.
Much less is known about the classes
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, and
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
. In particular, it has been a major open problem whether any of them are distinct (see, e.g.,
[
GR21
,
GR23
]
):
Question 1.7
.
For
n\geqslant 2
, are any of
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, and
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
distinct?
Here we partially answer Question
1.7
by exhibiting an LCL problem on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for each
n\geqslant 2
that belongs to
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
but not
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
:
Theorem 1.8.
For every integer
n\geqslant 2
, we have
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\setminus\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\,\neq\,\varnothing.
As a consequence,
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\neq\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
and
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\neq\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.
It remains an open problem to determine whether
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
.
In addition to making progress on Question
1.7
, Theorem
1.8
refutes a piece of conventional wisdom in descriptive combinatorics. Although measure and Baire category may behave quite differently
[
Oxt80
]
, in practice it is usually easier to solve an LCL problem Baire-measurably rather than
\mu
-measurably, and a natural expectation is that
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathtt{BAIRE}(\Gamma)
for every finitely generated group
\Gamma
. While this is true when
\Gamma
is a free group, and no counterexamples have been known before, Theorem
1.8
shows that this inclusion fails for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
n\geqslant 2
.
1.2.  Shift actions: the universal domain for descriptive combinatorics?
While the complexity classes described in Definition
1.5
contain LCL problems that can be solved on
all
free actions of
\Gamma
with specified regularity properties, it also makes sense to restrict one’s attention to particular actions of special interest. One such action is the shift:
Definition 1.9
(Shift action)
.
Let
\Gamma
be a group and let
X
be a set. We use
\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)\coloneqq X^{\Gamma}
to denote the set of all functions
x\colon\Gamma\to X
. The
shift action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
is given by the formula
(\gamma\cdot x)(\delta)\,\coloneqq\,x(\delta\gamma)\quad\text{for all $\gamma$, $\delta\in\Gamma$ and $x\in\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)$}.
We will only use the above definition for countable (in fact, finitely generated) groups
\Gamma
. If
\Gamma
is countable and
X
is a standard Borel, (zero-dimensional) Polish, or standard probability space, then so is
\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
equipped with a suitable product structure, and the shift action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
is Borel (as well as continuous and measure-preserving when these make sense).
In general, the shift action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
is not free, so it does not directly fit into the context provided by Definition
1.5
. To remedy this, we will only work on the
free part
of the shift action, i.e., the set
\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)
of all
x\in\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
such that
\gamma\cdot x\neq x
for every
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\{\bm{1}\}
. For a countably infinite group
\Gamma
and a Polish space
X
with
|X|\geqslant 2
, the set
\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)
is comeager in
\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
. Similarly, if
\Gamma
is countably infinite and
(X,\mu)
is a standard probability space, then
\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)
is conull in
\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
(with respect to the product measure
\mu^{\Gamma}
) unless
\mu
is concentrated on a single point. Therefore, in these cases there is no difference from the point of view of Baire category and measure theory whether one considers the whole space
\mathcal{S}(X,\Gamma)
or just
\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)
.
The following result shows that shift actions are in some sense “universal” for Borel and continuous solutions to LCL problems:
Theorem 1.10
(Seward–Tucker-Drob
[
ST16
]
, AB/Seward
[
Ber23a
, Thm. 1.12]
)
.
Fix a finitely generated group
\Gamma
. In the following statements,
\Pi
is an LCL problem on
\Gamma
.
(i)
For every standard Borel space
X
of cardinality at least
2
, we have
\mathtt{BOREL}(\Gamma)\,=\,\{\Pi\,:\,\text{$\Pi$ has a Borel solution on $\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)$}\}.
(ii)
For every zero-dimensional Polish space
X
of cardinality at least
2
, we have
\mathtt{CONTINUOUS}(\Gamma)\,=\,\{\Pi\,:\,\text{$\Pi$ has a continuous solution on $\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)$}\}.
Remark 1.11
.
In the papers
[
ST16
,
Ber23a
]
the above results are established in the case when
X
has exactly
2
points; the generalization to arbitrary
X
of cardinality at least
2
follows since any injection
2\to X
gives rise to a continuous

1.4.  Computable combinatorics
Another way to gauge the complexity of LCL problems is by using computability theory instead of descriptive set theory. This avenue of research has a long history dating back to the 1970s and 1980s; see the survey

2.5.  Rectangular toasts and finitary factors of i.i.d.
Before analyzing rectangular toasts from the point of view of finitary factors of i.i.d., it will be helpful to address a certain technical subtlety in Definition

.

,

(

(

\partial^{+}\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\bigcup_{K\in\mathcal{R}}\partial^{+}K
and define a map
f_{\mathcal{R}}\colon X\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
by
f_{\mathcal{R}}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\mathtt{R}&\text{if }x\in\partial\mathcal{R},\\
\mathtt{B}&\text{if }x\in\partial^{+}\mathcal{R},\\
\mathtt{G}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

3
.
To phrase Definition
3.1
another way, we color the points on the boundary of each piece
R
ed
, the points adjacent to some piece from the outside
B
lue
, and the other points
G
reen
.
This construction is illustrated in Fig.
4
. Note that, since
q\geqslant 1
,
\partial\mathcal{R}\cap\partial^{+}\mathcal{R}=\varnothing
, so

is well-defined.
Roughly, the solutions to our LCL problem
\mathsf{RT}(q)
will be the
\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
-colorings that “locally” look as if they came from this construction:
Definition 3.2
.
Given
q\geqslant 4
, we define the LCL problem
\mathsf{RT}(q)\coloneqq([0,2q]^{n},\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\},\mathcal{A})
, where
\mathcal{A}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\varphi\colon[0,2q]^{n}\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}\;:\;\varphi={{f_{\mathcal{R}}}|_{[0,2q]^{n}}}\text{ for some rectangular $q$-toast $\mathcal{R}\subseteq{[{{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}}]^{{<\infty}}}$}\big\}.
Here and in the sequel, we view
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as acting on itself by translation. Note that in Definition
3.2
, as well as in the rest of this section, we for convenience assume
q\geqslant 4
.
If
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is a free action, then a function
f\colon X\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
is an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling of
X
if and only if for every
2q
-square
Q
in
X
, there exists a rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}
such that
f
agrees with
f_{\mathcal{R}}
on
Q
. In particular, for any rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}\subseteq{[{{X}}]^{{<\infty}}}
, the map
f_{\mathcal{R}}
is an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling by definition. We wish to establish a converse to this fact: given an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling
f
, we would like to find a rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}
with
f=f_{\mathcal{R}}
. Unfortunately, this is not always possible. For instance, consider the map
f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{2}\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
given by
f(x,y)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}\mathtt{R}&\text{if }x=0,\\
\mathtt{B}&\text{if }x=1,\\
\mathtt{G}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
This is an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. Indeed, the labeling around each point of the form
(0,y)
locally looks as if that point is on the right boundary of a very large rectangle. However, this labeling clearly does not come from a rectangular toast.
Notice, though, that
f
would come from a rectangular toast if we allowed the “infinite rectangle”
(-\infty,0]\times(-\infty,\infty)
to be included. It turns out that, modulo allowing such “infinite rectangles,”
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labelings really
do
correspond to rectangular
q
-toasts.
Before stating this result precisely, we need some extra notation. Let
\mathsf{e}_{1}
, …,
\mathsf{e}_{n}
be the standard generators of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
 Given a free action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
, we let
G(X)
be the graph with
V(G(X))\coloneqq X
and
E(G(X))\,\coloneqq\,\{\{x,y\}\in[X]^{2}\,:\,\mathsf{dist}(x,y)=1\}\,=\,\{\{x,\mathsf{e}_{i}\cdot x\}\,:\,x\in X,\,1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}.
Note that
\mathsf{dist}
is precisely the path metric on
G(X)
.
In the special case when
X=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is the translation action of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
on itself, we let
G_{n}\coloneqq G(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
and observe that
G_{n}
is the Cayley graph
\mathsf{Cay}(\mathbb{Z}^{n},\{\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{e}_{n}\})
. Note that for every free action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
, every connected component of
G(X)
is isomorphic to
G_{n}
.
For any graph
G
and a set
U\subseteq V(G)
, we let
G[U]
be the
subgraph
of
G
induced
by
U
, i.e., the graph with vertex set
U
and edge set
E(G)\cap[U]^{2}
.
An
interval
in
\mathbb{Z}
is a set of the form
[a,b]
,
[a,+\infty)\coloneqq\{c\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,a\leqslant c\}
,
(-\infty,b)\coloneqq\{c\in\mathbb{Z}\,:\,c\leqslant b\}
, or
(-\infty,+\infty)\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}
, where
a\leqslant b
are integers.
Lemma 3.3.
Let
f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
be an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Consider an arbitrary connected component
C
of the induced subgraph
G_{n}[f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})]
. Let
R\coloneqq V(C)
and let
B
be the set of all points in
f^{-1}(\mathtt{B})
having a neighbor in
R
. Then
R=\partial S
and
B=\partial^{+}S
for some set
S
of the form
S=I_{1}\times\cdots\times I_{n}
, where
I_{1}
, …,
I_{n}
are
(
possibly infinite
)
intervals in
\mathbb{Z}
.
Proof
.
Let
E\subseteq E(G_{n})
be the set of all edges with one endpoint in
B
and the other one in
R
; we call such edges
bad
. Let
H
be the subgraph of
G_{n}
obtained by deleting the bad edges; that is,
V(H)\coloneqq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
E(H)\coloneqq E(G_{n})\setminus E
.
The following claim is the key to our argument:
Claim 3.4
(
R
and
B
are separated in
H
)
.
The set
R
is contained in a single component of the graph
H
, and that component is disjoint from
B
(
hence
H
has at least two components
)
.
With a little more work, one can show that
H
has
exactly two
components, one containing
R
and the other one
B
; we will not need this, however.
Intuitively, Claim
3.4
can be thought of as a discretized version of the Jordan–Brouwer separation theorem from topology, which says that a connected closed topological
(n-1)
-manifold embedded in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
separates
\mathbb{R}^{n}
into exactly two connected sets. This intuition informs our proof of the claim, which uses a discretized version of the mod 2 intersection theory that appears in some proofs of the Jordan–Brouwer theorem.
Proof of Claim
3.4
.
It suffices to prove the following subclaim:
Every cycle in
G_{n}
contains an even number of bad edges
. This implies the claim as follows.
First, it is clear that
R
is contained in a single component of
H
since
R=V(C)
,
C
is connected, and no edge in
C
is bad. Hence, if the claim failed, there would be a path
P
from
R
to some
y\in B
using no bad edges. By the definition of
B
, there must be some
x\in R
adjacent to
y
in
G_{n}
. Since
R
is in a single component of
H
we may assume
x
is the starting point of
P
. Adding the edge
\{y,x\}
to
P
yields a cycle in
G_{n}
with a single bad edge (namely
\{y,x\}
), contradicting the subclaim.
It is well known and easy to see that the binary cycle space of
G_{n}
is spanned by the
4
-cycles. (For example, this follows from the fact that the relations
\mathsf{e}_{i}+\mathsf{e}_{j}=\mathsf{e}_{j}+\mathsf{e}_{i}
for
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n
give a presentation of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.) Thus it suffices to verify the subclaim for 4-cycles.
So, let
\Sigma
be a 4-cycle in
G_{n}
. If none of its vertices are in
R
, then
\Sigma
contains no bad edges. Now suppose that
V(\Sigma)\cap R\neq\varnothing
and take an arbitrary vertex
w\in V(\Sigma)\cap R
. Since
f
is an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling, there exists a rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}
such that
f
and
f_{\mathcal{R}}
agree on
V(\Sigma)
. Since
f(w)=\mathtt{R}
, there is a rectangle
K\in\mathcal{R}
such that
w\in\partial K
. Moreover, as
q\geqslant 4
,
\Sigma
cannot meet any other rectangle in
\mathcal{R}
. Thus, an edge of
\Sigma
is bad if and only if it has one endpoint in
K
and the other one not in
K
. Since the cycle starts and ends at
w\in K
, the number of such edges must be even.
∎
Let
S
be the vertex set of the component of
H
that contains
R
.
We claim that
\partial S=R
. Indeed, any
x\in\partial S
must be an endpoint of a bad edge, and since
x\notin B
by Claim
3.4
, it follows that
x\in R
. For the other direction, if
x\in R
, then, by the definition of
\mathsf{RT}(q)
, there is a point
y\in B
adjacent to
x
. By Claim
3.4
,
y\notin S
, so
x\in\partial S
, as desired. A similar argument shows that
\partial^{+}S=B
.
It remains to show that
S
is a product of intervals. We will establish this by showing that
S
is “locally convex” in the following sense:
Claim 3.5.
Let
(w,x,y,z)
be a 4-cycle in
G_{n}
with
w
,
x
,
y\in S
. Then
z\in S
.
Proof.
Since
\partial S=R
and
\partial^{+}S=B
, if this fails
we must have
z\in B
while
w
,
y\in R
. However, we claim that if
(w,x,y,z)
is a 4-cycle in
G_{n}
with
f(w)=f(y)=\mathtt{R}
, then
f(z)\neq\mathtt{B}
.
Indeed, let
\mathcal{R}
be a rectangular
q
-toast such that
f
and
f_{\mathcal{R}}
agree on the vertices
w
,
x
,
y
,
z
and their neighbors. Then there are
K
,
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
such that
w\in\partial K
and
y\in\partial K^{\prime}
. Since
\mathsf{dist}(w,y)=2
and
q\geqslant 4
, we must have
K=K^{\prime}
. As
w
,
y\in K
and
K
is a rectangle, it follows that
z\in K
as well. For
f(z)
to be
\mathtt{B}
, then, there would need to be some other rectangle
K^{\prime\prime}\neq K
in
\mathcal{R}
with
\mathsf{dist}(z,K^{\prime\prime})=1
. But then
\mathsf{dist}(y,\partial K^{\prime\prime})=2
, which contradicts the definition of a
q
-toast as
y\in\partial K
. ∎
It remains to show that any
G_{n}
-connected set
S
satisfying Claim
3.5
is a product of intervals. For any
x
,
y\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
I(x,y)\coloneqq[\min(x,y),\max(x,y)]
be the interval between
x
and
y
, and for any
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}
,
let
I(x,y)\coloneqq I(x_{1},y_{1})\times\cdots\times I(x_{n},y_{n})
. It suffices to show that for all
x
,
y\in S
,
I(x,y)\subseteq S
.
We proceed by induction on the length of an
xy
-path
P
in
G_{n}[S]
. In the base case
x=y
, so
I(x,y)=I(x,x)=\{x\}
and there is nothing to show. For the inductive step, let
z
be the penultimate vertex of
P
. Then
I(x,z)\subseteq S
by the inductive hypothesis.
Without loss of generality, we may assume that
y=\mathsf{e}_{1}+z
. Then
I(x,y)\,\subseteq\,I(x,z)\cup\big(\{y_{1}\}\times I(x_{2},y_{2})\times\cdots\times I(x_{n},y_{n})\big).
Calling the second set in this union
T
, it remains to show that
T\subseteq S
. To this end, take any
w\in T
. Noting that
T
is
G_{n}
-connected, we argue that
w\in S
by induction on the length of a
yw
-path
Q
in
G_{n}[T]
. In the base case
w=y\in S
as desired. For the inductive step, let
v
be the penultimate vertex of
Q
. Then
(v,w,w-\mathsf{e}_{1},v-\mathsf{e}_{1})
is a 4-cycle in
G_{n}
. Since
v\in S
by the inductive hypothesis and
w-\mathsf{e}_{1}
,
v-\mathsf{e}_{1}
are in
I(x,z)\subseteq S
, we have
w\in S
by Claim
3.5
, as desired.
∎
We can now state the main result of this section: if an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
does not come from a rectangular
q
-toast, then it must contain infinite rays of red, blue, and green points.
Lemma 3.6.
Let
f\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\{\mathtt{R},\mathtt{B},\mathtt{G}\}
be an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Exactly one of the following holds:
(
RT1)
f=f_{\mathcal{R}}
for a rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}
, or
(
RT2)
there exist
x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and orthogonal
\mathsf{d}
,
\mathsf{d}^{\prime}\in\{\pm\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathsf{e}_{n}\}
such that for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
,
f(i\mathsf{d}+x)\,=\,\mathtt{R},\quad f(i\mathsf{d}+\mathsf{d}^{\prime}+x)\,=\,\mathtt{B},\quad\text{and}\quad f(i\mathsf{d}+2\mathsf{d}^{\prime}+x)\,=\,\mathtt{G}.
Proof
.
Options
(RT1)
and
(RT2)
are mutually exclusive since the rectangles in a rectangular
q
-toast are finite.
Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of all components of the graph
G_{n}[f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})]
.
For each
C\in\mathcal{C}
, fix a set
S_{C}
such that
\partial S_{C}=V(C)
and
\partial^{+}S_{C}
is the set of all blue points with a neighbor in
V(C)
,
given by Lemma
3.3
. Suppose for some
C\in\mathcal{C}
,
S\coloneqq S_{C}
is not a (finite) rectangle.
Since
S
is a product of intervals at least one of which is infinite and
\partial S\neq\varnothing
, we can choose
x\in\partial S
and
\mathsf{d}\in\{\pm\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathsf{e}_{n}\}
so that the set
L\coloneqq\{i\mathsf{d}+x\,:\,i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is contained in
\partial S\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})
. Furthermore, there is some
\mathsf{d}^{\prime}\in\{\pm\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathsf{e}_{n}\}
orthogonal to
\mathsf{d}
such that
L+\mathsf{d}^{\prime}\subseteq\partial^{+}S\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathtt{B})
.
The definition of
\mathsf{RT}(q)
implies that for any
y\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, if
f(y)=\mathtt{R}
and
f(\mathsf{d}^{\prime}+y)=\mathtt{B}
, then
f(2\mathsf{d}^{\prime}+y)=\mathtt{G}
.
Hence, in this case
(RT2)
holds.
Now assume
S_{C}
is a finite rectangle for all
C\in\mathcal{C}
and let
\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\{S_{C}\,:\,C\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Then
\partial\mathcal{R}=f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})
, and, since every blue vertex has a red neighbor,
\partial^{+}\mathcal{R}=f^{-1}(\mathtt{B})
. Therefore,
f=f_{\mathcal{R}}
. To confirm
(RT1)
, it remains to verify that
\mathcal{R}
is a rectangular
q
-toast.
Note first that all side lengths of each rectangle in
\mathcal{R}
are at least
q
. Indeed, if, say, the
i
-th side length of
K\in\mathcal{R}
is
\ell\leqslant q-1
, then there exists a point
x\in\partial^{+}K
such that
f(x)\,=\,\mathtt{B},\quad f(\mathsf{e}_{i}+x)\,=\,f(2\mathsf{e}_{i}+x)\,=\,\cdots\,=\,f((\ell+1)\mathsf{e}_{i}+x)\,=\,\mathtt{R},\quad f((\ell+2)\mathsf{e}_{i}+x)\,=\,\mathtt{B},
which cannot occur in an
\mathsf{RT}(q)
-labeling since
\ell+2\leqslant q+1<2q
.
Now suppose that there are
K\neq K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
with
\mathsf{dist}(\partial K,\partial K^{\prime})\leqslant q
. Let
x\in\partial K
,
x^{\prime}\in\partial K^{\prime}
witness this. Then there is a square of side length
q
, call it
Q
, containing both
x
and
x^{\prime}
. Using the definition of
\mathsf{RT}(q)
, let
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
be a rectangular
q
-toast
such that
f
agrees with
f_{\mathcal{R}^{\prime}}
on
Q
, and let
L
,
L^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
be such that
x\in\partial L
and
x^{\prime}\in\partial L^{\prime}
. Since
\mathsf{dist}(x,x^{\prime})\leqslant q
and
\mathcal{R}^{\prime}
is a
q
-toast, we must have
L=L^{\prime}
. Since every side length of
L
is at least
q
by the definition of a rectangular
q
-toast, the graph
G_{n}[\partial L\cap Q]
is connected. But
x^{\prime}\in\partial L\cap Q\subseteq f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})
, so
x^{\prime}
is in the same component of
G_{n}[f^{-1}(\mathtt{R})]
as
x
, a contradiction.
∎
3.2.  Partial 2-colorings
At this point, we need to modify the problem
\mathsf{RT}(q)
in order to (
a
) avoid case
(RT2)
in Lemma
3.6
, and (
b
) force the rectangular
q
-toast
\mathcal{R}
in case
(RT1)
to be complete. We will achieve these goals by requiring the green points to additionally carry a proper
2
-coloring. (A similar idea appeared in the proof of
[
Bra+17
, Thm. 6.1]
.) In this section we shall develop tools that will be employed to work with such
2
-colorings later.
Remark 3.7
.
It turns out that the
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-actions we shall work with automatically avoid case
(RT2)
away from a null/meager set, so the
2
-coloring is only truly needed to guarantee completeness.
Definition 3.8
(Partial
2
-colorings)
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be a free action. A
partial 2-coloring
of
X
is a function
c\colon X\to\{0,1,\mathtt{blank}\}
such that if
\mathsf{dist}(x,y)=1
and
c(x)
,
c(y)\in\{0,1\}
, then
c(x)\neq c(y)
. We say that a point
x\in X
is
uncolored
if
c(x)=\mathtt{blank}
and
colored
otherwise. The set of all colored points is denoted by
\mathsf{dom}(c)
.
Note that a partial
2
-coloring is a solution to a certain LCL problem with labels
\{0,1,\mathtt{blank}\}
.
As we saw in Example
1.4
, although the standard Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}
admits a proper 2-coloring, in the definable (e.g., Borel, measurable, etc.) context proper 2-colorings may fail to exist. The same is true for
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with
n\geqslant 2
, and, as we shall see, similar arguments impose restrictions on the domain of any Borel partial 2-coloring. Intuitively, Propositions
3.10
and
3.11
imply that under suitable assumptions on a
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
, the domain of a Borel partial
2
-coloring
c\colon X\to\{0,1,\mathtt{blank}\}
must “shatter” the graph
G(X)
into several smaller components; for instance, it is not possible for
\mathsf{dom}(c)
to have a connected intersection with every
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-orbit. We will also show in Proposition
3.12
that if a Borel set
U\subseteq X
“shatters”
X
into sufficiently small pieces—namely if
G(X)[U]
is component-finite—then we can find a Borel partial
2
-coloring with domain
U
.
Definition 3.9
(
c
-Separated points)
.
Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be a free action and let
c\colon X\to\{0,1,\mathtt{blank}\}
be a partial
2
-coloring. We say that points
x
,
y\in X
are
c
-separated
if
x
and
y
belong to
\mathsf{dom}(c)
and lie in different components of the graph
G(X)[\mathsf{dom}(c)]
.
Recall that
\mathcal{L}
denotes the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, and
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
the product measure.
Proposition 3.10.
Let
c\colon\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n})\to\{0,1,\mathtt{blank}\}
be a measurable partial
2
-coloring such that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}(\mathsf{dom}(c))>0
.
Then for every
\mathsf{d}\in\{\pm\mathsf{e}_{1},\ldots,\pm\mathsf{e}_{n}\}
, there exist
x\in\mathsf{dom}(c)
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that the points
x
and
k\mathsf{d}\cdot x
are
c
-separated.
Proof
.
Without loss of generality,
c^{-1}(0)
has positive measure. By the regularity of measure
[
Kec95
, Thm. 17.10]
, there exist a finite set
S\subset\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and nonempty open sets
U_{s}\subseteq[0,1]
for each
s\in S
such that, if
x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is chosen at random according to
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}
, then
\mathbb{P}\big[c(x)=0\,\mid\,x(s)\in U_{s}\text{ for all }s\in S\big]\,\geqslant\,\frac{2}{3}.
Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
be odd and large enough so that
(k\mathsf{d}+S)\cap S=\varnothing
. Now consider the nonempty open set
W
of all
x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n})
such that for each
s\in S
, we have
x(s)
,
x(k\mathsf{d}+s)\in U_{s}
. By the union bound, the probability that
c(x)=c(k\mathsf{d}\cdot x)=0
given
x\in W
is at least
1/3
. It follows that there exists some
x\in W
with
c(x)=c(k\mathsf{d}\cdot x)=0
. We claim that this point
x
is as desired.
If not, the graph
G(\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n}))
has a path
P
from
x
to
k\mathsf{d}\cdot x
contained in
\mathsf{dom}(c)
. Note that every component of
G(\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n}))
is isomorphic to the bipartite graph
G_{n}
. Thus, since
k
is odd, every path in
G(\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n}))
from
x
to
kd\cdot x
, including
P
, has an odd length. But this is impossible since
c(x)=c(k\mathsf{d}\cdot x)=0
and the colors
0
and
1
must alternate between
0
and
1
along
P
.
∎
Proposition 3.11.
Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
be a free continuous action on a Polish space

and

Proof
.
Without loss of generality,

is nonmeager. By
[

, there is a nonempty open set

such that

.
For a comeager set of points

. Fix any such

∎
On the other hand, we have the following positive results:
Proposition 3.12.
Let
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X

.

∎
Proposition 3.13.

We are now ready to define the LCL problem

Lemma 3.17.

\mathcal{R}\subseteq{[{{\mathbb{Z}^{n}}}]^{{<\infty}}}

It is clear from the definition of

. ∎

. In particular, almost every point is contained in finitely many rectangles. By Lemma
2.5
, this means that the set of all
\mathcal{R}
-complete points is null, i.e.,
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(\mathbb{Z}^{2}\cdot(X\setminus\bigcup\mathcal{R}))=1
, which implies that
\mathcal{L}^{\mathbb{Z}^{2}}(\bigcup\mathcal{R})<1
. Now we use Proposition
3.10
and Lemma
3.17
to arrive at a contradiction in exactly the same way as in the last step of the proof of Proposition
3.18
.
∎
Now we verify the other two properties of
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
stated in the theorem.
Claim 4.3.
There is a Baire-measurable solution to
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
on
\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{2})
(
i.e.,
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}\in\mathtt{BaireSHIFT}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
)
.
Proof
.
We modify the construction in the proof of Proposition
2.13
. Define
\rho\colon\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{2})\to 2
by
\rho(x)\coloneqq x(\bm{0})
. Call a square
K
in
\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{2})
safe
if
—
the side length of
K
, say
N
, is at least
q
,
—
for all
x\in X
:
—
if
x\in\partial K
, then
\rho(x)=1
,
—
if
0<\mathsf{dist}(x,\partial K)\leqslant q
, then
\rho(x)=0
,
—
and, additionally, if
x\notin K
and
\mathsf{dist}(x,\partial K)\leqslant 2N
, then
\rho(x)=0
.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be the set of all safe squares. The same argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.13
shows that
\mathcal{R}
is a square
q
-toast and
\bigcup\mathcal{R}
is a comeager (in fact, dense open) set. (Note that the square
R
used there to witness the density of
\bigcup\mathcal{R}
remains safe with the modified definition.) Then Lemma
3.15
yields a Baire-measurable
\mathsf{CRT}(q)
-labeling
f
of
\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{2})
such that
\pi\circ f=f_{\mathcal{R}}
. Note that if
K\in\mathcal{R}
is an
N
-square, then for all
K^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
with
K^{\prime}\not\subseteq K
, we have
\mathsf{dist}(\partial K,\partial K^{\prime})>2N
. This implies that for each
i\in\{0,1\}
, the sets
D_{1}(K)
and
D_{2}(K)
are disjoint from
\partial\mathcal{R}
. Therefore, letting
h_{i}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}{1}&\text{if }x\in D_{i}(K)\text{ for some }K\in\mathcal{R},\\
{0}&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}
we see that
(f,h_{1},h_{2})
is a Baire-measurable solution to
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
on
\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{2})
, as desired.
∎
Claim 4.4.
For every free computable action
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a computable set
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, there exists a computable solution to
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
on
X
. In other words,
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}\in\mathtt{COMPUTABLE}(\mathbb{Z}^{2})
.
Proof
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
X=\mathbb{N}
. As in the proof of Proposition
2.18
, we describe an algorithm that enumerates a sequence
R_{0}
,
R_{1}
, … with the following properties:
—
each
R_{i}
is an
N_{i}
-square in
X
for some
N_{i}\geqslant i+q
,
—
for all
i\neq j
, we have
\mathsf{dist}(\partial R_{i},\partial R_{j})>q
,
—
for all
x\in\mathbb{N}
,
x\in R_{x}
,
—
and, additionally, if
R_{j}\not\subseteq R_{i}
, then
\mathsf{dist}(\partial R_{i},\partial R_{j})>2N_{i}
.
Namely, once
R_{0}
, …,
R_{i-1}
have been computed, we cycle through the integers
j\geqslant i+q
until we find one such for all
t\in\{0,\ldots,i-1\}
,
\mathsf{dist}(\partial([-j,j]^{2}\cdot i),\,\partial R_{t})\,>\,\max\{q,2N_{t}\},
and, moreover, if
R_{t}\not\subseteq[-j,j]^{2}
, then
\mathsf{dist}(\partial([-j,j]^{2}\cdot i),\,\partial R_{t})\,>\,4j.
Such
j
must exist since the set
\{R_{0},\ldots,R_{i-1}\}
is finite. We then let
N_{i}\coloneqq 2j
and
R_{i}\coloneqq[-j,j]^{n}\cdot i
.
As in the proof of Proposition
2.18
,
\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\{R_{i}\,:\,i\in\mathbb{N}\}
is a computable compete square
q
-toast. Hence, by Lemma
3.16
, there is a computable
\mathsf{CRT}(q)
-labeling
f
with
\pi\circ f=f_{\mathcal{R}}
. As in the proof of Claim
4.3
, we now set, for each
i\in\{1,2\}
,
h_{i}(x)\,\coloneqq\,\begin{cases}{1}&\text{if }x\in D_{i}(R_{t})\text{ for some }t\in\mathbb{N},\\
{0}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
Notice that the function
h_{i}
is computable, since if
x\in D_{i}(R_{t})
for some

, then
R_{t}\subseteq R_{x}
, so
t\leqslant N_{x}
. Thus,
(f,h_{1},h_{2})
is a computable solution to
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
, as desired.
∎
We have now established all the required properties of
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
, and thus the proof of Theorem
1.25
is complete.
5.  Proof of Theorem
1.19
The goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.19
, i.e., that the inclusion
\mathtt{FFIID}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathtt{BaireSHIFT}(\Gamma)
is valid for every group
\Gamma
. As mentioned in the introduction, this result follows from the general analysis of the Baire-measurable combinatorics of the shift action presented in the paper
[
Ber21
]
by the second named author. The theory developed in
[
Ber21
]
is more general that what we require; below we only describe the tools necessary to deduce Theorem
1.19
.
Fix a group
\Gamma
and a finite set
\Lambda
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
be the set of all partial maps
\varphi\colon\Gamma\dashrightarrow\Lambda
with finite domains. There is a natural shift action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
given by
(\gamma\cdot\varphi)(\delta)\,\coloneqq\,\varphi(\delta\gamma)\quad\text{for all $\gamma$, $\delta\in\Gamma$ and $x\in\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)$},
where if
\delta\gamma\notin\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)
, then
(\gamma\cdot\varphi)(\delta)
is left undefined. We also let
\gamma\cdot D\coloneqq D\gamma^{-1}
for every finite subset
D\subseteq\Gamma
, so that
\mathsf{dom}(\gamma\cdot\varphi)=\gamma\cdot\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)
. A
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal
is a set
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
that is invariant under the shift action of
\Gamma
and closed under taking restrictions of functions. A
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal
\mathcal{I}
is
extendable
if for all
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)
, there is some
\psi\in\mathcal{I}
such that
\varphi\subset\psi
and
\gamma\in\mathsf{dom}(\psi)
.
Definition 5.1
(Join property)
.
A
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal
\mathcal{I}
has the
join property
if it is possible to assign to each
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
a finite subset
D(\varphi)\in\Gamma
so that, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi_{1}
, …,
\varphi_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
, and
\gamma_{1}
, …,
\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma
, if the sets
\gamma_{1}\cdot D(\varphi_{1})
, …,
\gamma_{k}\cdot D(\varphi_{k})
are pairwise disjoint, then
(\gamma_{1}\cdot\varphi_{1})\cup\ldots\cup(\gamma_{k}\cdot\varphi_{k})\in\mathcal{I}
.
We can now state the (somewhat weakened form of the) result from
[
Ber21
]
that we shall use:
Theorem 5.2
(AB
[
Ber21
, special case of Thm. 2.10]
)
.
Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and let
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
be an LCL problem on
\Gamma
. Then
\Pi\in\mathtt{BaireSHIFT}(\Gamma)
if and only if there exists an extendable
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
such that:
—
each
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
can be extended to a
\Pi
-labeling of
\Gamma
, and
—
\mathcal{I}
has the join property.
With this, we are ready to prove Theorem
1.19
.
Proof
of Theorem
1.19
.
The argument is similar to
[
Ber21
, proof of Lem. 5.1]
, with measure taking the place of Baire category. Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated group and let
\Pi=(W,\Lambda,\mathcal{A})
be an LCL problem on
\Gamma
. Suppose
\Pi\in\mathtt{FFIID}(\Gamma)
and let
f\colon\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)\to\Lambda
be a finitary
\Pi
-labeling of
\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)
. We shall use
f
to define a
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
satisfying the conditions of Theorem
5.2
, from which it will follow that
\Pi\in\mathtt{BaireSHIFT}(\Gamma)
, as desired.
Define a measurable
\Gamma
-equivariant function
\pi\colon\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)\to\mathcal{S}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
by
\big(\pi(x)\big)(\gamma)\,\coloneqq\,f(\gamma\cdot x)\quad\text{for all $x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)$ and $\gamma\in\Gamma$}.
Since
f
is a solution to
\Pi
,
\pi(x)
is a
\Pi
-labeling of
\Gamma
for every
x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)
. For
\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
, let
U_{\varphi}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(\Lambda,\Gamma)
be the clopen set of all functions
\Gamma\to\Lambda
that extend
\varphi
and define
\mathcal{I}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{\varphi\in\mathcal{S}_{<\infty}(\Lambda,\Gamma)\,:\,\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi})\text{ has positive measure}\big\}.
It is clear that, since the shift action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)
is measure-preserving,
\mathcal{I}
is a
(\Lambda,\Gamma)
-ideal. Moreover, since
\pi
sends points in
\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)
to
\Pi
-labelings of
\Gamma
, each member of
\mathcal{I}
can be extended to a
\Pi
-labeling of
\Gamma
. It remains to verify that
\mathcal{I}
is extendable and has the join property.
Take any
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
and
\gamma\in\Gamma\setminus\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)
. For each
\lambda\in\Lambda
, let
\varphi_{\lambda}\colon\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)\cup\{\gamma\}\to\Lambda
be the mapping that agrees with
\varphi
on
\mathsf{dom}(\varphi)
and sends
\gamma
to
\lambda
. Note that
U_{\varphi}\,=\,\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}U_{\varphi_{\lambda}},\qquad\text{hence}\qquad\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi})\,=\,\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi_{\lambda}}).
Since
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
, the set
\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi})
has positive measure. It follows that for some
\lambda\in\Lambda
,
\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi_{\lambda}})
must also have positive measure, i.e.,
\varphi_{\lambda}\in\mathcal{I}
. This shows that
\mathcal{I}
is extendable.
Finally, we need to argue that
\mathcal{I}
has the join property. This is where we use that
f
is finitary. Consider any
\varphi\in\mathcal{I}
. For each finite set
D\subseteq\Gamma
, let
X_{\varphi,D}\,\coloneqq\,\big\{x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)\,:\,\text{$\pi(x^{\prime})\in U_{\varphi}$ for almost all $x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)$ that agree with $x$ on $D$}\big\}.
Let us record the following observations, which follow immediately from the above definition:
—
The set
X_{\varphi,D}
is measurable by Fubini’s theorem.
—
The membership of a point
x
in
X_{\varphi,D}
is determined by the restriction of
x
to
D
.
—
For almost all
x\in X_{\varphi,D}
, we have
\pi(x)\in U_{\varphi}
.
Since
f
is finitary, almost every point
x\in\pi^{-1}(U_{\varphi})
belongs to
X_{\varphi,D}
for some finite set
D\subseteq\Gamma
. Thus, we can pick a finite set
D(\varphi)\subseteq\Gamma
such that the measure of the set
X(\varphi)\coloneqq X_{\varphi,D(\varphi)}
is positive.
Now take
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\varphi_{1}
, …,
\varphi_{k}\in\mathcal{I}
,
\gamma_{1}
, …,
\gamma_{k}\in\Gamma
and suppose the sets
\gamma_{1}\cdot D(\varphi_{1})
, …,
\gamma_{k}\cdot D(\varphi_{k})
are pairwise disjoint. Sample a random point
x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)
. Then, for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
,
x\in\gamma_{i}\cdot X(\varphi_{i})
with positive probability. The random event
E_{i}\coloneqq\{x\in\gamma_{i}\cdot X(\varphi_{i})\}
is determined by the restriction of
x
to
\gamma_{i}\cdot D(\varphi_{i})
. Since the sets
\gamma_{1}\cdot D(\varphi_{1})
, …,
\gamma_{k}\cdot D(\varphi_{k})
are disjoint, the events
E_{1}
, …,
E_{k}
are mutually independent, so with positive probability all of them occur simultaneously. In other words, the following set has positive measure:
E\,\coloneqq\,\{x\in\mathcal{F}([0,1],\Gamma)\,:\,x\in\gamma_{i}\cdot X(\varphi_{i})\text{ for all }1\leqslant i\leqslant k\}.
Note that for almost all
x\in E
and for all
1\leqslant i\leqslant k
, we have
\pi(x)\in U_{\gamma_{i}\cdot\varphi_{i}}
, or, equivalently,
\pi(x)\in U_{(\gamma_{1}\cdot\varphi_{1})\cup\ldots\cup(\gamma_{k}\cdot\varphi_{k})}
.
Therefore,
(\gamma_{1}\cdot\varphi_{1})\cup\ldots\cup(\gamma_{k}\cdot\varphi_{k})\in\mathcal{I}
, and the proof is complete.
∎
6.  Final remarks and open problems
6.1.  Unlabeled grids
Throughout this paper, we have been focusing on LCL problems that are defined on finitely generated groups (in particular,
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
) and their actions. In computer science and probability theory, it is also common to consider problems on “pure” graphs, without any additional structure. In the context of this paper, it is natural to ask whether our results extend to the framework of LCL problems on
unlabeled, undirected
n
-dimensional grids
, i.e., graphs isomorphic to the standard Cayley graph
G_{n}
of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(without edge directions or markers indicating which generator each edge corresponds to). The short answer is “yes,” with virtually the same proofs.
Formally, we can define an
LCL problem on
G_{n}
as an LCL problem
\Pi
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that the set of all
\Pi
-labelings of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is invariant under the natural action of the group
\mathsf{Aut}(G_{n})
of graph-theoretic automorphisms of
G_{n}
. Note that
\mathsf{Aut}(G_{n})
is generated by the group of translations of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(which is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
) and the group
\mathsf{Aut}_{\bullet}(G_{n})
of
root-preserving automorphisms
, i.e., the automorphisms
\varphi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying
\varphi(\bm{0})=\bm{0}
. The group
\mathsf{Aut}_{\bullet}(G_{n})
is finite and generated by permutations and sign changes of the generators.
The role of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-actions in this setting is taken by
G_{n}
-graphs
, i.e., graphs in which every connected component is isomorphic to
G_{n}
. If
\Pi
is an LCL problem on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and
G
is a graph isomorphic to
G_{n}
, we call
f\colon V(G)\to\Lambda
a
solution
to
\Pi
on
G
, or a
\Pi
-labeling
of
G
, if
f\circ\varphi
is a solution to
\Pi
on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for any (hence every) isomorphism
\varphi\colon\mathbb{Z}^{n}\to V(G)
. If
G
is a
G_{n}
-graph, then
f\colon V(G)\to\Lambda
is a
solution
to
\Pi
on
G
if it is a
\Pi
-labeling of each component of
G
. Note that if
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
is a free action, then the corresponding graph
G(X)
(as defined in §
3.1
on p.
3.1
) is a
G_{n}
-graph. Conversely, every
G_{n}
-graph is generated by a free
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-action in this way. However, not all
Borel
G_{n}
-graphs are generated by free
Borel
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-actions (even if
n=1
[
KM04
,
Mil04
]
; see also
[
Tho21
, §5]
for an analysis of the
n=2
case). In other words, in this framework we consider a restricted class of LCL problems, but attempt to solve them on a broader class of structures.
The classes of LCL problems
\mathtt{BOREL}(G_{n})
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(G_{n})
, and so on are defined by modifying the definitions of
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, etc. in obvious ways. We now have the following analog of Theorem
1.24
in this setting:
Theorem 6.1.
For every
n\geqslant 2
, there exists an LCL problem
\Pi
on
G_{n}
such that:
(i)
\Pi\in\mathtt{MEASURE}(G_{n})
,
(ii)
\Pi\notin\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
(
hence it is also not in
\mathtt{BAIRE}(G_{n})
)
,
(iii)
\Pi
has a Baire-measurable solution on
G(\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{n}))
,
(iv)
\Pi
has no finitary solution on
G(\mathcal{F}([0,1],\mathbb{Z}^{n}))
,
(v)
\Pi\in\mathtt{COMPUTABLE}(G_{n})
.
To prove Theorem
6.1
, we simply observe that the problem
\mathsf{CRT}(q)
used to witness Theorem
1.24
is, in fact, an LCL problem on
G_{n}
, because the notions of a rectangular
q
-toast and a partial
2
-coloring are automorphism-invariant. This immediately shows that parts
(ii)
,
(iii)
, and
(iv)
of Theorem
6.1
follow from the corresponding items in Theorem
1.24
. Modifying the arguments that go into proving parts
(i)
and
(v)
of Theorem
1.24
to make sure they apply to
G_{n}
-graphs and not just
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
-actions is routine, and we omit the details.
We conclude this discussion with two remarks. First, by taking the quotient under the natural action
\mathsf{Aut}_{\bullet}(G_{n})\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, it is not difficult to arrange the Baire-measurable labeling in item
(iii)
of Theorem
6.1
to be
\mathsf{Aut}_{\bullet}(G_{n})
-invariant.
Second, although the problem
\mathsf{CRT}^{+}
used in §
4
to prove Theorem
1.25
is not an LCL problem on
G_{n}
, it is not hard
to modify it to obtain an analogous version of Theorem
1.25
for unlabeled grids.
6.2.  Open problems
Many fundamental questions about the complexity of LCL problems on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
remain open. Fig.
1
summarizes the current state of our knowledge of this matter, and any relation not shown there leads to an interesting open problem. Here we give a list of a few such problems that we find particularly intriguing. In what follows,
n
is an integer at least
2
.
Question 6.2
.
Does the inclusion
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\subset\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
hold? (If it does, it must be strict.)
Question 6.3
.
Do we have
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
?
Question 6.4
.
Do we have
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})=\mathtt{FIID}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
?
Question 6.5
.
What is the relationship between
\mathtt{FFIID}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
and
\mathtt{BOREL}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
? What about
\mathtt{FFIID}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
and
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
?
No inclusion between these classes has been ruled out yet.
Question 6.6
.
Does the (necessarily strict) inclusion
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\subset\mathtt{COMPUTABLE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
hold?
Question 6.7
.
Call a free Borel action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a Polish space
X
Baire-universal
if
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is precisely the class of all LCL problems that admit a Baire-measurable solution on
X
. Note that Baire-universal actions exist. Namely, for each LCL problem
\Pi\notin\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
, we can fix a free Borel action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X_{\Pi}
that has no Baire-measurable
\Pi
-labeling and then take the disjoint union of
X_{\Pi}
over all
\Pi\notin\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
. However, this is not a very satisfying example, since we do not know in general which problems are not in
\mathtt{BAIRE}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})
. Is there a more “natural,” “concrete” example of a Baire-universal action? The shift action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}\mathcal{F}(2,\mathbb{Z}^{n})
is an obvious candidate, but it is
not
Baire-universal by Theorem
1.14
. But what about, say, the action on the circle via independent irrational rotations (see the paragraph following Proposition
2.12
)? Is it Baire-universal? More abstractly, does there exist a Baire-universal free continuous action
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}X
on a Polish space
X
with a dense orbit (such actions are called
generically ergodic
[
Tse22
, Prop. 20.18]
)?
There are also plenty of interesting problems for other finitely generated groups. Even for free groups, which are by far the best understood, some questions remain unanswered. For example:
Question 6.8
.
Do we have
\mathtt{FFIID}(\mathbb{F}_{n})=\mathtt{FIID}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
or
\mathtt{FIID}(\mathbb{F}_{n})=\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{F}_{n})
? We conjecture the answer is “no” to both.
Furthermore, there remain general questions that are of interest for all finitely generated groups. Here are two examples, the first of which is closely related to Question
6.4
.
Question 6.9
.
Given a finitely generated group
\Gamma
and a standard probability space
(X,\mu)
, let
\mathtt{FIID}(X,\mu,\Gamma)
be the class of all LCL problems on
\Gamma
that have a measurable solution on
\mathcal{F}(X,\Gamma)
. As a special case,
\mathtt{FIID}(\Gamma)=\mathtt{FIID}([0,1],\mathcal{L},\Gamma)
, where
\mathcal{L}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. Assuming
\mu
is not concentrated on a single point, does the class
\mathtt{FIID}(X,\mu,\Gamma)
depend on the space
(X,\mu)
? It does not when
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
(because
\mathtt{FIID}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z})
) and when
\Gamma
is non-amenable by a result of Bowen
[
Bow19
, Corl. 1.3]
. But what about, say,
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
?
Question 6.10
.
Let
\mathtt{MEASURE}_{\mathsf{pmp}}(\Gamma)
be the class of all LCL problems on
\Gamma
that admit a measurable solution on every free
measure-preserving
action
\Gamma\mathrel{\reflectbox{$\righttoleftarrow$}}(X,\mu)
, where
(X,\mu)
is a standard probability space. Clearly,
\mathtt{MEASURE}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathtt{MEASURE}_{\mathsf{pmp}}(\Gamma)
. Can this inclusion be strict? Once again, we know
\mathtt{MEASURE}_{\mathsf{pmp}}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z})
because
\mathtt{FIID}(\mathbb{Z})=\mathtt{MEASURE}(\mathbb{Z})
, but we do not know the answer for any group that is not virtually
\mathbb{Z}
.
