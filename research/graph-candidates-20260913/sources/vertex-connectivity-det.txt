Early Development Within the Baseline. Since the 60s, there has been an active line of
work on fast unweighted vertex connectivity [Kle69, Pod73, ET75, Eve75, Gal80, EH84, Mat87].
All of these algorithms are deterministic. The fastest among them [Eve75, Gal80] make O(n + κ2 )
maxﬂow calls, where κ denotes the vertex connectivity. However, when κ = Ω(n), they still require
Ω(n2 ) maxﬂow calls, which is as slow as the straightforward algorithm.

Beating the Baseline via Randomization. In the 80s, Becker et al. [BDD+ 82] were the ﬁrst
to surpass the baseline for unweighted vertex connectivity. Their randomized algorithm makes
Õ(n) maxﬂow calls, which takes Õ(mn1.5 ) = Õ(n3.5 ) time using the available ﬂow algorithm. Then,
Linial, Lovász, and Wigderson [LLW88] discovered a connection between vertex connectivity and
rubber band networks, leading to a randomized algorithm with Õ(nω + nκω ) = O(n3.373 ) time.3
    Later in the 90s, Henzinger, Rao, and Gabow [HRG00] surpassed the baseline in the weighted
case. Their randomized algorithm in weighted graphs takes Õ(mn) = Õ(n3 ) time, improving upon
[LLW88]. Also, they showed a deterministic algorithm in weighted graphs with O(m2 ) time. This
bound, however, still does not beat the baseline on dense graphs.

Directed Case. The minimum vertex cut problem can be generalized to directed graphs in both
weighted and unweighted cases. The goal here is to ﬁnd the smallest vertex set S such that G \ S
is not strongly connected. Since Gabow’s algorithm and the straightforward algorithm work on
directed graphs with the same guarantee, the two questions above also capture the state of the art
for deterministic algorithms in directed graphs.5

1.2     Our Tools
Next, we highlight the four technical tools that we developed and may be useful for future applica-
tions. The ﬁrst two are employed for both Theorems 1.1 and 1.2, while the last two are used only in
Theorem 1.2. To introduce the techniques in an intuitive way, we focus on unweighted undirected
graphs in this section.

1.2.1    Crossing Family.
At a high level, a crossing family P is a family of pairs of elements such that, for every two
suﬃciently large sets L and R, there must exist a “crossing” pair (x, y) ∈ P where x ∈ L and
y ∈ R.
   Below, we provide a formal deﬁnition of our crossing family, where L and R can belong to two
diﬀerent universes A and B, respectively.

Definition 1.4 (Asymmetric crossing family). Given two sets A, B and two integers ℓ, r where
ℓ ≤ |A|, r ≤ |B|, and ℓ ≤ r, an (A, B, ℓ, r)-crossing family P is a subset of A × B such that, for any
L ⊆ A and R ⊆ B with sizes at least ℓ and r, respectively, we have P ∩ (L × R) 6= ∅. The degree
of an element x ∈ A in P is |{y ∈ B | (x, y) ∈ P}|.

    Our algorithm in weighted graphs relies crucially on the asymmetric crossing family. We show
a construction with the following guarantee.

1.2.2   Common Neighborhood Clustering
Crossing families are useful when there exists a minimum vertex cut (L, S, R) where |L| = Ω(|S|).
To address the opposite case where |L| = o(|S|), we exploit the concept of common neighborhood
clustering, introduced recently in [BJMY25]. Below, NG (u) denotes the neighbors of u in G.

Lemma 1.7 (Common-Neighborhood Clustering). For any graph G = (V, E) and parameter ℓ,
there exists a collection of clusters V1 , . . . , Vz ⊆ V with the following properties. For any minimum
vertex cut (L, S, R) where |L| ≤ ℓ,

   1. L ⊆ Vi for some cluster Vi ,

   2. for every cluster Vi and for every u, v ∈ Vi , the symmetric difference

                                     |NG (u)△NG (v)| = O(ℓ log n), and

   3. every vertex belongs to at most O(log n) clusters.




                                                   4
Use Case. Lemma 1.7 is surprisingly useful in many ways. The intuition is as follows. First,
we know that the minimum vertex cut (L, S, R) is “captured” by some cluster Vi because, by
Item 1, both L and S are contained in NG [Vi ] := NG (Vi ) ∪ Vi for some Vi . Second, the structure
of inside each cluster Vi can greatly speed up the ﬂow computation. Roughly speaking, suppose
we want to compute a (s, t)-minimum vertex cut where s, t ∈ Vi , by using the existing idea from
[LNP+ 21, BJMY25], we are able to delete all edges8 from a node u ∈ Vi to a node in N (s), without
changing the (s, t)-minimum vertex cut. But after deletion, by Item 2, there are only O(ℓ log n)
edges left adjacent to every vertex except s, t. Thus, this clustering is very eﬀective in the case
when ℓ is small, e.g., when |L| ≪ |S|.
    Lastly, even if we try to work on each cluster separately, the total work is small because the
total number of edges of the graph induced by NG [Vi ] overall clusters Vi is at most Õ(m) by Item 3.

Previous Usage: Randomized, Unweighted, Undirected. Common-neighborhood cluster-
ing in [BJMY25] was used in randomized reductions from global to (s, t)-minimum vertex cuts
in parallel and distributed models. However, their algorithms are randomized and work only for
unweighted undirected graphs.

Our Usage: Deterministic, Weighted, Directed. To prove Theorem 1.1, we extend the
reach of this technique much further into the deterministic setting, and also to directed and
weighted graphs. See Section 3 for details on how we combine both crossing families and common-
neighborhood clustering to work in weighted directed graphs.

1.2.4      Selectors

The ﬁnal tool for our O(mκ)-time     algorithm for unweighted undirected graphs is a pseudorandom
object called selectors. Intuitively, a selector is a family S of subsets that, given any two disjoint
sets L and S where |L| ≥ ǫ|S|, there must exist a set U ∈ S that “hits L once” and “misses S.”

Definition 1.9 ((n, k, ǫ)-selector). For every positive integers n, k with k < n and parameters
ǫ ∈ [0, 1], an (n, k, ǫ)-selector S is a family of subsets of [n] such that, for every two disjoint subsets
L and S of [n] with sizes satisfying ǫk < |L| ≤ k and |S| ≤ k, there exists a set U ∈ S such that
|U ∩ L| = 1 and |U ∩ S| = 0.

4.6    Terminal Reduction: Proof of Lemma 4.3
This section is devoted to proving Lemma 4.3. We ﬁrst restate the omitted bottlenecks in the
overview and explain how we solve it.
    In the overview, we mentioned that common-neighborhood clustering is not eﬃcient as trivially
it needs Oe (mn) time. The algorithm presented in Section 4.3 is eﬃcient enough as logn as the
input graph has degree κ which we can guarantee because all nodes with degrees much larger than
κ must not be in L and can be deleted. However, according to Lemma 4.15(2), it requires L to
be connected in G. It is not guaranteed that LU is a connected induced subgraph. Remember


                                                     34
the deﬁnition of LU : all clusters that are completely inside L. G[L] is connected indeed, but G[L]
contains three parts (i) LU , (2) X ∩L, (3) nodes in other clusters crossing S but not in LU . Deleting
the second and third parts could cause LU to be disconnected.

Our solution. We try to add (not too many) extra edges to make LU connected. This is reﬂected
from Line 6 to Line 14 in the algorithm. The idea is: for every x ∈ X ∩ L, we want to make sure
that all neighbors of x inside LU are connected by extra edges. A trivial way to do this is by
adding a spanning subgraph on neighbors of x. However, this simple strategy does not solve the
problem: assume neighbors of x are v1 , ..., vk , after adding a spanning subgraph connecting them
(for example, a star rooted at v1 ), if v1 is not in LU , then the star helps nothing for connecting
LU : there are no edges added to the induced subgraph on LU ! We solve this issue by the shaving
lemma Lemma 4.27. At a high level, it helps to shave nodes out of the neighbors of x, so that the
remaining nodes are "roughly in LU " in the sense that they have a small neighborhood symmetric
diﬀerence just as the nodes in LU , which is enough for us. For this shaving lemma, think of A as
the neighbors of x, and A′ as the neighbors of x inside LU , we want to ﬁnd R covering A′ such that
R itself has a small neighborhood symmetric diﬀerence.

Lemma 4.27. There is a deterministic algorithm Shaving(G, A, a) that takes as inputs an undi-
rected graph G = (V, E), a vertex set A and a integer a, return R ⊆ A. Moreover, if there exists
B ⊆ V, A′ ⊆ A satisfying the following conditions

   • |A′ | ≥ 0.99|A|,

   • for any u ∈ A′ we have NG (u) ⊆ B and |B − NG (u)| ≤ a,

then the returned set R further satisfies

   • A′ ⊆ R,

   • |NG (u)△NG (v)| ≤ 5a for any u, v ∈ R.

    A careful reader might notice that even if we connect all neighbors of x inside LU for every
x ∈ X ∩ L, LU still could be disconnected: remember L has three parts, and the third part (nodes
in clusters crossing S but not in LU ) is also removed from L, which could disconnect LU . The
solution is to give a better analysis: if after removing the third part L is disconnected into many
components, instead of trying to cover the whole LU using common-neighborhood clustering, we
can try to cover each of the components, which are indeed connected. This does not change the
algorithm but puts more challenges on the analysis.

A     Exposition of Gabow’s Algorithm
Through this section, we aim to provide an exposition of Gabow’s algorithm for ﬁnding the minimum
vertex cut in light of recent developments in maxﬂow literature. We prove the following theorem.

Theorem A.1. There exists an algorithm that takes an undirected graph G = (V, E) and a cut
parameter k as inputs and outputs either a vertex cut of size less than k or guarantees that the graph
                                 √
is k-vertex connected in Õ(n + k n) maxflows on graphs of size at most m where |V | = n, |E| = m.

    The main idea of Gabow’s algorithm is to ﬁnd a pair of vertices (s, t) such that s ∈ L, t ∈ R so
that applying a unit-vertex-capacity maxﬂow across the pair would return the separator S of size
less than k. Gabow uses the Ramanujan expander to ﬁnd such a pair.

A.4    Gap Enlargement
We now see how to increase the gap and construct a graph with a large gap. We ﬁrst do the
following preliminary observations.

Lemma A.14 (Lemma 3.1 from [Gab06]). Let G = (V, E) be a graph.

  1. For any vertex x ∈ V with κ(x) > κG , x belongs to the vertex mincut of G.

  2. For any pair (x, y) ∈ V × V with κ(x, y) > κG , E ∪ {(x, y)} does not destroy any vertex
     mincut in G.

Proof.    • If κ(x) = κG , then there exists a mincut that separates x from the rest of the graph
      G. Since κ(x) > κG , every mincut of G contains x.

  • For any pair (x, y), if κ(x, y) > κG , then there exists no mincut such that x and y lie on both
    sides of the mincut. Thus, adding an edge does not destroy any mincut of the graph G.


    These two important observations play a key role in constructing the graph H from G that has
a large gap. Based on Item 1, we have the guarantee that the vertex x belongs to the mincut of
the graph G when we assume the mincut is of size less than k. Let (L, S, R) be any mincut of the
graph G. We can remove x from the graph G \ {x}, and the set S \ {x} remains the mincut of the
residual graph “G − x”. This modiﬁcation reduces δG by 1. However, removing x from G might
also reduce δG by 1.
    Let F be the set of vertices whose degree is δG − 1 in the residual graph. For any u ∈ F ,
we can increase the degree back to δG by adding an edge with one of its non-neighbors, say v,
after conﬁrming that they always lie on the same side of every mincut by computing κ(u, v). If
κ(u, v) ≥ k and we have κG < k, then we know according to Item 2 that adding edge (u, v) does

                                                56
not destroy any mincut, thereby increasing the degree of u to δG . Thus, it takes |F | maxﬂows to
increase all the degrees in F .
    We formally deﬁne the algorithm below that increases the gap by 1 and prove its correctness
and runtime.
  Algorithm 4: IncreaseGap
    Input: A graph H = (VH , EH ) with gap γ(H), a cut (L, S, R)
    Output: A graph H ′ = (VH ′ , EH ′ ) with gap γ(H) + 1, an updated cut (L, S, R)
  1 Choose any x ∈ VH . Compute κH (x) and the corresponding cut (X, Z, Y )
  2 if |Z ∪ (V \ VH )| < |S| then
  3     (L, S, R) ← (X, Z ∪ (V \ VH ), Y )
  4 end
  5 Let H ′ = H \ x removing x from the graph H
  6 Let F = {y ∈ NH (x) | degH ′ (y) = δ(H) − 1}
  7 for u ∈ F do
  8     Iterate over VH ′ to ﬁnd w such that (u, w) ∈/ EH ′
  9     Compute κH ′ (u, w) and the corresponding cut (U, Q, W )
 10     if |Q ∪ (V \ VH ′ )| < |S| then
 11         (L, S, R) ← (U, Q ∪ (V \ VH ′ ), W )
 12     end
 13     H ′ = H ′ ∪ (u, w) adding the edge (u, w) to H ′
 14 end
 15 return H ′ , (L, S, R)


Claim A.15. The Algorithm 4 takes a graph H with gap γ(H) as input and outputs a graph H ′
with gap γ(H) + 1, both of size at most H, in O(|VH |) maxflows on graphs of size at most m, where
m is the size of the original graph. It also maintains the smallest vertex cut in the original graph
G.
Proof. We maintain the invariant that (L, S, R) is a valid vertex cut in the original graph G and
update it whenever we ﬁnd a smaller one. Assuming κG < k and conditioned on not ﬁnding a cut
of size less than k so far, we have the guarantee that V \ VH is contained in every mincut.
    Hence, (X, Z ∪ (V \ VH ), Y ) is a valid cut in G. Therefore, we update (L, S, R) with the newly
found cut. Conditioned on not ﬁnding the mincut, we have the guarantee that x belongs to every
mincut in G according to Item 1. Thus, we can safely remove it from the graph H, constructing a
new graph H ′ . We have κ(H ′ ) = κ(H) − 1.
    However, δ(H ′ ) can also decrease by 1, keeping the gap the same. Now, we increase the minimum
degree of H ′ by adding edges to the vertices with degree δ(H) − 1, checking if (s, t)-vertex mincut
across the two vertices in H ′ occurs after extending the cut to the graph G by taking the union
with V \ VH ′ . Thus, we increase the minimum degree by 1, which increases γ(H ′ ) by one. The
number of edges in H ′ is, at most, the number of edges in H, as we only add edges to a vertex if
an edge is deleted from it due to the removal of x.
    Computing κ(x) takes n maxﬂows. It takes a maxﬂow call to increase the degree of a vertex in
F . Hence, the total number of maxﬂow calls is at most n + |F | ≤ 2n = O(n).

   We are now ready to prove Lemma A.8 by using Claim A.15.
