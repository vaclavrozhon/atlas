      We survey selected open problems in the theory of synchronizing automata, centered around
      the famous Černý conjecture. A deterministic finite automaton is called synchronizing if it
      admits a reset word whose action maps all states to a single state. The Černý conjecture
      states that every synchronizing automaton with n states possesses a reset word of length at
      most (n − 1)2 . We discuss avoiding words, compressing a state with another, synchroniza-
      tion of a (given or any) subset, complexity of deciding the synchronizability, average reset
      threshold, and linear-algebraic methods. Some new auxiliary results are also presented.


1     Introduction
We survey some open problems from the core of the theory of synchronizing automata, and
we present their state-of-the-art. We take the perspective focused on general (unrestricted)
automata rather than on particular subclasses with additional assumptions. The paper also
includes some new auxiliary and motivational results, which emerged from attempts of solving
the problems.
    We refer to excellent recent Volkov’s surveys: The general survey around the Černý conjec-
ture [32] and the list of subclasses of automata for which a better upper bound than the general
one is known [33].
    The first four problems seem to be closely related to each other and to the Černý problem;
they also seem to be of similar difficulty. Hence, progress in any of them will likely be helpful
in solving the others. We conclude them with a generalization.
    In the second part, we discuss the fundamental computational problem of checking whether
an automaton is synchronizing, the average reset threshold (where recently a breakthrough
result has appeared [7]), and some ideas for bypassing limitations of the linear-algebraic methods
(which are widely applicable in the theory of synchronizing automata).
    Most of the concepts touched can be visually illustrated and interactively studied using the
SynchroViewer tool [28].

1.1    Preliminaries
We consider deterministic finite complete semiautomata A = (Q, Σ, δ), where Q is the set of
states, Σ is the alphabet, and δ : Q × Σ → Q is the transition function, extended naturally to a
function Q × Σ∗ → Q. Throughout the paper, by n we always denote the number of states |Q|.

    The image of a subset S ⊆ Q by the action of a word w ∈ Σ∗ is δ(S, w) = {δ(q, w) | q ∈ S},
and its preimage is δ −1 = {q ∈ Q | δ(q, w) ∈ S}.
    A word w ∈ Σ∗ is reset (or synchronizing) if |δ(Q, w)| = 1, i.e., every state is mapped by the
action of w to the same state. An automaton is synchronizing if it admits a reset word.
    We say that a word w compresses a (necessarily nonempty) subset S ⊆ Q if |δ(S, w)| < |S|,
and it extends a subset S ⊊ Q if |δ −1 (S, w)| > |S|. Also, we say that a word w synchronizes a
subset S ⊆ Q if |δ(S, w)| = 1.
    An automaton is strongly connected if for every pair of states p, q ∈ Q, there is a word w with
δ(p, w) = q. When considering synchronization problems, the most interesting cases are strongly
connected synchronizing automata, as the Černý problem can be reduced to them.

2.2   Avoiding Words
The avoiding word problem is similar to synchronization: we want to find a word that ensures
the automaton will not be in a given state. Avoiding words first appeared in [31] but under a
different name.
    A word w ∈ Σ∗ is avoiding a state q ∈ Q if q ∈
                                                 / δ(Q, w), i.e., whenever we apply w, we know
that the automaton cannot be in q. A state q ∈ Q is avoidable if it admits an avoiding word. In
a synchronizing automaton with n ≥ 2 states, all states are avoidable, since we can use a reset
word, followed optionally by a letter that maps the single state to another one.
    It may be useful to note that a word avoiding a state q is equivalent to extending Q \ {q} to
Q.
    The main open problem concerning avoiding words is bounding the avoiding threshold – the
length of the shortest words avoiding a given state q, or generally, the maximum length such
that every avoidable state can be avoided with a word no longer than that.
    For example, the avoiding threshold of a state qi in the Černý automaton from Figure 1 is
i + 1, hence the avoiding threshold of the automaton is n.
Conjecture 2 (Avoiding threshold [18]). In a synchronizing automaton with n ≥ 2 states, every
state has an avoiding word of length at most 2n − 2.
    However, there are only two known particular (over a minimal alphabet) examples reaching
the bound 2n − 2, and 2n − 3 is the best known lower bound for all n ≥ 6 [11].
    Avoiding can also be considered from a given subset S ⊆ Q instead of from Q. Then a word
w is avoiding a state q from S if q ∈
                                    / δ(S, w).
    The best upper bound on avoiding threshold is quadratic, obtained through the following
lemma:
Lemma 3 ([27, Rephrased Lemma 1]). Let (Q, Σ, δ) be an n-state automaton, S ⊆ Q be non-
empty, and A ⊊ S be non-empty. If any state from A is avoidable, then there exists a word w
length at most n − |A| satisfying either (1) A ⊈ δ(S, w) (avoid) or (2) |δ(S, w)| < |S| (compress).
    By its iterative application, it follows that the upper bound for avoiding one state (|A| = 1)
is (n − 2)(n − 1) + 2.

2.3    Compressing a State with Another
For a state q ∈ Q, we consider words whose action map q to the same state together with some
other state p ̸= q. A word w ∈ Σ∗ compresses a state with another if δ(q, w) = δ(p, w) for some
p ∈ Q \ {q}. We call the length of the shortest such words the compress-with-another threshold.
This problem is very natural and closely related to the others, but so far largely overlooked and
has not appeared in the published literature.
Conjecture 6 (Compress-with-another threshold). For a synchronizing strongly connected n-
state automaton (n ≥ 2), for every state, the compress-with-another threshold is at most O(n).
    The assumption that the automaton is strongly connected is essential. Without it, it is easy
to construct automata with a state with a quadratic compress-with-another threshold.
Proposition 7. For every n ≥ 2, there exists a synchronizing n-state automaton with a state q
whose compress-with-another threshold is Θ(n2 ).

Proof. We take the Černý automaton on n − 1 as in Figure 1, and we add a new state p and a
new letter c. The actions of a, b fix p, whereas: δ(p, c) = q0 , δ(Q \ {p}) = q⌊n/2⌋ .
     To compress p with another state, one must use c at some point, and then compress
{q0 , q⌊n/2⌋ }. It is well-known that compressing two states in the Černý automaton requires
a quadratic word in terms of their distance on the cycle (e.g., [5]).

    For the strongly connected case, compress-with-another threshold rarely exceeds n. We know
only one potential candidate for a larger value.

[9]. However, this fact seems to be very difficult to prove, and hence we leave it as a conjecture
for this construction.

simple proof of an O(n2 ) upper bound on the avoiding threshold. Here it is the idea: To avoid
a state q, we find a word w compressing {q, p} for some other state p. If the word avoids q, we
are done. Otherwise, we apply a shortest word (of length ≤ n − 1) whose action maps q to p.
If by that, q has not been avoided, then we have both q, p in the image, so the procedure can
be repeated. We repeat it at most n − 1 times until eventually a singleton is reached, each time
appending a word of length at most O(n).

2.4    Synchronization of a Given Subset
Another natural generalization concerns the length of words needed to synchronize a given
subset. For S = Q, this is the Černý conjecture, and for |S| we know the tight upper bound
n(n − 1)/2.
   In general, the following conjecture has been stated by Cardoso:
Conjecture 9 ([5, Conjecture 5.1.1]). For a synchronizing n-state automaton, every subset S
of states can be synchronized with a word of length at most:

                              (n − 1)2 −               (2n − |S|     − 1).

   It has been shown that the Černý automata have subsets meeting this bound for every |S|.
   A better upper bound than the trivial one (obtained from summing the upper bounds on
compression) for any |S| ≥ 3 would improve the known upper bound on the reset threshold.

2.5    Synchronization of Any k-subset
The counterpart variation of the previous problem is synchronization of any subset of k states.
Here the subset is not fixed: we ask for a word that is synchronizing for some subset with k
states, so this is the question about synchronizing the easiest one.
    For k = 2, this is trivial, as any non-permutational letter does the job. For k = 3 and a
synchronizing automaton, this was studied under the name triple rendezvous time [13] The best
upper bound is quadratic but better than the trivial one, derived using linear-algebraic methods.


Theorem 10 ([13]). In a synchronizing n-state automaton, the shortest words synchronizing a
3-subset have length at most 0.1545n2 + O(n). Furthermore, the bound n + 3 is attainable.
    For k = 4, 5, there are better bounds than the trivial one [2]. For non-synchronizing automata,
the tight bound is Θ(nk ) [2], thus the situation is very similar to that of the avoiding words.
    The case k = 3 is very similar to the previous problem of compressing a state with another.
Indeed, we necessarily first use a non-permutational letter a ∈ Σ to compress a pair of states,
and then we need to compress the state in their image of this pair with some other state – the
only difference is that exactly one state is missing in the image δ(Q, a) so it cannot be that other
state.
    We can risk the following:
Conjecture 11 (cf. [2, Conjecture 20]). In a synchronizing n-state automaton, the shortest
words synchronizing any k-subset (2 ≤ k ≤ n) have length at most O(kn).
   Note that it is very similar to our Conjecture 5 about avoiding a k-subset.
   In fact, compression a state with another is somewhere in between both variants of subsets
synchronization. They can be combined into one bigger open problem:
Open Problem 12. For a subset S ⊆ Q and an integer 0 ≤ k ≤ |Q| − |S|, find upper bounds on
the length of the shortest words w such that |δ(S ∪ S ′ , w)| = 1 for some S ′ ⊆ Q \ S of size |S ′ | = k.
   For compressing a state with another, we set |S| = {q} and k = 1. Of course, it could be
generalized further to include avoiding and compression to a given subset size.

2.6   Deciding the Synchronizability
The best-known algorithm for deciding whether a given automaton is synchronizing works in
O(|Σ| · n2 ) [10]. It is a simple algorithm which builds a tree on pairs of states in a backward
way to check whether from every pair of states we can reach a singleton. However, there are no
clues whether this is the best possible, in particular regarding the quadratic factor in n. For the
simplicity of the following question, let us assume a fixed alphabet.
Open Problem 13. Given A under a fixed alphabet, is there a faster algorithm than O(n2 )
checking whether it is synchronizing?
    A possible way to argue for the impossibility of a (significant enough) improvement could be a
conditional lower bound, assuming some established hypothesis from computational complexity.
An example of such a problem is the nonempty intersection of languages recognized by two given
DFAs: If two binary tree-shaped DFAs can be solved in O(n2−ε ) time, for any ε > 0, then the
Strong Exponential Time Hyphothesis (SETH) is false [20] (there are even stronger results than
contradicting SETH).
    Recall that the synchronizability is equivalent to the condition that all pairs of states are
compressible. Curiously, if one asks whether a given pair of states is compressible, the problem
cannot be solved in O(n2−ε ). The reduction is the same as used to show PSPACE-completeness
of subset synchronization [22], which also reduces from the nonempty intersection problem with
an unrestricted number of given DFAs. It is enough to apply it for two input automata and
combine the results. Since the given DFAs are binary and we add only one extra letter to the
alphabet, we can also binarize the final construction at the cost of doubling the number of states.
Proposition 14. Given an automaton A = (Q, Σ, δ) over a fixed alphabet and a pair of states
p, q ∈ Q, there is no algorithm checking whether {p, q} is compressible in time O(n2−ε ), for every
ε > 0, assuming SETH.
    In [16], it is shown that deciding if an automaton is synchronizing is NL-complete, even if
the automaton is binary and strongly connected. The same holds for checking if a given pair of
states is compressible. Unfortunately, the constructions from these results do not seem to help
with resolving Open Problem 13.
    We note that for a random binary automaton, there exists an algorithm deciding if it is
synchronizing in O(n) time [4]. By random, we mean taking each transition uniformly and
independently at random, so each outgoing transition has exactly n choices, and there are
n|Σ|·n such random automata. This algorithm works because a random binary automaton is
synchronizing with high probability, and its synchronizability can also be confirmed in linear
time w.h.p., whereas the remaining cases are treated with the standard quadratic algorithm as
a fallback.

2.7   Average Reset Threshold
The question about the expected reset threshold of a random n-state automaton has been widely
studied, e.g., [3, 7, 14, 17, 19, 24]. The binary case is the most fundamental. In the unary case,
the probability of being synchronizing is exactly 1/n (we count the rooted trees on n labeled
vertices – there are nn−1 of them versus nn of all possible letter actions).
    However, most efforts concern an easier question: an upper bound on the average reset
threshold that holds with high probability. Of course, the questions are not equivalent, as a

2.8                        Improving Linear-Algebraic Methods
The linear-algebraic methods, which originate from early works of Pin ([21]), have demonstrated
spectacular successes in proving better upper bounds on reset threshold for particular subclasses
42                                                                  Synchronizing Automata: Open Problems


of automata (e.g., [1, 3, 8, 13, 15, 21, 25, 26]).
    Furthermore, it is currently applied behind the best cubic upper bound as well ([23, 27], and
in [12] for the old Pin-Frankl bound, although a bit differently). Recently, using the technique,
a quadratic upper bound (2n2 − 7n + 7) has been shown for the class of synchronizing automata
with a transitive permutation group and where all letters have rank at least n − 1 [34]. However,
the method by itself does not yield tight bounds in most cases.

2.8.1         Introductory Definitions
We associate a natural linear structure with an automaton A . By Rn we denote the real1
n-dimensional linear space of row vectors. Without loss of generality, we assume that Q =
{1, 2, . . . , n}. For a subset S ⊆ Q, [S] ∈ Rn is the characteristic 0-1 vector of S, where the q-th
entry is 1 if q ∈ S, and 0, otherwise.
    Analogously, a word w ∈ Σ∗ corresponds to a linear transformation of Rn . By [w], we
denote the matrix of this transformation in the standard basis [1], . . . , [n] of Rn . Clearly, the
matrix [w] has exactly one non-zero entry in each row, because the automaton is deterministic.
In particular, [w] is row stochastic, i.e., the sum of entries in each row is equal to 1, hence
multiplying by [w] preserves the sum of the entries of a vector. Then we have [uv] = [u][v] for
every two words u, v ∈ Σ∗ .
    Given a subset S ⊆ Q and a word w ∈ Σ∗ , the [S][w] is the cumulative image, which is equal
to [δ(S, w)] if and only if two of states in S were compressed by w. However, a q-th entry of
[S][w] is non-zero if and only if q ∈ δ(S, w).
    On the other hand, when considering preimages, we always have [S][w]T = [δ −1 (S, w)], where
[w]T is the transposition of [w].
    When X is a set of vectors, by V = span(X) we denote the linear subspace of Rn generated
by the vectors from X as a linear combination. Let dim(V ) denote the dimension of V , and
also for a set of vectors, dim(X) is dim(span(X)).

2.8.2         Ascending Chain of Subspaces
Let Σi and Σ≤i denote all words over Σ of length equal i and at most i, respectively.
   The common way to utilize linear algebra for synchronizing automata is to build a chain of
subspaces starting from a subset S:

                                  Li = span({[S][w] | w ∈ Σ≤i }), for i = 0, 1, . . .,

or, when working with preimages:

                                  Li = span({[S][w]T | w ∈ Σ≤i )}, for i = 0, 1, . . ..

The key argument is that if the next subspace (generated using longer words) has the same
dimension as the previous one, then all the further subspaces stabilize and are the same. So if
reaching a certain dimension implies that there is a word of a desired property, then we have a
linear bound on its length.
    To work on a concrete example, we recall the proof of the upper bound on the reset threshold
of an Eulerian automaton [15], for its relative simplicity. The utilized property of an Eulerian

vector norm0 (v) has the sum of entries equal to 0. Also, for a set of non-zero 0-1 vectors V , let
norm0 (V ) be the set of the normalized vectors from V .
    For a non-empty subset S ⊊ Q, we build the chain of subspaces:
                                                                   
                         Li = span norm0 ([S])[w]T | w ∈ Σ≤i , for i = 0, 1, . . ..

If for an i, there are vectors in Li with sum ̸= 0, this means the number of positive and neg-
ative entries is different than in norm0 ([S]), hence there exists some word w ∈ Σ−1 such that
|δ −1 (S, w)| ̸= |S|. By the Eulerian property, there exists an extending word for S of length at
most i. Since the maximum possible dimension is n and L0 has dimension 1, we have i ≤ n − 1.
     Applying the argument iteratively (at most (n − 2) times), starting from δ −1 ({q}, a) for a
letter that extends {q}, we end up with the upper bound 1 + (n − 2)(n − 1) on the length of a
reset word, i.e., δ −1 ({q}, aw) = Q, where |w| = (n − 2)(n − 1). This is the state-of-the-art upper
bound on the reset threshold of an Eulerian automaton, whereas the best known lower bound
       2
is ⌊ n 2−3 ⌋ (conjectured to be tight) [29].

2.8.3    Generalization and an Improvement
Instead of always starting independently from a new subset S, we can build our chains of
subspaces starting from more vectors (more initial subsets) at once. In this way, we can increase
the initial dimension, which will let the chains reach the maximum dimension faster. As a
drawback, this leads to conditional bounds: we will obtain an extending word for one of the
initial subsets, but will not know for which one.
    The idea of starting from more subsets has already appeared in the proofs for one-cluster
automata, e.g., [25, 26], yet this is different (and independent) from our proposal, as iterations
are applied to one subset that emerges from the previous one as usual.
    First, for S1 , . . . , Sj , let us define:
                                                                                  
        L0 = span (norm0 ([S1 ], . . . , [Sj ])) ;   Li = span L0 [w]T | w ∈ Σ≤i , for i = 0, 1, . . ..

Then it gives an extending word w for some of S1 , . . . , Sj of length at most i ≤ n − dim(L0 ).
    Now, to use it in the construction of a reset word: Starting from S1 = δ −1 ({q}, a), suppose
that we iteratively find the shortest extending words w1 , . . . , wk for the current subsets S1 , . . . , Sk ,
ending up with Q, where k ≤ n − 2. We want to bound the (sum of the) lengths of these
extending words. For S1 , . . . , Sk , we have the upper bound n − dim(span(norm0 ([S1 ], . . . , [Sk ])))
on the length of one them. We remove the extended subset and use the argument for the set of
k − 1 vectors, repeating it until no subset is left.
    First, we relate the dimension of a normalized vectors’ subspace with the dimension of the
0-1 vectors’ subspace, which will simplify our dimension consideration to only the latter case.
Let 1n be the vector with all 1s.
Lemma 18. Let X be a set of 0-1 vectors in Rn that contain 1n . Then dim(X) = dim(norm0 (X))+
1.
44                                                          Synchronizing Automata: Open Problems


Proof. To show dim(X) ≥ dim(norm0 (X)) + 1, we observe that all the normalized vectors are in
span(X): for a vector norm0 (v), which has only a and −b entries, we multiply v by a + b and
subtract 1n multiplied by b. Additionally, the normalized vectors cannot generate vectors with
non-zero sum, so clearly X generates a larger subspace.
    To show dim(X) ≤ dim(norm0 (X)) + 1, we observe that adding 1n to the set norm0 X allows
us to generate all the vectors from X. For the normalized vectors, we apply the inverse transform
to the above: to a vector with a and −b entries, we add 1n multiplied by b and then divide by
a + b.

   The improvement depends on the dimension of the subspaces generated by the vectors of
the subsets. Unfortunately, in the general case, the initial dimension can be logarithmic in the
number of vectors, but it can be linear in the best case. We show an elementary combinatorial
proof of the logarithmic lower bound.
Lemma 19. Let X be a set of non-zero 0-1 vectors in Rn . Then dim(X) ≥ ⌈log2 |X|⌉.

Proof. We construct a binary tree as follows. For a set X ′ of 0-1 vectors, we find the first
distinguishing position, which is the smallest index such that there is a vector in X ′ that has 0
and another that has 1 on this position. Then we split the set into two, depending on whether
they have 0 or 1 on this position. For both subsets, we construct the subtree recursively. If
there is no distinguishing position, then we already have only one vector in the set, so we end
with a leaf. Since we have exactly |X| leaves and the tree is binary, the height of the tree is at
least ⌈log2 |X|⌉ (the root is at level 0).
    We show that we can find a subset Y ⊆ X of linearly independent vectors by following the
longest path in the tree from the root to the leaf. We start from the leaf, taking the stored
vector as the first one into our set Y . Being at a node x, we go up to its parent x′ . Note that
all vectors taken so far have the same value on the distinguishing position of x′ . We follow the
second edge from x′ up to any leaf of that subtree, taking a vector v stored there. Then v has
a different value at the distinguishing position of x′ than all the vectors in Y taken so far.
     1. If v has 1 at the position of x’, then it is linearly independent with Y , since all vectors in
        Y have 0 on this position. So we take v to Y .
     2. If v has 0 at the position of x′ , then we have two subcases. If all vectors in Y and v have 0
        on all earlier positions (the path from the root to x′ is labeled only by 0s), then we ignore
        v and continue. Note that this subcase can happen only once, as all further cases must be
        (1). Otherwise, all vectors in Y and v have 1 on some common (earlier) position, so the
        vectors in Y preserve the sum on that position and on the position of x′ , whereas v breaks
        it. Hence, v is linearly independent with Y and we take it.
   Altogether, we visit ⌈log2 k⌉ + 1 nodes, where at most once we do not add a vector. So we
have at least ⌈log2 k⌉ linearly independent vectors.

consider just raw vectors [S1 ], . . . , [Sk ]. Consider the case of one initial subset separately.

    Unfortunately, the logarithmic upper bound on the initial dimension is tight in general:
trivially, 2k vectors that cover all 0-1 combinations on k positions generate the dimension log2 k.
The bound is also tight if all the vectors have pairwise different sums (as it in our case).
Lemma 21. For all k ≤ n, there is a set X of k 0-1 vectors in Rn with sums of entries 1, 2, . . . , k
such that dim(X) = ⌈log2 k⌉ (when k is not a power of 2) or dim(X) = 1 + log2 k (otherwise).
Proof. Let j be the largest integer such that 2j − 1 ≤ k, hence j = ⌊log2 (k + 1)⌋. We set j vectors
with the sum of the entries equal to 20 , 21 , . . . , 2j−1 , respectively, and where the 1s appear at
pairwise disjoint positions. The above bound ensures that the number of needed positions is at
most k. We need to add the remaining k − j vectors: note that each natural number up to 2j − 1
is expressible as the sum of our powers of 2, hence we can take vectors that are generated from
our j generating vectors.
    If 2j − 1 = k, then we already have k vectors and the dimension is equal to j. Then k + 1 is
a power of 2, thus j = ⌊log2 (k + 1)⌋ = log2 (k + 1) = ⌈log2 k⌉.
    If 2j − 1 < k, then for the vectors with 2j , . . . , k 1s, we need one more generating vector with
1s on k − 2j + 1 so far unused positions. Then for each 2j , . . . , k, we can find a vector with that
sum which can be generated using the additional generating vector and a subset of our j vectors.
So we have ⌊log2 (k + 1)⌋ + 1 generating vectors. Since k + 1 is not a power of 2, this is equal to
⌊log2 k⌋ + 1. If k is not a power of 2, we end with ⌈log2 k⌉. Otherwise, we end with 1 + log2 k.
    However, it might led to better upper bounds, as the logarithmic worst-case is unlikely to
happen with vectors in the context of an automaton. The following is an informal question on
that:
Open Problem 22. What are the tight upper bounds for the initial dimensions of the subspace
of vectors of subsets appearing from an iterative application of the linear-algebraic method for
constructing a word?
