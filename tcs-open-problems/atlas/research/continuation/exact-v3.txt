The Exact Matching problem asks whether a bipartite graph with edges colored red and blue admits a perfect matching with exactly t t red edges.
Introduced by Papadimitriou and Yannakakis in 1982, the problem has resisted deterministic polynomial-time algorithms for over four decades, despite admitting a randomized solution via the Schwartz–Zippel lemma since 1987. We establish the Affine-Slice Nonvanishing Theorem (ASNC) for all bipartite braces: a Vandermonde-weighted determinant polynomial is nonzero whenever the exact- t t fiber is nonempty.
This yields a deterministic O ⁡ ( n 6 ) O(n^{6}) algorithm for Exact Matching on all bipartite graphs via the tight-cut decomposition into brace blocks.
The proof proceeds by structural induction on McCuaig’s brace decomposition.
We handle the McCuaig exceptional families via a parity-resolved cylindric-network positivity argument, the replacement determinant algebra, and the narrow-extension cases (KA, J ​ 3 → D ​ 1 J3\to D1 ).
For the superfluous-edge step, we introduce two closure tools: a matching-induced Two-extra Hall theorem that resolves the rank- ( m − 2 ) (m{-}2) branch via projective-collapse contradiction, and a distinguished-state q q -circuit lemma that eliminates the rank- ( m − 1 ) (m{-}1) branch entirely by showing that any minimal dependent set containing the superfluous state forces rank m − 2 m{-}2 .
A Lean 4 formalization accompanies the paper.
The formalization reduces the main theorem to eight explicit hypotheses corresponding to results proved here and in McCuaig (2001), with all algebraic tools, the induction skeleton, and the combinatorial infrastructure fully machine-checked. Introduction 1.1. The Exact Matching problem Let G = ( A ∪ B , E ) G=(A\cup B,E) be a bipartite graph with | A | = | B | = n |A|=|B|=n , equipped with an edge coloring ρ : E → { 0 , 1 } \rho\colon E\to\{0,1\} (blue/red) and a target integer t t .
The Exact Matching (EM) problem asks: Does G G admit a perfect matching M M with exactly t t red edges, i.e., | { e ∈ M : ρ ⁡ ( e ) = 1 } | = t |\{e\in M:\rho(e)=1\}|=t ? This problem was introduced by Papadimitriou and Yannakakis [ 25 ] in 1982, who conceived it while studying constrained spanning tree problems.
At the time of its introduction, the problem was conjectured to be NP-hard.
A few years later, Mulmuley, Vazirani, and Vazirani [ 23 ] gave a randomized polynomial-time algorithm using the Schwartz–Zippel lemma [ 27 , 31 ] and a weight isolation technique.
This placed Exact Matching in the complexity class RP \mathrm{RP} , making it one of the few natural problems known to separate P \mathrm{P} from RP \mathrm{RP} conditionally. Despite sustained effort over four decades, no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm has been found for the general case.
Deterministic progress has been confined to special graph classes and parameterized regimes: planar graphs [ 30 ] , exact weight matching in bipartite graphs with bounded neighborhood diversity or bandwidth [ 10 ] , correct-parity matching [ 8 ] , and dense graphs [ 9 ] .
On the algebraic side, Fenner, Gurjar, and Thierauf [ 11 ] placed bipartite perfect matching in quasi-NC.
Gurjar and Thierauf [ 13 ] gave a deterministic parallel algorithm, and Svensson and Tarnawski [ 28 ] extended the quasi-NC result to general graphs.
Karzanov [ 16 ] studied exact weight matching in complete bipartite graphs.
In the online setting, bipartite matching admits an optimal competitive ratio of 1 − 1 / e 1-1/e [ 15 ] .
The problem remains a natural candidate for testing the P = RP \mathrm{P}=\mathrm{RP} hypothesis and sits at the intersection of algebra, combinatorics, and complexity theory. 1.2. Proof overview The proof of ASNC ( Theorem 1.2 ) proceeds by structural induction on bipartite matching-covered graphs, following the brace-theoretic decomposition framework of McCuaig [ 21 ] . Step 1: Tight-cut reduction. Using Hall-block convolution ( Theorem 3.4 ), we reduce to brace graphs (bipartite graphs satisfying the Hall + 2 +2 condition: | N ⁡ ( S ) | ≥ | S | + 2 |N(S)|\geq|S|+2 for all proper nonempty S S ).
This step is an exact determinant factorization that holds for all n n . Step 2: Minimal vs. nonminimal braces. For minimal braces (those where deleting any edge destroys the brace property), McCuaig’s theory provides a classification into narrow local extension types (stable extension, index-1, index-2) and three exceptional families (biwheels, prisms, Möbius ladders).
We handle: • Stable extension / index-2: via two-node interpolation (KA, Corollary 4.5 ), using the coprimality of distinct Vandermonde bases. • Index-1: via three-node Vandermonde elimination ( Corollary 4.6 ), reducing to same-base divisibility. • McCuaig’s exceptional families: biwheels via jet extraction at a unique-minimizer row, prisms and Möbius ladders via a parity-resolved cylindric-network positivity argument ( Section 5 ). A superfluous edge e = ( r , c ) e=(r,c) in a brace can be deleted while preserving the brace property.

Proof architecture The proof combines three ingredients that have not previously appeared together: (1) Graph-structural induction on McCuaig’s brace decomposition tree, proving ASNC for all braces via local moves (superfluous-edge deletion, narrow extensions). The tight-cut decision reduction then extends the algorithm to all bipartite graphs. (2) Algebraic elimination via replacement determinants and generalized Vandermonde coprimality, converting local graph moves into polynomial divisibility contradictions. (3) Matching-induced two-extra Hall closure , exploiting the perfect matching in the support graph of the actual G G -packet to exclude the residual projective-collapse case. Bipartite graphs and perfect matchings Throughout, G = ( A ∪ B , E ) G=(A\cup B,E) denotes a bipartite graph with parts A = { 0 , … , n − 1 } A=\{0,\ldots,n-1\} (rows) and B = { 0 , … , n − 1 } B=\{0,\ldots,n-1\} (columns), equipped with an edge coloring ρ : E → { 0 , 1 } \rho\colon E\to\{0,1\} .
A perfect matching of G G is a bijection σ : A → B \sigma\colon A\to B such that ( i , σ ⁡ ( i ) ) ∈ E (i,\sigma(i))\in E for all i i .
The existence of a perfect matching can be characterized by Tutte’s theorem [ 29 ] or, in the bipartite case, by Hall’s marriage theorem [ 14 ] .
We write PM ⁡ ( G ) \mathrm{PM}(G) for the set of perfect matchings of G G .
The matching polytope of G G (the convex hull of characteristic vectors of perfect matchings) is a face of the Birkhoff polytope [ 5 , 7 ] . For a target t ∈ { 0 , … , n } t\in\{0,\ldots,n\} , the exact- t t fiber is F G , t = { σ ∈ PM ⁡ ( G ) : ∑ i = 0 n − 1 ρ ⁡ ( i , σ ⁡ ( i ) ) = t } . F_{G,t}=\Bigl\{\sigma\in\mathrm{PM}(G):\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\rho(i,\sigma(i))=t\Bigr\}. 2.2. Foundational properties This subsection collects four structural results that underpin the induction.
The first (cycle reachability) characterizes the combinatorial structure of achievable red counts. The second (affine closure) establishes the polyhedral geometry of exact- t t fibers. The third (universal nonvanishing at small n n ) provides the base case for the structural induction, and the fourth (row-initial-form nonvanishing) gives a sufficient condition used in the biwheel proof. Theorem 2.5 (Cycle reachability) . Let G = ( A ∪ B , E ) G=(A\cup B,E) be a bipartite graph with edge coloring ρ \rho and let M 0 M_{0} be any fixed perfect matching of G G .
For each even-length alternating cycle C C (edges alternating between M 0 M_{0} and E ∖ M 0 E\setminus M_{0} ), define the red-count displacement Δ ⁡ ( C ) = | { e ∈ C ∖ M 0 : ρ ⁡ ( e ) = 1 } | − | { e ∈ C ∩ M 0 : ρ ⁡ ( e ) = 1 } | . \Delta(C)=|\{e\in C\setminus M_{0}:\rho(e)=1\}|-|\{e\in C\cap M_{0}:\rho(e)=1\}|. Proof. The proof rests on the classical symmetric-difference decomposition for matchings. Forward direction. Let M M be any perfect matching.
The symmetric difference M 0 ⊕ M = ( M 0 ∖ M ) ∪ ( M ∖ M 0 ) M_{0}\oplus M=(M_{0}\setminus M)\cup(M\setminus M_{0}) decomposes into vertex-disjoint even-length alternating cycles C 1 , … , C k C_{1},\ldots,C_{k} (every vertex has degree exactly 0 0 or 2 2 in M 0 ⊕ M M_{0}\oplus M , and bipartiteness forces even length).
For each C i C_{i} , the matching M M uses the non- M 0 M_{0} edges of C i C_{i} and agrees with M 0 M_{0} outside ⋃ i C i \bigcup_{i}C_{i} .
Therefore | M ∩ R | = | M 0 ∩ R | + ∑ i = 1 k Δ ⁡ ( C i ) . |M\cap R|=|M_{0}\cap R|+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta(C_{i}). Reverse direction. Conversely, given any collection { C i } \{C_{i}\} of vertex-disjoint alternating cycles, flipping M 0 M_{0} along ⋃ i C i \bigcup_{i}C_{i} (replacing M 0 ∩ C i M_{0}\cap C_{i} by C i ∖ M 0 C_{i}\setminus M_{0} in each cycle) yields a valid perfect matching M ′ M^{\prime} .
The matching property is preserved because within each cycle the substitution replaces a perfect matching of the cycle’s vertex set with another, and outside the cycles nothing changes.
The red count of M ′ M^{\prime} is | M 0 ∩ R | + ∑ i Δ ⁡ ( C i ) |M_{0}\cap R|+\sum_{i}\Delta(C_{i}) by construction.
∎ Remark 2.6 . Theorem 2.5 holds for general (non-bipartite) graphs as well, provided one restricts to even-length alternating cycles.
Odd-length alternating walks (involving blossom structure) do not give valid perfect-matching transitions: flipping along an odd walk disrupts the matching constraint at the blossom’s stem vertex. Cycle reachability reduces Exact Matching to a constrained cycle-packing problem: given M 0 M_{0} , one seeks a collection of vertex-disjoint alternating cycles whose displacements sum to t − | M 0 ∩ R | t-|M_{0}\cap R| .
The difficulty lies in the interaction between vertex-disjointness and displacement values: the set of achievable displacement sums is not, in general, a numerical semigroup or even a contiguous interval.
For instance, K n , n K_{n,n} with R = M 0 R=M_{0} (the identity matching) achieves every red count except t = n − 1 t=n-1 , since a permutation with n − 1 n-1 fixed points necessarily has n n fixed points. Structural Induction Framework The proof of ASNC proceeds by structural induction on bipartite matching-covered graphs, following McCuaig’s brace-theoretic decomposition [ 21 ] .
This section presents the induction framework in detail: the brace-theoretic reduction, the patch transfer theorem that governs how local graph modifications act on the boundary-minor state, the minimality of the boundary-minor state as an induction object, and the superfluous-edge identity that drives the induction on nonminimal braces. 3.1. Brace theory We recall the key definitions from McCuaig’s theory of braces [ 21 , 22 ] .
See also Robertson and Seymour [ 26 ] for related structural decomposition results, Lovász [ 19 ] and Lovász and Plummer [ 20 ] for background on matching theory and Pfaffian orientations, and Kothari and Narain [ 17 ] for recent work on tight cuts. Definition 3.1 (Brace) . A bipartite graph G = ( A ∪ B , E ) G=(A\cup B,E) is a brace if it is connected, has a perfect matching, and for every proper nonempty subset S ⊊ A S\subsetneq A , | N ⁡ ( S ) | ≥ | S | + 2 . |N(S)|\geq|S|+2. Equivalently, G G is 2 2 -extendable: every matching of size at most 2 2 extends to a perfect matching. Definition 3.2 (Superfluous edge) . An edge e ∈ E ⁡ ( G ) e\in E(G) is superfluous in a brace G G if G ∖ e G\setminus e is also a brace.
A brace is minimal if it has no superfluous edges. In a brace, a superfluous edge is the same as a strictly thin edge of index 0 0 , and it must join two vertices of degree at least 3 3 . Remarks on obstructions Remark 7.32 (Why simpler strategies fail) . The two-sided character of the proof is not an artifact of the presentation.
Several natural one-sided strategies are structurally blocked: • Visibility. One might try to detect the superfluous edge from the leading term of its row’s polynomial.
This fails because the superfluous edge can be the dominant term in that row, hiding its cofactor contribution. • Pairwise divisibility. One might try to show that two column-hole cofactors cannot both vanish.
This fails because the identity v ⊤ ​ N = 0 v^{\top}N=0 (cofactor vectors are orthogonal to the remaining columns) forces mutual divisibility automatically. • Span exclusion. One might argue that the superfluous edge’s column cannot lie in col ⁡ ( N ) \operatorname{col}(N) for graph-structural reasons.
This fails because Vandermonde interpolation is too flexible: coefficients can always be found to place any vector in the column span, regardless of which edges the graph has. • Column relabeling. Permuting column indices to get a nicer configuration is inadmissible because the Vandermonde bases μ i = λ + α i \mu_{i}=\lambda+\alpha_{i} are tied to specific row labels. Permuting columns changes the polynomial non-trivially. The Two-extra Hall closure overcomes the rank- ( m − 2 ) (m{-}2) obstructions by exploiting the perfect matching in the G G -packet support graph.
