                                                                                      Abstract
                                               One of the most basic conjectures in quantum complexity theory states that every t-query
                                            quantum algorithm can be simulated on most inputs by a poly(t)-query classical algorithm. If
                                            true, this would provide broad justification for the need for structure in quantum speedups.
                                               We settle this conjecture for parallel quantum algorithms, showing that every t-query d-round
                                                                                                            2
                                            quantum algorithm can be simulated on most inputs with tO(d ) classical queries. This suggests
                                            that for unstructured problems, superpolynomial speedups would require quantum circuits of
                                            superconstant depth, and exponential speedups would further require polynomial depth. In
                                            contrast, most known speedups for structured problems are achieved by highly parallel, low-depth
                                            algorithms.
                                               Our techniques also carry new implications for the status of BPP vs. BQP relative to a
                                            random oracle, a similarly longstanding problem.

1       Introduction
A major goal of quantum computing research is to understand the nature of problems that ad-
mit superpolynomial speedups over classical computation. Prototypical examples of problems
admitting such dramatic quantum advantage—Simon’s problem [Sim97], Period-Finding [Sho97],
Forrelation [Aar10, AA18], among others—are all highly structured. In Simon’s problem and
Period-Finding, structure comes in the form of a hidden subgroup; in Forrelation, structure
comes in the form of a promise that one function is correlated with the Fourier transform of another.
    As Aaronson writes in [Aar22], three decades of quantum algorithms research suggest a “law of
conservation of weirdness”: Superpolynomial quantum speedups appear limited to settings where
quantum algorithms can exploit some form of global structure to concentrate amplitudes on the
correct answers. Understanding the extent to which such a law holds is central to understanding
the power and limitations of quantum computing. For example, it would further explain why
superpolynomial speedups have been prevalent in number-theoretic cryptography but not for
NP-complete problems.
    A particularly elegant formalization of such a law, in the query model where most quantum
algorithms operate, is given by the following conjecture:

Conjecture (The simulation conjecture). Let A be a t-query quantum algorithm. For every ε, δ > 0,
there is a classical algorithm that makes poly(t, 1/ε, 1/δ) queries and approximates A’s acceptance
probabilities to within an additive ε on 1 − δ fraction of inputs.

     This conjecture first appeared in [Aar05, Aar08, AA14] and has been attributed to folklore
dating back to 1999. To see how it relates to the “need for structure”, note that it implies
that F : {0, 1}N → {0, 1, ∗} can admit a superpolynomial quantum-classical query separation
only if F −1 ({0, 1}) is a very small set, corresponding to a highly specific promise on F ’s inputs.1
Relatedly, this conjecture, if true, would rule out the standard query-complexity approach to proving
BPP = ̸ BQP relative to a random oracle—a similarly longstanding open problem [FR99]. Both
the simulation conjecture and the status of BPP vs. BQP relative to a random oracle appear in
Aaronson’s list of “Ten semi-grand challenges for quantum computing theory” [Aar05] and Fortnow’s
list of “Open oracle questions for the 21st century” [For21].

be a bounded degree-t polynomial. There is a variable i ∈ [N ] such that

2     This work
While the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture has established itself as a problem of independent interest
and import in discrete Fourier analysis [O’D12, FHH+ 14], it is natural to ask if we have made the
simulation conjecture substantially more difficult by viewing quantum algorithms abstractly as
polynomials. Indeed, Aaronson and Ambainis themselves concluded their paper with the suggestion
that one could conceivably resolve the simulation conjecture without going through their conjecture,
by reasoning directly about quantum algorithms.

from the fact that query weights upper bound influences after suitable normalization. On the other
hand, we will also show that Conjecture 1 is already sufficient to yield the simulation conjecture
(Claim 9.3).

Remark 2.1 (Strong versions of Conjecture 1 and the simulation conjecture). We will also consider
a strong version of Conjecture 1 where the poly(δ) dependence is replaced with a 1/ polylog(1/δ)
dependence (see Conjecture 2). This version implies a strong version of the simulation conjecture
with a polylog(1/δ) dependence. As we discuss in Section 2.3, these strong versions carry added
implications for random-oracle separations.

2.1.1   Comparison with the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture
 Query weights vs. influences. Related to the fact that Conjecture 1 is specific to quantum
 algorithms is that it concerns query weights rather than influences. Both are measures of a variable’s
“importance”. However, query weights are a property of a quantum algorithm A, whereas influences
 are a property of its acceptance probability f : {0, 1}N → [0, 1]. Two algorithms with different query
weights can share the same acceptance probability and the same influences. Through Conjecture 1,
we are therefore proposing a “syntactic”/“whitebox” approach to the simulation conjecture, which
 contrasts with Aaronson and Ambainis’s “semantic”/“blackbox” approach.
     While both types of approaches have their advantages, our work highlights two advantages of
 query weights. The first, as already mentioned, is that Conjecture 1 can only be easier to prove
 than the Aaronson–Ambainis conjecture. As we will soon discuss in Section 2.2, this enables us to
 make progress on the simulation conjecture. The second is that query weights are, in a formal sense,
 algorithmically much easier to estimate than influences. As we will discuss in Section 2.3, we use
 this to derive new implications for the status of BPP vs. BQP relative to a random oracle.

The hybrid method vs. the polynomial method. As a related distinction, Aaronson and
Ambainis’s approach to the simulation conjecture is based on the polynomial method for quantum
query lower bounds [BBC+ 01]. Ours, as we detail in Section 4, is instead based on the hybrid
method [BBBV97], which uses query weights to quantify the progress a quantum algorithm is able
to make towards distinguishing nearby inputs. In their paper, [BBBV97] used this method to lower
bound the query complexity of unstructured search (and notably, established the optimality of


                                                   3
Grover’s algorithm even before its discovery). The hybrid method can be viewed as a precursor
to the adversary method [Amb02], which has in turn grown in sophistication and power over
the years, culminating in proofs that the general adversary bound characterizes quantum query
complexity [HLŠ07, Rei09, LMR+ 11]. Our work shows that the basic hybrid method is already
useful for the simulation conjecture. An avenue for future work is to see if more can be gained by
combining our techniques with the full generality of the adversary method.

2.2    Parallel quantum algorithms
As evidence of the viability of our approach, we make progress on Conjecture 1, and hence the
simulation conjecture, by taking the parallelism of quantum algorithms into account. While a
sequential query algorithm makes a single query after another, a parallel algorithm is able to make
multiple queries at once:

    Round complexity is the query model’s abstraction of circuit depth, while the number of
parallel queries per round is analogous to circuit width. Round complexity is also synonymous with
adaptivity, with 1-round algorithms being most commonly called nonadaptive.
    In quantum computing, as in classical computing, parallelism is a standard way to trade
hardware for time. Small-depth quantum circuits are especially desirable because they also reduce
exposure to decoherence, making them valuable on NISQ devices where full error correction is
not yet available [Pre18]. Their importance persists even in the fault-tolerant setting, since error
correction and fault-tolerant implementations incur substantial overheads in practice [Got10, Aar22].
For these reasons, parallel quantum algorithms have received significant attention in the query
model [vD98, Zal99, GR04, NY04, Mon10, MJM15, KLPY10, JMdW17, Bur19, GSTW24, CGV25].
    We defer the statement of our result regarding Conjecture 1 to the body of the paper and state
only its implication for the simulation conjecture:

Nonadaptive algorithms. Previously, the simulation conjecture was open even for 1-round
algorithms. In fact, prior works point to such algorithms being surprisingly powerful in the context
of quantum-classical query separations. Simon’s problem, Period-Finding, and Forrelation
are all solved by nonadaptive quantum query algorithms. Similarly, Yamakawa and Zhandry’s
recent exponential separation for an unstructured search problem is also achieved by a nonadaptive
quantum query algorithm [YZ24].
    In light of Theorem 1, any counterexample to the simulation conjecture must instead be based
on quantum query algorithms that are highly adaptive.

2.3   Implications for random-oracle separations
Bernstein and Vazirani [BV97], in their paper that defined BQP, exhibited an oracle relative to
which BPP ≠ BQP. Shortly after, Fortnow and Rogers [FR99] asked if the same is true relative to a
random oracle, a problem that remains open today. The simulation conjecture, if true, would rule
out the standard approach of obtaining such a separation via query complexity.
   We show that Conjecture 1, if true, would represent an even more significant “technique-
independent” barrier. Under its strong version, we get:


   Theorem 2. Assuming the strong version of Conjecture 1, PromiseBPPO ̸= PromiseBQPO
   for a random oracle O if and only if PromiseBPP ̸= PromiseBQP.


    In words, the status of PromiseBPP vs. PromiseBQP is equivalent in the random oracle and
unrelativized worlds. We prove the forward implication; the reverse implication is standard and holds
unconditionally [BG81, FR99]. Under the standard version of Conjecture 1, we still get a barrier
but lose the equivalence: If PromiseBQPO ̸⊆ PromiseHeurBPPO then PromiseBPP =          ̸ PromiseBQP
(see Theorem 11).

Unconditional equivalences. By our proof of Theorem 1, we further obtain unconditional
versions of Theorem 2 for parallel quantum circuits. For example, for the class of polylogarithmic-
depth circuits (QNC), we get:




                                                  5
    Theorem 3. PromiseQNCO ̸⊆ PromiseQuasiBPPO for a random oracle O if and only if
    PromiseQNC ̸⊆ PromiseQuasiBPP.

    Here QuasiBPP refers to randomized quasipolynomial time.
    QNC, in addition to its practical relevance, has been shown to be a rather powerful subclass
of BQP. Notably, Cleve and Watrous [CW00] constructed QNC circuits for the quantum Fourier
transform, thereby showing that Shor’s factoring algorithm can be implemented using such circuits
with polynomial-time classical pre- and post-processing. Results like this suggest that QNC already
captures certain important aspects of quantum advantage. Alas, Theorem 3 shows that providing
evidence of such advantage in the form of a random-oracle separation is no easier than simply
proving an unrelativized separation.
    To our knowledge, Theorems 2 and 3 give the first natural examples of unresolved complexity-
theoretic statements that can be shown to be equivalent in the random-oracle and unrelativized
worlds. While the sweeping Random Oracle Hypothesis [BG81] was quickly shown to be false [Kur82,
Har85, CCG+ 94], it remains of interest to understand the extent to which the random-oracle world
reflects the unrelativized world. Indeed, [CCG+ 94] concluded by asking: “It would be very interesting
to know if there are identifiable problem classes for which the random oracle results do point in the
right direction.” Our results represent progress towards such an understanding.
Remark 2.2 (Promise problems). Our proofs of these equivalences require the generality of promise
problems. In particular, we rely on the existence of a PromiseBQP-complete problem, whereas BQP
has no known complete problems.


3    Independent work
Independently and concurrently, Liu and Mutreja [LM26] gave a poly(t)-query classical simulation
of nonadaptive t-query quantum algorithms on most inputs. Their proof extends to handle quantum
algorithms that make O(log t/ log log t) adaptive singleton queries before one parallel batch of t
queries. They obtained these results by proving a “dense indistinguishability conjecture” for such
algorithms, and showing that this conjecture implies the simulation conjecture.
    Also independently and concurrently, Escudero Gutiérrez, Palazuelos, and Saucedo [EPS26]
gave a O(tr2 )-query classical simulation of nonadaptive t-query rank-r quantum algorithms on most
inputs. Rank-r algorithms measure on all qubits and accept on at most r of the measurement

4.4   Implications for random-oracle separations: Proofs of Theorems 2 and 3
Separations in the query model yield relativized separations, but the converse does not necessarily
hold: simulations in the query model do not necessarily yield relativized simulations. In particular,
the simulation conjecture alone does not imply that BPPO = BQPO for a random oracle O. While
it guarantees that every quantum Turing machine making poly(n) queries to an oracle has an
approximator that only makes poly(n) classical queries, it does not guarantee that this approximator
can be implemented using only poly(n) classical time steps.
    To prove Theorem 2, we show that the specific approximators that we construct via Conjecture 1
do admit time-efficient implementations assuming PromiseBPP = PromiseBQP. To sketch why, first
recall that Conjecture 1 implies the simulation conjecture via a regularity lemma, which we now
state formally:

Lemma 4.7 (Regularity lemma). Let A be a t-query quantum algorithm. There is a classical
decision tree T of depth poly(t, 1/η, log(1/δ)) such that the restricted algorithm Aπ is η-regular with
probability at least 1 − δ with respect to a random root-to-leaf path π ∼ T induced by a uniform
random input x ∼ {0, 1}N .

   This tree T is constructed using a simple greedy algorithm: If A is already η-regular, we are
done. Otherwise, there must be at least one variable i ∈ [N ] with query weight larger than η. We
query any such variable at the root of our decision tree and recurse on Axi =0 and Axi =1 . We use a
martingale argument to show that a random path through this tree will w.h.p. terminate after not
too many steps, and Lemma 4.7 follows.
   For our proof of Theorem 2, we further need an algorithmic version of this regularity lemma:

Lemma 4.8 (Algorithmic regularity lemma, see Lemma 10.1 for the formal version). If PromiseBPP =
PromiseBQP, there is an efficient randomized algorithm that takes as input a representation of A as
a quantum oracle circuit and on any input O ∈ {0, 1}N computes that path π in T that O follows.

    A natural attempt at proving Lemma 4.8 would be to estimate the query weight of each variable
i ∈ [N ], in search of one with weight at least η. This would be the root node in the path π, and we
can then recurse. However, such a search is too time consuming: A BQP oracle algorithm makes
at most poly(n) = polylog(N ) queries to O ∈ {0, 1}N , and so our classical simulation has to take
at most polylog(N ) classical
                          P time steps. We instead give a PromiseBQP algorithm for estimating
sums of query weights, i∈S Ex [Wi (x)] for any subset S ⊆ [N ]. With this subroutine in hand, a
standard branch-and-prune algorithm (á la [GL89, KM93]) allows us to identify a variable of high
query weight with only a logarithmic dependence on N .
    Theorem 2 then follows easily, and the proof of Theorem 3 is completely analogous.


                                                  12
Complexity of finding high query weight vs. high influence variables. [AA14]’s reduction
of the simulation conjecture to their conjecture is also based on a regularity lemma, where their
notion of a regular quantum algorithm is one whose influences, rather than query weights, are
all low. They also showed that their regularity lemma can be made algorithmic, but under the
much stronger assumption that P = P#P rather than PromiseBPP = PromiseBQP. The crux of this
difference lies in the fact that our subroutine for finding a variable of high query weight, as discussed
above, is in PromiseBQP, whereas [AA14]’s subroutine for finding one of high influence is in NP#P .
    This highlights a dual advantage of query weights over influences. On one hand, since query
weights upper bound influences (after suitable normalization, see Proposition A.2), any regularity
lemma for low query weights also serves as one for low influences. And yet, somewhat paradoxically,
our regularity lemma for query weights admits a much more efficient algorithmic implementation
than [AA14]’s one for influences.

8     Incorporating ε-dependence and removing N -dependence
In this section we show how statements such as Lemma 6.1 and Theorem 7 can be generically
improved in two ways:

    1. We show how they imply a version that bounds ε-sized deviations rather than the constant-
       sized ones. That is, we get a version where δ := Pr[|f (x) − E[f (x)]| ≥ ε] at the cost of a
       poly(1/ε)-overhead in the regularity parameter. (Note that if min{Pr[Accf ], Pr[Rejf ]} ≥ δ
       then either Accf or Rejf witnesses the fact Pr |f (x) − E[f (x)]| ≥ 61 ≥ δ.)

    2. We then show how any dependence on N in the regularity parameter can be removed by

       in the introduction, though we cannot quite apply it as a blackbox in our setting.

We first show that if Conjecture 2 (the strong version of Conjecture 1 where the dependence on δ is

Theorem 10 (Theorem 2 restated). Assuming Conjecture 2, PromiseBPPO ̸= PromiseBQPO for a
random oracle O with probability 1 if and only if PromiseBPP ̸= PromiseBQP.

Proof. The easy direction is essentially the same argument as in [FR99] which showed that if
BPP = ̸ BQP then they are also unequal relative to a random oracle with probability 1. We use the
promise version of this statement. Briefly, if PromiseBPP ̸= PromiseBQP, there is some promise

oracle O. Furthermore, an argument of [BG81] (originally for languages but easily extending
to promise problems) shows that if L ∈   / PromiseBPP, then it is also not in PromiseBPPO with
probability 1 over the randomness of O.
     The harder direction is to show that if PromiseBPP = PromiseBQP then PromiseBPPO =
