1        Introduction

Consider a poset—or equivalently an n-vertex directed acyclic graph (DAG) G = (V, E).    ⃗ Suppose
we are also given a boolean function f : V → {0, 1} defined on the vertex set of this graph. Function
f is said to be monotone if f (u) ≤ f (v) for all (u, v) ∈ E.
                                                           ⃗ We study algorithms that efficiently
determine the distance to monotonicity of f , denoted dmon (f ), which is defined as the minimum
number of entries of f that have to change in order to make f monotone.
    Monotonicity testing has been studied extensively in the literature (see Section 1.3). However,
nearly all these works study the query complexity of this problem. That is, they assume that the
graph G is entirely known in advance and can be accessed for free, and study how many queries
to f are needed in order to estimate its distance to monotonicity. Our focus instead is on massive
inputs, where the graph G cannot be stored and randomly accessed by the algorithm.
   More specifically, we study streaming algorithms for approximating distance to monotonicity:
The edges of G arrive one by one in a stream, and the algorithm has space much smaller than this
input. As standard in the literature of streaming graph algorithms, we allow O(n)
                                                                             e    space1 , which is
enough to receive and store the function f entirely, but is much smaller than the graph G which
could include Ω(n2 ) edges.

1.1        Main Technique: Query Complexity of Maximum Matching Size

The key contribution of our work is to tie the streaming complexity of monotonicity testing to
sublinear time algorithms for estimating the size of maximum matching. The latter problem has
been studied extensively on its own over the last two decades, and has a rich literature with numerous
applications (see Section 1.3). However, to our knowledge this is the first application of this problem
in the streaming setting. Additionally, as we soon discuss, our work motivates the study of the query
complexity of the maximum matching problem in alternative models, where the queries differ from

Approximating maximum matching size using vertex queries. We describe the high-level
approach to proving Theorem 4, which shows how to approximate the size of the maximum matching
in G using subset queries.
   Our starting point is a “peeling”-type algorithm [PR07, OR10, KKS14, CLM+ 20, KMNFT20]
which iteratively matches and removes highest degree vertices. While we can’t implement this

                                                      3
algorithm in our setting, it is useful to first describe it and discuss what goes wrong in implementing
it. In an idealized peeling algorithm, we divide the vertices into two halves randomly. Then
each vertex from the first half “proposes” to a single neighbor chosen uniformly at random and
independently. Then every vertex in the second half that receives at least one proposal “accepts”
one of those arbitrarily and the two vertices get matched together. Now fix a vertex v and suppose
that deg(v) ≥ ∆/2 where ∆ is the current maximum degree of the graph. Under the event that v
is in the second half, it receives a proposal with probability at least 1 − (1 − 1/2∆)∆/2 ≥ 0.1. This
means that after this process, a constant fraction of vertices of degree at least ∆/2 get removed
from the graph. We can therefore afford to remove all vertices of degree at least ∆/2 from the graph
regardless of whether they are matched, since a constant fraction of them have been matched in
expectation. This reduces the maximum degree from ∆ down to ∆/2, so O(log ∆) iterations of this
process results in a constant approximate maximum matching in expectation.
    As discussed, we cannot actually carry out this process in our setting. The main reason is that
each vertex needs to propose to a randomly selected neighbor. Implementing this step requires Θ(n)
neighbor queries, and subset queries are not helpful in reducing the query complexity to poly log(n).
To get around this problem, we implement a different variant of the peeling algorithm that does
not explicitly build a matching, but only provides an estimate of the matching size.
    Our first ingredient is a degree estimation procedure in the subset query model, which we call
a subset degree estimator. The subset degree estimator has the following functionality: Given a
set of active vertices U ⊆ V , for every vertex v ∈ V the procedure
                                                             2  returns a (1 + ε)-multiplicative
approximation to dv := |N (v) ∩ U |. The procedure uses O logε3 n subset queries and succeeds with
probability 1 − 1/ poly(n). Its underlying technique is a sampling argument that uses geometrically
decreasing sampling probabilities.
    To see how the estimator works, fix a vertex v and let dv = |N (v) ∩ U |, noting that the value of d
is unknown to the algorithm. For a sampling probability p, include every vertex of U independently
with probability p to set S and query N (S) using a subset query. The event that v ∈   / N (S) happens
when none of the d neighbors of v is sampled, which happens with probability (1 − p)dv . When
p ≈ 1/2dv , this probability is bounded away from 0 and 1 by a constant, hence by enough repetitions
and a Chernoff bound, one could estimate the value of (1 − p)dv , and thus dv , multiplicatively. By
trying out different values of p between 1/ poly(n) and 1 geometrically, we ensure that for each
vertex v, one of these trials achieves p ≈ 1/2dv . The advantage of this approach is that a single set
of subset queries suffices to estimate |N (v) ∩ U | for all vertices v simultaneously.
    We next modify the standard peeling algorithm so that it constructs an implicit fractional
matching rather than an integral matching. Suppose the current degree threshold is ∆max , and let
U be the set of vertices that have not yet been removed. Let H ⊆ U contain the vertices whose
degree in the induced graph of U is approximately ∆max . For every u ∈ H, we add a small amount
of fractional weight, on the order of ε/∆max , to each edge from u to another vertex in U . Therefore,
the total weight of a vertex (that is the sum of weights on its edges) u ∈ H increases proportionally
to its degree in G[U ], whereas the total weight of a vertex v ∈ U \ H increases proportionally to
the number of its neighbors in H. Vertices are removed from U once their saturation becomes close
to 1. Since every vertex in H has degree comparable to ∆max , its total weight increases by Ω(ε)
in every iteration, and hence all vertices in H are removed after O(1/ε) iterations. We can then
decrease ∆max and repeat the process on the remaining set U .
    The subset degree estimator allows us to implement this fractional peeling process without
storing all the edges of the graph. At the beginning of an iteration, we estimate dU (v) = |N (v) ∩ U |


                                                   4
for all vertices to identify the high degree set H. We then run the estimator again with H as the
input, obtaining estimates of dH (v) = |N (v) ∩ H| for all vertices. The estimates dU (v) and dH (v)
determine the increase in each vertex saturation, so the algorithm only needs to maintain the active
set and one saturation value per vertex.
    Running this fractional process until every vertex is removed would naturally approximate the
maximum fractional matching value. Using only the general 3/2 integrality gap bound for frac-
tional matchings gives a (3 + ε)-approximation to the maximum matching. To obtain a (2 + ε)-
approximation, we use a separate low degree phase. The low degree phase entails additional tech-
nicality that is described in Section 4.2.
    Finally, using our subset degree estimator within the MWU framework of [Liu24], we obtain a
(1 + ε)-approximation to the minimum fractional vertex cover using poly(log n, 1/ε) subset queries.
This gives a (1.5 + ε)-approximation to maximum matching in general graphs, and a (1 + ε)-
approximation for bipartite graphs. This is proved in Section 7.


1.2   Future Directions and Open Problems

Although adjacency-matrix and adjacency-list queries are the standard models in the literature on
sublinear-time and query algorithms, our work highlights the importance of studying two stronger
models: vertex queries and subset queries. For maximum matching, we establish strong separations
between these models and the traditional query models, for which Ω(n) queries are necessary. An
interesting open question is whether similar separations arise for other natural problems.


1.3   Further Related Work

Monotonicity testing. The query complexity of testing whether a function is monotone, namely
distinguishing monotone functions from those that are ε-far from monotone (in the Hamming dis-
tance), is one of the earliest property testing problems to be considered [EKK+ 00, GGL+ 00]. The
tolerant version of the problem, distinguishing functions that are ε-close to monotone, from those
that are ε′ -far from monotone is considered in the work that introduces tolerant testing [PRR06]
(see also [ACCL07]), where it was demonstrated that tolerant testing and the estimating the dis-
tance to having the property are essentially equivalent tasks. Since then, a large body of work has
considered algorithms and lower bounds for testing and tolerant testing of monotonicity over vari-
ous domains and distance measures (examples include [DGL+ 99, FLN+ 02, Fis04, ACCL08, SS10,
BGJ+ 10, BCGM12, BRY14, CS13, CS14, CST14, CDST15, CS16, CWX17, KMS18, BCS18, CS19,
BB15, BB21, LRV22, BCS20, PRW22, BCS23, BKKM23, Jr.23, Jr.24, CDL+ 24, BKR24, CS25,
CDH+ 25, FJ25, Yos26]).

Property testing vs. streaming algorithms. A connection between property testing and
streaming has been explored in [MMPS17, PS18, CFPS20] where it is shown how to transform
constant query property testers to the random-order single-pass streaming model with constant
space. Note that the aforementioned lower bounds demonstrate that in most settings that have
been considered, monotonicity testing requires nonconstant dependence on the domain size of the
function.

Sublinear time algorithms for maximum matching. Sublinear-time matching algorithms
have been a central topic of study and have enjoyed sustained interest in the past few decades


                                                 5
[PR07, NO08, YYI09, ORRR12, LRY17, KMNT20, Beh21, BRRS23, BRR23b, BKS23b, BRR23a,
BRR24, ABRR25, MRT25]. Beyond addressing a fundamental problem in graph theory, they are
deeply connected to many other settings and problems, including spanning trees, Steiner trees, and
the traveling salesman problem [CKK20, CKT23b, CKT23a, BRRS24, MRTV25, MRTV26]. In
addition, they have served as a key tool in dynamic algorithms for estimating the maximum matching
size [Beh23, BKS23a, BKSW24, ABR24], and more recently in [BG24, AKK25] for maintaining an
approximate maximum matching in dynamic graphs, with an update time tied to the density of
Ruzsa-Szemerédi graphs. This work serves as yet another important implication of such algorithms.

2.1   Definitions and Notation

For a graph G = (V, E), n will refer to |V |, and we will denote by µ(G) the size of a maximum
matching in G. We say that an algorithm succeeds with high probability if for any constant c > 0,
the algorithm can be made to succeed with probability at least 1 − n−c . We call an estimate x̂ of a
value x an α-approximation if it satisfies

                                              x/α ≤ x̂ ≤ x,

and an (α, β)-approximation if it satisfies

                                          x/α − β ≤ x̂ ≤ x.



Definition 2.1 (Edge and label stream). Let G = (V, E) be a directed acyclic graph and f : V →
{0, 1} be a boolean function. In an edge and label stream of G and f , edges of G and example-label
pairs (v, f (v)) are revealed in an arbitrary order.
Definition 2.2 (Violation graph). For a DAG G = (V, E) and a boolean function f : V → {0, 1},
the violation graph Gf is the undirected graph which has an edge {u, v} if u can reach v but f (u) >
f (v) (or v can reach u but f (v) > f (u)).


2.2   Standard Facts

Fact 2.3 (Distance to monotonicity and matching in Gf ). For a DAG G = (V, E) and a boolean
function f : V → {0, 1}, we have dmon (f ) = µ(Gf ).
Fact 2.4 (Integrality gap bound for fractional matchings). For every graph G, νf (G) ≤ 23 µ(G),
where νf (G) denotes the maximum size of a fractional matching in G.

4     Implementation With Subset Queries

We have given an algorithm that estimates matching size, given an oracle that provides degree
estimates and an oracle that provides maximal (integral) matchings. In this section, we will modify
this algorithm to work with subset-query access to the graph (Definition 1.2). In Section 4.1 will
straightforwardly implement the ComputeDegrees oracle with subset queries.
    Then, in Section 4.2, we will present a query-efficient alternative to Algorithm 3: instead of
computing global matchings, we give a local computation algorithm (LCA) that computes the output
value xv at a single vertex v by exploring (in expectation) ∆O(1/ε) vertices near v. The algorithm
works by recursively running the LCA for randomized greedy maximal matching, with each level of
recursion representing an iteration of Algorithm 3. This allows us to determine how many times v
is matched and thus how much to increase xv . We use this to sample from the output vector ⃗x in
order to estimate the final matching value.

    We claim that it suffices to prove E[Q(e, 0)] = ∆O(1/ε) for all e. To see this, first note that the
adjacency lists are accessed at most twice in each call to EdgeValue (once for each endpoint, to
obtain all the neighboring edges), and once in each call to VertexValue (to obtain the adjacent
edges). Therefore, it suffices to bound the total number of (recursive) calls to VertexValue
and EdgeValue by n∆O(1/ε) . Furthermore, except the initial calls to VertexValue(v, 1/ε − 1),
every call to VertexValue(·, t) is triggered by a call to EdgeValue(·, t + 1), and each call to
EdgeValue(·, t + 1) is responsible for at most two of the calls to VertexValue(·, t). Therefore,
it suffices to bound the total number of calls just to EdgeValue by n∆O(1/ε) . Additionally, for
t > 0, each call to EdgeValue(e, t) makes a call to EdgeValue(e, t − 1). Hence, since there are
1/ε levels, it suffices to bound the total number of calls made to EdgeValue(·, 0) by n∆O(1/ε) .
Finally, note that there are at most m ≤ n∆ edges. As a result, it suffices to bound the number of
calls to EdgeValue(e, 0) by ∆O(1/ε) for each edge e.

7    Better Approximation Ratios with Subset Queries

In this section, we show that if we restrict our attention to subset queries, then we can outperform
Algorithm 1 in terms of approximation ratio. We remark that Algorithm 1 applies under weaker
guarantees, namely those of the ComputeDegreesθ subroutine in Section 6, and can therefore be
implemented efficiently using either subset or vertex queries. This flexibility comes at the cost of a
larger approximation ratio. In contrast, the algorithm in this section requires stronger guarantees
(as in Assumption 3.1) that are achievable only through subset queries.
