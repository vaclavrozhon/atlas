                                                                                               1. Introduction
                                             Hilbert’s Tenth Problem (H10) for a ring R asks for an algorithm to decide correctly, for each
                                           f ∈ Z[X1 , . . . , Xn ], whether the diophantine equation
                                                                                                 f (X1 , ..., Xn ) = 0
                                           has a solution in R. A stronger form allows polynomials with coeﬃcients in certain recursive sub-
                                           rings of R.
                                              Davis, Putnam, and Robinson, largely working in the 50s and 60s [17], approached H10 by study-
                                           ing diophantine sets: a set A ⊆ Zn is diophantine if there is a polynomial f ∈ Z[X1 , . . . , Xm , Y1 , . . . , Yn ]
                                           such that
                                                                   (a1 , . . . , an ) ∈ A ⇐⇒ ∃x1 . . . xm f (x1 , . . . , xm , a1 , . . . , an ) = 0,
                                           for all a1 , . . . , an ∈ Z. They proved that a negative solution to H10 would follow from the existence
                                           of a diophantine subset of Z of ‘exponential growth’. Moreover they gave a negative solution to
                                           the related ‘exponential’ version of H10 for Z in which f is allowed to be an exponential polyno-
                                           mial. In 1970, Matiyasevich [45] completed the proof of the celebrated ‘DPRM theorem’ by ﬁnding
                                           a diophantine set of exponential growth using the Fibonacci sequence:
                                           Theorem 1.1 (Davis-Putnam-Robinson-Matiyasevich). H10 for Z is unsolvable, i.e. there is no algo-
                                           rithm that decides correctly whether or not each multivariable polynomial has a solution in Z.
                                              Since then, Hilbert’s Tenth Problem has been studied in a wide range of rings, ﬁelds, and asso-
                                           ciated structures, in particular valued ﬁelds. Indeed, the study of H10 in ﬁelds and rings brings
                                           together a variety of results and techniques from model theory, including the theories of local ﬁelds,
                                           due to Tarski [73], Ax–Kochen [2], and Ershov [28], especially uniform aspects of these theories, as
                                           well as deﬁnability in global ﬁelds, ﬁeld arithmetic, and valuation theory.
                                              In the case of function ﬁelds of positive characteristic, continuing a long line of work beginning
                                           with Pheidas [53], Eisentrager and Shlapentokh showed in [27] that H10 – in its stronger form – is
                                           unsolvable if the ﬁeld does not contain the algebraic closure of a ﬁnite ﬁeld; this in particular applies
                                           to global function ﬁelds. For results over function ﬁelds of characteristic zero, see e.g. [38, 39, 24,
                                           25, 61]. In another direction, Rumely showed in [60] that H10 is solvable for the ring of algebraic
                                           integers. For more comprehensive overviews of this subject, the reader is encouraged to consult
                                           [40, 66].

   Perhaps the most important active area of research in this direction is related to H10 for Q, which
is still an open problem. Poonen showed in [54] that, for certain large sets of primes S, H10 over
Z[S –1 ] has a negative answer. Another major breakthrough came through the use of local-global
principles to ﬁnd new families of diophantine subsets of Q. Firstly, in [57] Robinson used the Hasse-
Minkowski theorem, a local-global principle on quadratic forms, to show that every valuation ring
O of a number ﬁeld K is diophantine over K, and that Z is deﬁnable in Q by a ﬁrst-order formula in
the language of rings –see Section 2 for a brief introduction to the terminology of ﬁrst-order logic.
Subsequent steps were made in the work of Eisenträger [23] who used the Albert-Brauer-Hasse-
Noether theorem, a local-global principle for central simple algebras, and of Poonen [55].
   The ideas of Eisenträger and Poonen kickstarted a series of discoveries. More precisely, in [55],
Poonen proved a striking theorem: Z is ‘universal-existentially deﬁnable’ in Q; that is, it is de-
scribed by a ﬁrst-order formula that consists of universal quantiﬁers, followed by exisential quan-
tiﬁers, followed by a quantiﬁer-free formula. Building on this result, Koenigsmann showed in [41]
that Q \ Z is diophantine in Q; Park in [52] in turn generalized this from Q to general number
ﬁelds. In each case, the authors make use of the Hasse-Minkowski local-global principle for qua-
dratic forms. In a new direction, in [18], Dittmann generalized this machinery from quaternion
algebras to general central simple algebras, and replaced the Hasse-Minkowski theorem with the
Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem. This allowed him to show that ‘irreducibility is diophan-
tine’, Por more precisely: for each global ﬁeld K and each n ∈ N, the set {(a0 , . . . , an–1 ) ∈ K n |
X n + i<n ai X i is irreducible in K[X ]} is diophantine over K. Further use has been made of the
approach using central simple algebras, for example by Daans [13, 15] and Anscombe–Dittmann–
Fehm [1].
   The above shows this is a growing research line of rich interaction between logic and model
theory, and algebra and number theory. Moreover, these results suggest natural extensions and
generalizations in several directions, which we explore in the last section of this survey: on the one
hand, the diophantine sets studied in these papers and the local-global behaviour they exhibit can ﬁt
into a framework of diophantine families. On the other hand, one may replace the Hasse-Minkowski
and Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorems with other local-global principles arising naturally in
arithmetic geometry, and obtain diﬀerent diophantine sets.
   In this article, we survey the stream of work following [57, 23, 55] in which local-global princi-
ples are used to great eﬀect. After a concise introduction to the topic, we survey how the Hasse-
Minkowski and Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorems have been used to prove striking new deﬁn-
ability results in the above-mentioned works of Eisenträger, Poonen, Koenigsman, Park, Dittmann
and Daans. In particular, in Subsection 5.1 we compare the techniques and results of these papers,
in which the authors have been concerned with similar questions. We hope that such an analysis
will be useful for future uses of local-global principles in this study of deﬁnability in number theory,
as we discuss in Subsections 5.2 and 5.3.

   Roughly speaking, we say a local-global principle holds when a property is true over a global ﬁeld
if and only if it is true over all, or all but ﬁnitely many, completions of that global ﬁeld. One of the
main reasons why local-global principles can be useful in the study of arithmetic properties of global
objects is that, typically, the analogous properties over complete local ﬁelds are more accessible.
The above-mentioned Hasse-Minkowski and Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorems are concerned
with the property of a quadratic form being isotropic, resp. of a central simple algebra splitting (or
equivalently, a Brauer group element being trivial).
   In arithmetic geometry one is concerned with rational points and arithmetic invariants of al-
gebraic varieties and may study local-global principles for the existence of rational points (see
e.g. [37, 33, 51]) and for the behaviour of arithmetic invariants such as endomorphisms and au-
tomorphisms (see e.g. [72], [74]).
   There are many other contexts where local-global questions can be considered. Indeed, local-
global principles are studied also in related research areas like arithmetic dynamics (see e.g. [35, 3]),
and recently, in arithmetic geometry over a base ﬁeld which is not a global ﬁeld but for instance
                                                    2
a function ﬁeld over a number ﬁeld [36], or a function ﬁeld over a discretely valued ﬁeld [6, 7, 8].
In the literature there are also examples of diﬀerent kinds of local-global questions, not formulated
in the setting of ﬁelds at all. In those cases only the basic idea of studying properties in relation to
behaviour at primes is borrowed from the classical local-global setup. For example, in [71] the local
behaviour of polynomial rings R[X ], where R is a commutative ring, is considered over its localiza-
tions at maximal ideals. In [43] some properties of the characters of a group are encoded by those
of the normalizers of its p-Sylow subgroups. However, as stated above, in the present paper we will
consider the classical local-global background of a global ﬁeld and its completions.

   This survey is intended for a broad audience, so we do not assume a detailed background in either
logic or number theory. Accordingly, its structure is as follows: in Section 2 we brieﬂy introduce
the necessary tools and terminology from logic, and likewise in Section 3 we introduce the alge-
braic tools, in particular quaternion algebras and central simple algebras. We deﬁne the notion of a
local-global principle and give the Hasse-Minkowski and Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorems as
two important examples. In Section 4 we survey the main results and methods contained in Robin-
son’s seminal work and the above-mentioned papers [23, 55, 41, 52, 18, 13], in which the authors
adopt similar methods of proof, in particular by making use of the Hasse-Minkowski and Albert-
Brauer-Hasse-Noether local-global principles. Finally, in Section 5, we make comparisons between
the surveyed articles, in order to establish a common model-theoretic framework. We explore two
further directions: on the one hand, we describe one possible model-theoretic viewpoint of dio-
phantine sets satisfying some local-global principle; while on the other hand, we propose two other
local-global principles from arithmetic geometry that could be used to study diophantine sets.

                                2. Prereqisites from model theory
  We begin by introducing a few basic concepts from ﬁrst-order logic.
2.1. First-order logic and deﬁnable sets. We give a brief introduction to the basic tools of math-
ematical logic: formulas, structures, and deﬁnable sets. Generally speaking, logic is the study of
valid reasoning.
   In the same spirit, the sub-discipline of
   mathematical logic is the study of valid symbolic reasoning within formal languages, and it in-
cludes applications to theories drawn from mathematics. Prominent examples of formal languages
are the so-called ﬁrst-order languages, in which a signiﬁcant proportion of mathematics can be
formalized. These languages are of particular importance in model theory, which studies the re-
lationship between ﬁrst-order theories and the mathematical structures that model them. In this
survey we will restrict most of our discussion to the ﬁrst-order language of rings.
   For more information, especially for a more general perspective on model theory, readers are
encouraged to consult [34, 44].
   The ﬁrst-order language of rings, denoted Lring , is constructed in several stages, as follows. We
begin with the symbols of the language, which are of two types: the logical and the non-logical.
   The logical symbols are
     — connectives: ∧ for conjunction, ∨ for disjunction, ¬ for negation, → for implication, and ↔
        for biconditional,
     — variables: x1 , x2 , x3 , . . . , y, z, . . .,
     — quantiﬁers: ∀ for the universal quantiﬁer "for all" and ∃ for the existential quantiﬁer "there
        exists",
     — punctuation: = for equality, and the parentheses ( ).
   The non-logical symbols are the function symbols +, ·, and –, and the constant symbols 0, and 1. All
these symbols are combined according to the following recursive construction to form two families:
the terms and the ﬁrst-order formulas. The terms are the smallest collection of ﬁnite strings in the
above symbols containing all the variables, the constant symbols 0 and 1, and such that if t1 and t2
are terms then (t1 + t2 ), (t1 · t2 ), and (t1 – t2 ) are terms. The (ﬁrst-order) formulas are the smallest
                                                     3
collection of ﬁnite strings in the above symbols containing t1 = t2 , for all terms t1 , t2 , and also
containing (¬ϕ), (ϕ ∧ ψ), (ϕ ∨ ψ), (ϕ → ψ), (ϕ ↔ ψ), (∀x) ϕ, and (∃x) ϕ, for all formulas ϕ, ψ and
each variable x. Informally, a ﬁrst-order formula is a well-formed ‘ﬁnitary’ mathematical statement,
where quantiﬁers may range over elements (as opposed to sets or other ‘higher order’ objects).
  Examples of ﬁrst-order formulas in Lring are:
(2.1)                              ((1 + 1) + 1) = 0,
(2.2)                              (∀x)(x · y) = (y · x),
(2.3)                              (∀x)(∃y)(y · y) = x,
(2.4)                              (∀x)(((x · x) + (y · y)) = 1 → (¬(x · y) = 0))
For readability we may make various informal simpliﬁcations. For example, Equation (2.2) may be
written as (∀x)x · y = y · x, and (2.3) may be written as (∀x)(∃y)y · y = x without ambiguity. In
each of the examples (2.2)–(2.4) above, the variable x is in the scope of a quantiﬁer: in other words,
x is bound by that quantiﬁer. If a variable is not bound by any quantiﬁer, it is free. For example, y is
free in (2.2) and (2.4). We write ϕ(x1 , . . . , xn ) for ϕ to signify that the free variables of ϕ are among
x1 , . . . , xn .
   An Lring -structure (R, +R , ·R , –R , 0R , 1R ) is a set R together with with binary operations +R , ·R ,
and –R on R, and constants 0R , 1R ∈ R. Thus every ring is naturally an Lring -structure, as we would
hope, but there are plenty of Lring -structures that are not rings. When working only with rings,
further notational simpliﬁcations are possible, for example (2.1) may be written as 1 + 1 + 1 = 0
and (2.4) as ∀x (x 2 + y 2 = 1 → xy 6= 0). Moreover we usually write an Lring -structure simply as
e.g. (R, +, ·, –, 0, 1), without distinguishing between the structure and the language, when there is no
risk of ambiguity. We may even write simply R for (R, +, ·, –, 0, 1) when the operations and constants
are clear from the context.
   We deﬁne a notion of ‘truth’: given a ring (R, +, ·, –, 0, 1) and elements a1 , . . . , an ∈ R, each formula
ϕ(x1 , . . . , xn ) may be interpreted by substituting each element ai into the place of its corresponding
variable xi . Then the truth of ϕ(a1 , . . . , an ) in R may be determined from (really, deﬁned by) the usual
mathematical meanings of ‘and’, ‘or’, ‘implies’, etc., and the universal and existential quantiﬁers.
We write R |= ϕ(a1 , . . . , an ) to mean that ϕ(a1 , . . . , an ) is true in R. For example, the formula ϕ(y)
appearing in (2.2) has one free variable, namely y; and for a ring R and an element b ∈ R, ϕ(b) is
true in R (i.e., R |= ϕ(b)) if and only if b commutes multiplicatively with each element of R. For a
full deﬁnition of interpretation, and of the relation |=, see for instance [44, Deﬁnition 1.1.6].
   A sentence is a formula with no free variables. The truth of a sentence ϕ in a structure R does
not depend on any elements ai . Thus, in a given structure, each sentence is either true or false, and
is not both. For example, Equations (2.1) and (2.3) are sentences. By contrast, a ﬁrst-order formula
ϕ(x1 , . . . , xn ) with free variables x1 , . . . , xn deﬁnes the set of tuples (a1 , . . . , an ) such that ϕ is true
when each xi is replaced by ai . More broadly: A set A ⊆ Rn is deﬁnable if there is an Lring -formula
ϕ(x1 , . . . , xn , z1 , . . . , zl ) and there are parameters c1 , . . . , cl ∈ R such that for all (a1 , . . . , an ) ∈ Rn
                              (a1 , . . . , an ) ∈ A ⇐⇒ R |= ϕ(a1 , . . . , an , c1 , . . . , cl ),
i.e. if and only if ϕ(a1 , . . . , an , c1 , . . . , cl ) is true in R.
   There are several special kinds of deﬁnability, distinguished by the syntactic complexity of the
deﬁning formula, as follows. A formula ϕ is
       • quantiﬁer-free if it contains no quantiﬁers;
       • diophantine if it consists of existential quantiﬁers followed by a single polynomial equality
         with integer coeﬃcients; f (x1 , . . . , xn , y1 , . . . , ym , z1 , . . . , zl ) = 0;
       • existential if it consists of existential quantiﬁers followed by a quantiﬁer-free formula;
       • universal if it consists of universal quantiﬁers followed by a quantiﬁer-free formula;
       • universal-existential if it consists of universal quantiﬁers followed by an existential formula.
For example, the formula (2.1) is quantiﬁer-free, whereas the formulas (2.2) and (2.4) are universal,
and the formula (2.3) is universal-existential.

   In the study of H10, we are mostly concerned with diophantine and existential formulas and
the sets they deﬁne. Straight from the deﬁnition, every diophantine formula is existential. Given
a ring R and a subset A ⊆ Rn , we say A is existentially deﬁnable (respectively, diophantine) if it is
deﬁned by an existential (respectively, diophantine) formula. Likewise, A is universally (respectively,
universal-existentially) deﬁnable if it is deﬁned by a universal (resp., universal-existential) formula.
Existentially deﬁnable sets are preserved in extensions: if R ⊆ S is an extension of rings, and A ⊆ Rn
and B ⊆ S n are both deﬁned by the same existential formula, then A ⊆ B [34, Theorem 2.4.1].
Moreover, one repeatedly uses that ﬁnite intersections and ﬁnite unions of diophantine sets are
diophantine.
Remark 2.1. We might also consider a deﬁnition of ‘deﬁnability’ for sets A of a ring R that requires
that the parameters c1 , . . . , cl are elements of a given subring C ⊆ R. We then say that A is deﬁnable
over C, and likewise for ‘existentially deﬁnable’, etc. In the majority of this article we make no such
restriction, and for clarity we often write e.g. ‘A is diophantine over R’ to remind the reader that
both A is deﬁnable in the structure R and that the parameters may be any elements of R.
Remark 2.2. In many of the rings and ﬁelds we consider, a set is diophantine if and only if it is
existentially deﬁnable. More precisely, there are several standard methods that may be used to ﬁnd

2.2. Theories. Remaining in the language of rings, a theory is simply a set of sentences. Accord-
ingly, for a ring R, we deﬁne the theory of R, denoted Th(R), to be the set of all Lring -sentences that
hold in R. Similarly the existential theory of R, denoted Th∃ (R), is the set of existential Lring -sentences
that are true in R. A theory T axiomatizes the class of its models, i.e., those Lring -structures R such
that R |= ϕ for every ϕ ∈ T . For example the class of ﬁelds is axiomatized by the usual axioms for
ﬁelds: associativity and commutativity of additions, etc., including the axiom
                                               (∀x)(∃y) (x = 0 ∨ xy = 1)
that describes the existence of multiplicative inverses.
   Given a theory T , naturally we ask ourselves if there is an algorithm (i.e., a Turing machine) that
correctly determines whether or not any given sentence in the language of rings is true in all models
of T . When there is such an algorithm, we say that T is decidable; otherwise it is undecidable. For
example, as a consequence of Gödel’s famous First Incompleteness theorem, the theory Th(Z) of
the ring Z is undecidable. In particular, Peano’s axioms are incomplete. This is in contrast with the
situation for R and C whose complete theories are decidable and admit recursive axiomatizations,
due to Tarski [73], and for Qp , due to Ax–Kochen [2], and independently by Ershov [28].

                                                               6
3.1. Quaternions and Central Simple Algebras. Throughout, we let K be a ﬁeld; we will as-
sume for simplicity that char(K) 6= 2. All K-algebras are assumed to be associative and to have a
multiplicative identity. Moreover we consider only K-algebras of ﬁnite dimension.
Deﬁnition 3.1. A K-algebra A is simple if it has no non-trivial two-sided ideals. It is a central simple
algebra if moreover its centre is K.
   The dimension of a central simple algebra A/K is a square integer, and its positive square root
is called its degree. A central simple algebra A/K is said to be split if it is isomorphic to a matrix
algebra Mn (K) for some n. Otherwise we call it non-split. For any ﬁeld extension L/K, we may
consider A ⊗K L, which is a central simple L-algebra. For K a global ﬁeld and p a place of K, we will
want to consider the Kp -algebra A ⊗K Kp , where Kp denotes the completion of K with respect to p.
The set ∆A/K of places p of K at which A ⊗K Kp is non-split is always ﬁnite (we will also denote it
simply by ∆A , when K is clear from the context).
Deﬁnition 3.2. For a, b ∈ K × , we denote by Ha,b the quaternion algebra over K with generators i, j
satisfying i2 = a, j 2 = b, and ij = –ji. Thus,
                                         
                                 Ha,b = w + xi + yj + zij w, x, y, z ∈ K .
   Every four-dimensional central simple algebra over K is isomorphic to Ha,b for some a, b ∈ K ×
[4, Proposition 1]. Every quaternion algebra Ha,b is either split, so isomorphic to the matrix algebra
M2 (K); or it is a division algebra, in which case it is non-split. For an extension L/K, we denote
Ha,b (L) := Ha,b ⊗K L. For a global ﬁeld K and a place p of K, we say Ha,b is split at p if Ha,b (Kp ) ≃
M2 (Kp ), and is ramiﬁed at p otherwise.
Deﬁnition 3.3. (a) The reduced trace of an element α = w+xi+yj+zij of Ha,b is deﬁned as tr(α) = 2w.
(b) Its reduced norm is deﬁned as nr(α) = w 2 – ax 2 – by 2 + abz2 .
   Any central simple algebra A/K has reduced trace and norm maps, deﬁned on A with values in K
(cf. [31, §2.6]), which we will also denote by tr and nr, respectively. The reduced trace is K-linear
and the reduced norm is multiplicative on A× .
   Let A be a degree n central simple K-algebra. For α ∈ A, let Pα (X ) = X n + an–1 X n–1 + · · · + a0 be
the reduced characteristic polynomial of α over K. Then tr(α) = an–1 and nr(α) = (–1)n a0 .
Deﬁnition 3.4. We say that two central simple algebras A and A′ over K are Brauer equivalent if
there exist integers m, m′ > 0 such that
                                    A ⊗K Mm (K) ≃ A′ ⊗K Mm′ (K).
This deﬁnes an equivalence relation on the set of central simple algebras over K, equipped with the
tensor product. The set of equivalence classes is an abelian group denoted by Br(K), see [9] and [70]
for more details. Finally, the class of a central simple algebra A/K is trivial in Br(K) if and only A is
split.
3.2. Local-global principles. Let K be a global ﬁeld and p a place of K. Let MK be the set of all
places of K. A quadratic form F (X1, ..., Xn ) ∈ K[X1 , ..., Xn ] is isotropic over K if the equation F = 0
has a nontrivial solution in K.
Theorem 3.5. [42, Theorem VI.3.1] Let F (X1, ..., Xn ) ∈ K[X1 , ..., Xn ] be a quadratic form, where K is
a global ﬁeld. If F is isotropic over Kp , for all completions Kp of K, then F is isotropic over K.
   This result was proved by Minkowski in 1923 in the case when K = Q and by Hasse in 1924 in
the case when K is a number ﬁeld. Since then many mathematicians have been inspired to study
similar so-called local-global problems, i.e., problems in which the validity of a certain property is
assumed to hold in all, or all but ﬁnitely many, completions of a global ﬁeld K and one asks if the
same property holds in K. When the answer to a local-global problem is aﬃrmative one says that a
local-global principle or a Hasse principle holds.

they are formulated. For instance, if in Theorem 3.5 we take F = X 2 – a and K = Q, then we get

   In the previous section, we surveyed a number of recent articles that use local-global principles to
answer model-theoretic questions. In essence, the surveyed articles introduce similar notions of sets

using local-global principles to construct global algebras with suitable properties. In this section,
we make some more detailed comparisons between the methods employed in the surveyed articles
in Subsection 5.1, give a more model-theoretic outlook in Subsection 5.2, and sketch two possible
applications of this framework using other local-global principles in Subsection 5.3.

Question 5.1. Under what conditions on K, and relative to what information, can one axiomatize
the L-theory of K?
   For number ﬁelds this problem seems to be extremely hard, since even to describe D amounts
to deciding which varieties admit some form of local-global principle. On the other hand, pseudo-
algebraically closed ﬁelds, pseudo-real closed ﬁelds, pseudo-p-adically closed ﬁelds, and others, sat-
isfy local-global principles for various classes of varieties: the complete Lring -theories of such ﬁelds
are already understood and there is a large literature. For example see [30, Chapter 12], [56], and
[29]. In such cases, it seems reasonable to expect that the L-theory of K can be axiomatized in a
similar way. Perhaps it is possible to ﬁnd a ﬁeld K – outside of the classes mentioned previously –
for which we can axiomatize K, and in so doing we might ﬁnd new classes of ﬁelds of wider model
theoretic interest. Further study of these structures K may well shed light on the possibilities and
limitations of the local-global method in the analysis of diophantine sets in global ﬁelds.

5.3. Other local-global principles. As described in Subsection 4.4, two local-global principles
were used repeatedly in the surveyed articles to obtain diophantine sets; namely, the Hasse-Minkowski
and Albert-Brauer-Hasse-Noether theorems, cf. Theorem 3.5 and Theorem 3.6, respectively. In this
subsection, we put forward two other local-global principles, both arising from the study of ellip-
tic curves, which can potentially be used in a similar way to obtain other diophantine sets. For a
comprehensive introduction to the arithmetic of elliptic curves, cf. e.g. [5, 69].
   Elliptic curves have already been used in the context of Hilbert’s Tenth problem for global ﬁelds.
For example, see [10], where the authors use a conjecture on the properties of elliptic divisibility
sequences on an elliptic curve to deduce some results on the complexity of deﬁning Z in Q; see also
[67, 68, 46].

5.3.1. Endomorphism rings of elliptic curves. Let K be any ﬁeld and consider elliptic curves over K.
   An isogeny between elliptic curves E1 , E2 is a K-morphism E1 → E2 of algebraic groups. Any
nonzero isogeny is surjective and hence has a ﬁnite kernel. The set HomK (E1 , E2 ) of isogenies from
E1 to E2 inherits a group structure from the group structure on E2 , by pointwise addition: (ϕ+ψ)(P) :=
ϕ(P) + ψ(P). We will denote the inverse of ϕ in this group by –ϕ. There is a natural action of Z on
EndK (E1 , E2 ) given by (n, ϕ) 7→ n · ϕ, where n · ϕ means the sum of |n| copies of sgn(n)ϕ, making
EndK (E1 , E2 ) a Z-module.
   For an elliptic curve E/K, its endomorphism ring EndK (E) := HomK (E, E) has an additional multi-
plication structure from composing isogenies. It has no zero divisors and is a torsion-free Z-module.
Moreover, it is a Z-order in the endomorphism algebra End0K (E) := EndK (E) ⊗Z Q, which means it
is a lattice (i.e., a ﬁnitely generated Z-module whose scalar extension to Q spans End0K (E)) that is
also a subring. As a consequence, one shows that the endomorphism ring EndK (E) is either Z, an
order in a quadratic imaginary ﬁeld, or an order in a quaternion algebra. The latter moreover only
occurs when char(K) > 0 (since End0K (E) is commutative when char(K) = 0); we call the elliptic
curve supersingular in this case. See Sections 10.1 and 10.2 of [75] for an introduction to lattices and
orders, respectively.
   We may localize any Z-lattice L in a Q-vector space V at any prime ℓ by forming Lℓ := L ⊗Z Zℓ ,
which is a Zℓ -lattice in the Qℓ -vector space V ⊗Q Qℓ . For any prime ℓ, we have a natural injection of L
into Lℓ . Lattices over Z (or any Dedekind domain) satisfy a local-global principle [75, Theorem 9.4.9
and Lemma 9.5.3]: given L, the map N 7→ (Nℓ )ℓ–prime determines a bijection between Z-lattices N
in V and the collection of tuples (Nℓ )ℓ of Zℓ -lattices in V ⊗Q Qℓ satisfying Lℓ = Nℓ for all but ﬁnitely
many primes ℓ. We note here that the choice of the lattice L is irrelevant, as for any other such L′ ,
                                                    18
one has Lℓ = L′ℓ for all but ﬁnitely many primes ℓ, so there is no “real" dependence on L; see also the
comment and remark after [75, Theorem 9.4.9].
   Similarly, we may localize a Z-order O in a Q-algebra R at any prime ℓ by forming Oℓ := O ⊗Z Zℓ ,
which is now a Zℓ -order in the Qℓ -algebra R ⊗Q Qℓ ; again, for any prime ℓ the order O naturally
embeds into Oℓ . Moreover, for Z-lattices, being an order is a local property, i.e., checking that a
given lattice is an order can be done locally at each prime. Hence, the lobal-global principle for
Z-lattices above specializes to a local-global principle for Z-orders in R.
   For ℓ 6= char(K) a prime, the ℓ-adic Tate module (or ℓ-divisible group) of an elliptic curve E/K is
Tℓ (E) := limn E[ℓn ], where the inverse limit is taken with respect to the natural multiplication-by-ℓ
          ←−
maps. The Galois group Gal(K̄/K) acts on each E[ℓn ] in a way which respects the inverse limit,
and hence extends to the Tate module Tℓ (E); cf. [69, III.7]. Due to Tate’s theorem, localizations
of endomorphism rings, viewed as orders in their respective endomorphism algebras, have an in-
terpretation in terms of Tate modules. More precisely, for any two elliptic curves E1 , E2 , there is
a natural injective map HomK (E1 , E2 ) ⊗Z Zℓ ֒→ Hom(Tℓ (E1 ), Tℓ (E2 )), where the right hand side
consists of Gal(K̄/K)-equivariant morphisms of Zℓ -modules (cf. Deﬁnition 34.1.60 of [75]). Tate’s
theorem shows that this map is an isomorphism when K is a ﬁnite ﬁeld; Faltings showed the same
when K is a number ﬁeld.
   When char(K) =: p > 0 and ℓ = p, instead of the Tate module of E one takes its (contravariant)
Dieudonné module M(E), or (categorically) equivalently, its associated p-divisible group; cf. [76, §1.2]
for both of these notions. When K is moreover ﬁnite, Tate also showed that HomK (E1 , E2 ) ⊗Z Zp is
isomorphic to Hom(M(E2 ), M(E1 )).

   Putting the above together, for an elliptic curve E over a ﬁnite or number ﬁeld K we have an exact
sequence

(5.2)                   0 → EndK (E) →         EndK (E) ⊗ Zℓ ≃       End(Tℓ (E)),

where the product runs over all primes ℓ, including possibly ℓ = char(K), though in that case
Tℓ (E) := M(E) is the Dieudonné module. The image of the second map lands in the restricted product
where all but ﬁnitely many entries are the identity map.

   Inspired by the use of local-global principles for quaternion algebras in the surveyed articles, we
aim to use the local-global behavior of endomorphism rings of elliptic curves over ﬁnite ﬁelds and
number ﬁelds mentioned above to obtain diophantine sets. To do this, it is necessary to ﬁrst deter-
mine the next term in the exact sequence (5.2), since the above-mentioned restricted product is hard
to work with. It should be noted that while endomorphism rings are orders that have reduced trace
and norm maps, the exact sequence (5.2) is a map of elements of orders, so a suitable replacement for
trace and norm maps is needed in order to deﬁne the analogues of the sets S and T from the articles
we surveyed in Section 4.
5.3.2. Torsion points on elliptic curves. In this subsection, we let K be a number ﬁeld. As in the
previous sections, we denote by MK the set of all places of K.
   Let A be a commutative connected algebraic group deﬁned over K. In [20], the authors propose a
local-to-global approach to study the divisibility of K-points of A by integers. More precisely, they
ask the following:
Question 5.2. Let m ∈ N and P ∈ A(K). Suppose that for all but ﬁnitely many p ∈ MK , there exists
Qp ∈ A(Kp ) such that P = mQp in A(Kp ). Does this imply there exists Q ∈ A(K) such that P = mQ
in A(K)?
  If the answer to this question is positive, we say that A satisﬁes a local-global principle for m-
divisibility. One can quickly reduce to the case where m = pn for p a prime number and n ∈ N. There
have been many recent advances in the study of this question; for more details the interested reader
can consult the recent survey [19]. One of the main tools is to translate the question into a group
                                                   19
cohomology problem, where the obstruction to the local-global principle for m-divisibility is given
by a subgroup of H 1 (Gal(K(A[m])/K), A[m]), denoted by Hloc    1 (Gal(K(A[m])/K), A[m]), considered in
[20]. Similar groups were introduced before by Tate (as stated by Serre in [63]) and Sansuc [62]. The
group H 1 (Gal(K(A[m])/K), A[m]) contains a subgroup which is isomorphic to the Tate-Shafarevich
group X(K, A[pn ]) (see [19] for further details). The vanishing of Hloc 1 (Gal(K(A[m])/K), A[m]) pro-
vides a suﬃcient condition for the local-global principle to hold [20, Proposition 2.1]. There are also
known examples where it does not hold [21, 22, 48].
   In the case where A = E is an elliptic curve, it was shown in [20, Theorem 3.1] that the local-global
principle for m-divisibility holds for m = p a prime number. Later in [49], the authors proved this

                      n
for all powers m = p of every prime number p > 3 2 + 1 . The bound has been extended to
p ≥ 5 when K = Q in [50] and this is best possible because of counterexamples produced for powers
of p = 2, 3 in [48, 12]. In addition, in the special case when P is a torsion point of an elliptic curve E
deﬁned over a number ﬁeld K, it was shown in [32] that the local-global principle for pn -divisibility
holds for all p ≥ 3 and n ≥ 1.

   which holds whenever E is a commutative algebraic group and in particular when E is an elliptic
curve.
   Taking the articles surveyed in Section 4 as motivation, we propose to use the local-global prin-
ciple for pn -divisibility, through the exact sequence (5.3), for the purpose of proving that certain
sets, naturally occurring in arithmetic, are diophantine. In particular, we would like to work in a
setting where the local-global principles holds, so that X(K, E[pn ]) vanishes. A ﬁrst crucial step
would then be to understand the image of the map π in (5.3), which would allow us to determine its
cokernel and hence complete the sequence to a short exact sequence. It seems however that, so far,
the cokernel of π is not well understood.
   Another important point is to choose a suitable interpretation of H 1 (K, E[pn ]) and hence suitable
presentations of its elements, e.g. as pn -coverings of E, or E[n]-torsors, or theta groups (cf. [11, §1]).
This choice will determine the context in which the analogues of the sets S and T need to be deﬁned
and hence in which way the model-theoretic arguments can be adapted and extended.
