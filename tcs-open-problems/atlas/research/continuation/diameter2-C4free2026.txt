                                                                                   Abstract
                                                     We show that list 3-coloring a C4 -free graph of diameter-2 can be done in
                                                 polynomial-time. Our algorithm is based on a structural characterization show-
                                                 ing that many such graphs are not 3-colorable. In particular, we show that C4 -
                                                 free graphs of diameter-2 without universal vertices, where the maximum degree
                                                 is at least 17, are not 3-colorable.


                                          1      Introduction
                                          For an integer k ≥ 1, we write [k] = {1, 2, . . . , k}. Given is a finite simple unweighted
                                          connected graph G = (V, E). A map c : V → [k] for an integer k is called a k-coloring
                                          if c(u) ̸= c(v) for all edges uv ∈ E. In such cases, we say that G is k-colorable. The
                                          smallest k for which G admits a k-coloring is called the chromatic number of G.

                                             k-Coloring
                                          Input:    A graph G = (V, E).
                                          Question: Is G k-colorable?

                                              One can also consider a more general variant called list k-coloring. Each vertex v
                                          is given a list L(v) ⊆ [k] of colors it may have; if there exists a k-coloring c : V → [k]
                                          where c(v) ∈ L(v), then we say that the graph is list k-colorable. Note that list
                                          k-colorable graphs are necessarily k-colorable, but not vice versa.

                                             List k-Coloring
                                          Input:    A graph G = (V, E) and a list L : V → 2[k] .
                                          Question: Is G list k-colorable?

                                              2-Coloring is equivalent to showing that a graph is bipartite. k-Coloring, and
                                          therefore List k-Coloring, on the other hand, is NP-complete for all k ≥ 3 [11].
                                          Since, much attention has been devoted to solving the problems on restricted graphs,
                                          such as those with forbidden (induced) subgraphs [10, 2], those on bounded diameter
                                          [15, 12], or a combination thereof [14, 9]. There are numerous thorough surveys on
                                          the topic [17, 16, 5]. We study list 3-coloring on bounded diameter.

Most of these results follow a similar recipe. Color a subset of the vertices and prop-

on their neighbours from the list. Check that the remaining vertices must each have
a list of size at most 2; as 2-List-Coloring2 can be solved in polynomial-time [4],
it remains to show that finding the first suitable pre-coloring takes polynomial-time.

Our contribution. We take a structural approach and show that most C4 -free
graphs of diameter-2 are not 3-colorable.

Theorem 1.1. List 3-Coloring C4 -free Diameter-2 is polynomial-time solvable.
    To show Theorem 1.1, we show that most C4 -free graphs of diameter-2 are not 3-
colorable if the maximum degree is at least 17. Determining the maximum degree
of a graph can be done in polynomial-time. The list 3-colorability of the remaining
finitely many graphs can be hardcoded into a lookup table.

assuming N2 ̸= ∅, we show that vertices in N2 can be partitioned based on their
unique adjacent vertex in N1 . There are ∆ parts in the partition; we show that each
part contains the same number of elements, which is either ∆ − 2 or ∆ − 1. We also

2    Preliminaries
Let G = (V, E) be a graph. Let u, v ∈ V . We say that u covers v via adjacency
if uv ∈ E. We say that a vertex u covers v via a proxy w (or just covers v via
proxy if the context is clear) if there exists a vertex w such that uw, wv ∈ E. The
degree of u, denoted degG (u), is the number of edges incident with u. We remove the
subscript and write deg(u) if the reference graph is clear. The minimum degree of G
is δ(G) = minv deg(v); the maximum degree of G is ∆(G) = maxv deg(v).
     We consider unweighted graphs. The length of a path is the number of edges it
contains. The distance between two vertices is the number of edges in a shortest
path between them. The diameter of G, written diam(G) is the maximum distance
between any pair of its vertices. Note that in a graph of diameter-2, u covers v via
adjacency or via proxy, or both.
     We write NiG (v) = {u : d(u, v) = i} to denote the set of vertices which are
distance i away from v. We drop the superscript if there is no ambiguity in the
reference graph. Let u, v ∈ V . We merge u and v by replacing them with a new
vertex w, where N1 (w) = N1 (u) ∪ N1 (v).
     Let S ⊆ V . The induced subgraph G[S] is the graph whose vertex set is S and
whose edge set consists of all elements in E with both endpoints in S. We say that G
is induced (H1 , H2 , . . . , Hp )-free if G does not contain an induced subgraph isomorphic
to Hi for all i ∈ [p]. We say that G is H-free if G does not contain a subgraph that
is isomorphic to H.
     In this paper, we consider C4 -free graphs. These are induced (C4 , D4 , K4 )-free
graphs, where K4 is the complete graph on 4 vertices and D4 is a diamond graph,
a graph on four vertices and five edges (so a K4 without an edge). Since graphs
containing K4 as a subgraph are not 3-colorable, and since identifying a K4 takes
polynomial-time, one can also restrict to induced (C4 , D4 )-graphs. Nevertheless, for
ease of notation, we formulate our results for C4 -free graphs. Note that there is
a so-called diamond elimination rule [15]. It is not immediately clear if this rule
preserves induced C4 -freeness. Thus, we have decided to also exclude D4 as an induced
subgraph. See Section 7 for a discussion on the matter.
     The following lemma is in the spirit of Observation 1 in [15].
Lemma 2.1. Let G = (V, E) be a C4 -free graph. If G has more than two vertices,
then for every u ∈ V , the maximum degree of G[N1 (u)] is 1.
Proof. If there were a vertex y in G[N1 (u)] of degree-2 with neighbours x and z,
then uxyz is a C4 , which is prohibited.

3     Characterizing C4 -free graphs of diameter-2
We introduce notation which will be used in the rest of this paper. We write G =
(V, E) to mean a C4 -free graph of diameter-2. We let ∆ denote the maximum degree
of G, and let v0 be a vertex with degree ∆. We call v0 a root. If ∆ = 2, then G must
be isomorphic to C5 , which is 3-colorable. We assume ∆ ≥ 3. Let N1 = N1 (v) =
{x1 , x2 , . . . , x∆ } be the set of neighbours of v0 , and let N2 = N2 (v).
    A vertex that is adjacent to every other vertex is called a universal vertex. List
3-Coloring C4 -free Diameter-2 is polynomial-time solvable if there exists a uni-
versal vertex.

Lemma 3.1. If N2 = ∅, then List 3-Coloring C4 -free Diameter-2 is polynomial-
time solvable.
Proof. If there exists a proper list 3-coloring, there exists one where v0 is given one of
at most three colors in L(v0 ). Each guess returns an instance of list-2 coloring, which
can be solved in polynomial-time [4].

    So we assume henceforth that N2 is non-empty. See Figure 1 for all C4 -free graphs
of diameter-2 when ∆ ≤ 3.

Proof. Let u ∈ N2 . The vertex u must cover v0 via proxy, so it must have at least

Lemma 3.2, uy ∈ E where y ∈ N1 . If xy ∈/ E, then u must cover x via a proxy w ∈ N2 ,
i.e., uw, wx ∈ E. So x has a neighbour in N2 .

Lemma 3.4. Suppose xi and xj are non-adjacent. Then Vi and Vj are adjacent, and
the edges between Vi and Vj form a perfect matching. In particular, |Vi | = |Vj |.

Proof. As xi xj ∈ / E, every vertex in Vi must cover xj via a proxy in Vj . Similarly,
every vertex in Vj must cover xi via a proxy in Vi . Two vertices in Vi cannot have
a common neighbour apart from xi , as that would result in a C4 . So |Vi | ≤ |Vj |.
Similarly, |Vj | ≤ |Vi |. We obtain our perfect matching and the claim follows.
   Note also that if Vi and Vj are non-adjacent, then xi and xj must be adjacent.

Lemma 3.5. Vi is adjacent to at least ∆ − 2 parts for all i ∈ [∆].
Proof. By Lemma 2.1, xi has at most one neighbour in N1 , and so xi is non-adjacent
to at least ∆ − 2 vertices in N1 . By Lemma 3.4, Vi is adjacent to at least ∆ − 2
parts.

Lemma 3.6. The canonical partition is balanced, i.e., |V1 | = |V2 | = · · · = |V∆ |.
Proof. Let xi ∈ N1 . If xi is not adjacent to any vertices in N1 , then we are done
by Lemma 3.4. So suppose xi xj ∈ E for some xj ∈ N1 . By Lemma 2.1, xi and xj have
no other neighbours in N1 . By invoking Lemma 3.4 twice, we obtain |Vi | = |Vk | = |Vj |
for each k ∈ [∆] \ {i, j}. Such a set [∆] \ {i, j} is non-empty as ∆ ≥ 3, which gives
the required result.
Lemma 3.7. |Vi | ∈ {∆ − 2, ∆ − 1} for all i ∈ [∆].
Proof. For the upper bound, observe that ∆ ≥ deg(xi ) ≥ |Vi | + 1, where the +1
term comes from the edge xi v0 . We now show the lower bound (see Figure 2 for an
illustration). If there are two non-adjacent parts, select these as Vi and Vj . Otherwise,
select any pair of parts. Let v ∈ Vi . We study how vertices in Vj are covered by v
via proxies. By Lemma 3.4 and Lemma 3.5, v is adjacent to a vertex in every part
that is not Vj , so v is adjacent to a vertex in at least ∆ − 2 parts. If |Vj | ≤ ∆ − 3,
then by the pigeonhole principle, two neighbours of v must have the same neighbour
in Vj . This gives a C4 , and thus |Vj | ≥ ∆ − 2. By Lemma 3.6, |Vi | = |Vj | ≥ ∆ − 2 for
all i ∈ [∆].

Lemma 3.8. If there are non-adjacent parts, then |Vi | = ∆ − 2 for all i ∈ [∆].
Proof. Let Vi , Vj be non-adjacent parts. Let ui ∈ Vi . Then ui covers the vertices
of Vj only via proxy in other parts; there are ∆ − 2 such edges, and thus |Vj | ≤ ∆ − 2.
Consequently, by Lemmas 3.6 and 3.7, we obtain |Vi | = ∆ − 2 for all i ∈ [∆].

   • Type-1 if xi has no neighbours in N1 and v has no neighbours in Vi ;

   • Type-2 if xi has a neighbour in N1 and v has no neighbours in Vi ;
   • Type-3 if xi has a neighbour in N1 and v has one neighbour in Vi ;
Lemma 3.9. Every vertex in N2 is of Type-1, of Type-2, or of Type-3.
Proof. Since N1 is P3 -free by Lemma 2.1, xi has at most one neighbour in N1 . Suppose
first that xi has a neighbour in N1 . Let v ∈ Vi . Since Vi ⊆ N1 (xi ), v has at most one
neighbour in Vi by Lemma 2.1. Then v is either of Type-2 or of Type-3.
    So suppose xi has no neighbours in N1 , and suppose v has a neighbour y in Vi .
Note that v must cover xj via proxy in Vj , for all j ∈ [∆] \ {i}. This gives deg(v) ≥
∆ − 1 + 2 = ∆ + 1, where the +2 term comes from the edges vy, vxi . This contradicts
our choice of ∆, and so v cannot have a neighbour in Vi . Thus v must be of Type-
1.

Lemma 3.10. A vertex of Type-1 is of degree ∆.

Proof. Let v ∈ Vi be of Type-1. Then v must cover all vertices in N1 via adjacency or
via proxy. Via adjacency, it can cover exactly one vertex xi , since by assumption, xi
has no neighbours in N1 . All other ∆ − 1 vertices in N1 must be covered via proxy,
and no two vertices in N1 can be covered by the same proxy, by Lemma 3.2. So
deg(v) = ∆.

Lemma 3.11. A vertex of Type-2 is of degree ∆ − 1.

covers another vertex xj . All other ∆ − 2 vertices in N1 must be covered via proxy,
and no two vertices in N1 can be covered by the same proxy, by Lemma 3.2. It cannot
cover vertices in N1 twice via proxy, as that would form a C4 . So deg(v) = ∆ − 1.

 Type-2.

    For the remainder of this proof, we write p(u) to denote the number of vertices
in Vj which are covered by u via proxy. As |Vj | = ∆ − 2 by assumption and as u must
cover all vertices of Vj , we must have p(u) ∈ {∆ − 3, ∆ − 2}. Indeed, p(u) > ∆ − 2
will lead to a vertex being covered twice via proxy and hence a C4 ; on the other
hand, p(u) < ∆ − 3 will mean that u does not cover all vertices of Vj , violating
the diameter-2 condition. If p(u) = ∆ − 3, then u covers each vertex of Vj exactly
once. If p(u) = ∆ − 2, then one vertex must be covered twice, since v is covered via
adjacency. To prevent covering the same vertex twice via proxy, this would mean v
must be covered once via adjacency and once via proxy. Then, u and v must lie in
a C3 .

Case 1: (a, b) = (1, 1). By Lemma 3.10, since we do not consider the edge xi u
in G[N2 ], we have degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆ − 1. The edge uv does not cover any vertices of Vj
via proxy. Each of the remaining ∆ − 2 edges will cover one vertex in Vj via proxy,
since Vi and Vj are adjacent to all parts. So p(u) = ∆ − 2.

Case 2: (a, b) is (1, 2) (or (2, 1)). By Lemma 3.10, degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆ − 1. Again, uv
does not contribute to p(u) just like in Case 1. Of the remaining ∆ − 2 edges, one
is lost (i.e. it does not cover anything in Vj via proxy) as Vk is adjacent to Vi and
is non-adjacent to Vj . Thus p(u) = ∆ − 3. In case of (2, 1), we swap the roles of u
and v.

Case 3: (a, b) is (1, 3) (or (3, 1)). By Lemma 3.10, degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆−1. Via uv, we
also cover w via proxy. Of the remaining ∆ − 2 edges, one is lost as Vk is non-adjacent
to Vj . Thus, p(u) = 1 + ∆ − 3 = ∆ − 2.

Case 4: (a, b) = (2, 2). By Lemma 3.11, degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆ − 2. The edge uv does
not contribute to p(u). From the remaining ∆ − 3 edges, we get that p(u) ≤ ∆ − 3.
However, we lose one edge as Vk is non-adjacent to Vj . Thus, p(u) < ∆ − 3 which
means this case is impossible.

Case 5: (a, b) is (2, 3) (or (3, 2)). By Lemma 3.11, degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆ − 2. Via uv,
we also cover w via proxy. Of the remaining ∆ − 3 edges, one is again lost to Vk , and
thus p(u) = 1 + ∆ − 4 = ∆ − 3. (Compared to Case 4, u now also covers a vertex w
of Vj via proxy using the edge uv.)

Case 6: (a, b) = (3, 3). By Lemma 3.12, degG[N2 ] (u) = ∆ − 1. Via uv, we also
cover w via proxy. Of the remaining ∆ − 2 edges, one is lost to Vk , and thus p(u) =
1 + ∆ − 3 = ∆ − 2.

4    Necessary conditions for a proper 3-coloring
Throughout this section, we assume the existence of a proper 3-coloring c : V → [3]
and show some necessary conditions regarding the canonical partition and ∆. Without
loss of generality, we assume c(v0 ) = 1. Then c(xi ) ∈ {2, 3} for all i, and so the vertices
in Vi may only receive colors from [3] \ {c(xi )} = {1, 5 − c(xi )}. We say that Vi is of
color class 5 − c(xi ), denoted by c(Vi ) = 5 − c(xi ). For example, if c(xi ) = 2, then Vi
is of color class 3 or c(Vi ) = 3. If c(xi ) = 3, then Vi is of color class 2 or c(Vi ) = 2.

Lemma 4.1. Suppose Vi and Vj are non-adjacent parts. If there exists a proper 3-
coloring, then they must be of different color classes, i.e., c(Vi ) ̸= c(Vj ).
Proof. As xi and xj are adjacent, c(xi ) ̸= c(xj ). So c(Vi ) ̸= c(Vj ).
    Suppose c(V1 ) = c(V2 ) = 2. By the contrapositive of Lemma 4.1, V1 , V2 are
adjacent. Suppose also that both parts contain a non-Type-2 vertex u1 ∈ V1 , u2 ∈ V2
where u1 u2 ∈ E. By Lemma 3.14, u1 and u2 lie in a C3 . Since c(V1 ) = c(V2 ), the
third point of the C3 , say u3 , must be in a part of color class 3. We say that u1 u2 u3 is
a (V1 , 2)-triangle. Formally, we call a C3 xyz a (Vi , 2)-triangle if x ∈ Vi where c(Vi ) =
2, y ∈ Vj where c(Vj ) = 2 and Vi ̸= Vj , and z ∈ Vk where c(Vk ) = 3. In other words,
a (Vi , 2)-triangle contains a vertex from Vi , a vertex from the same color class as Vi ,
and a vertex from a different color class from Vi .
    For example, consider Figure 3b. Suppose c(V1 ) = c(V3 ) = 2 and c(V2 ) = c(V4 ) =
3 (it is routine to check that this would not be extendable to a proper 3-coloring).
Then a1 c2 d2 and a2 c1 d1 are two (V1 , 2)-triangles. It is worth noting that they are
also both (V3 , 2)-triangles.
Lemma 4.2. Suppose V1 contains a Type-1 vertex or a Type-3 vertex, and sup-
pose c(V1 ) = 2. Consider two (V1 , 2)-triangles abc, xyz where a, x ∈ V1 . The C3 ’s
must be vertex disjoint except possibly at the vertex in V1 , i.e., {b, c} ∩ {y, z} = ∅.
Proof. Otherwise, we need to consider two cases. Assume without loss of generality
that b = y if {b, c} ∩ {y, z} ̸= ∅. The two cases are a ̸= x, b = y and a = x, b = y. In
the first case, b has two neighbours a, x in V1 , which means x1 abx forms a C4 . In the
second case, c, z are both neighbours of a and b. So azbc forms a C4 . In both cases
we obtain a C4 , which gives the required contradiction.

Lemma 4.3. Suppose c(V1 ) = 2. Suppose there at least t-many (V1 , 2)-triangles.
Let d denote the number of parts of color class 3. Then there exists a part of color
class 3, which contains at least t/d vertices which must be given the color 3.
Proof. This follows immediately from Lemma 4.2, as vertices with color 3 in (V1 , 2)-
triangles must be distinct.
Lemma 4.4. Suppose there are at least 3 parts Vi , Vj , Vk of the same color class. If
there exists a proper 3-coloring, then each part must contain the same even number ℓ
of vertices, and in each of Vi , Vj , Vk , exactly ℓ/2 vertices must be colored 1 and exactly
ℓ/2 vertices must be colored c(Vi ).
Proof. By the contrapositive of Lemma 4.1, Vi , Vj , Vk are pairwise adjacent. By
Lemma 3.4, there is a perfect matching on the vertices in any pair of parts Vi , Vj , Vk .
Suppose without loss of generality that c(Vi ) = 2. Suppose α vertices of Vi are col-
ored 1 and ℓ − α vertices are colored 2. Then ℓ − α vertices of Vj are colored 1 and α
are colored 2 (by the matching on Vi and Vj ), and similarly ℓ − α vertices of Vk are
colored 1 and α are colored 2 (by the matching on Vi and Vk ). By comparing the
perfect matching on Vj and Vk , we must have that ℓ − α vertices of Vk are colored 2.
This means that ℓ − α = α, which gives α = ℓ/2.
Corollary 4.5. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4. If |Vi | = ∆ − 2 and if ∆ is odd, G is not 3-colorable.
Proof. Suppose there is a proper 3-coloring. If ∆ ≥ 5, then there are at least three
parts of the same color class. If |Vi | = ∆ − 2, then ∆ must be even by Lemma 4.4.

In this section, we exhaustively check the 3-colorability of all possible graph configu-

Type-1 vertices (Lemma 5.2 and Lemma 5.6), only Type-3 vertices (Lemma 5.5),
only Type-1 and Type-2 vertices (Lemma 5.3), only Type-1 and Type-3 vertices

based on proof similarity. For all cases apart from Lemma 5.7, we show that the
claims hold for ∆ ≥ 4.
    We begin with the following lemma, which will be used in the proofs of Lemmas 5.2
to 5.4.
Lemma 5.1. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4, and let v ∈ N2 be a Type-1 vertex. Then the number
of edges incident to v which are contained in a C3 is even.

Proof. If the number in the claim is odd, then at least two of the C3 ’s must share an
edge, which would result in a C4 . This is a contradiction.
Lemma 5.2. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4. Suppose N2 contains vertices only of Type-1, and
suppose |V1 | = ∆ − 2. If such a graph exists, then it is not 3-colorable.

Proof. If ∆ is odd, then by Corollary 4.5, the graph would not be 3-colorable. So we
assume ∆ is even. Let v ∈ V1 . By Lemma 3.14, all incident edges of v in G[N2 ] is in
a C3 . But there are exactly degG[N2 ] (v) = ∆ − 1 many such edges, which is an odd
number of edges. This contradicts Lemma 5.1, and thus such graphs do not exist.
Lemma 5.3. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4. Suppose N2 contains vertices only of Type-1 and
Type-2, and it contains at least one vertex of each type. If such a graph exists, then
it is not 3-colorable.
Proof. By Lemma 3.8, each part contains ∆−2 elements. By Corollary 4.5, the graph
is not 3-colorable when ∆ is odd. So we assume ∆ is even. By Corollary 3.15, there
are ∆ − 2 Type-1 parts and 2 Type-2 parts. Relabelling as needed, let V1 be a Type-1
part, and let v ∈ V1 . Observe that in G[N2 ], exactly ∆ − 3 edges incident to v are
contained in a C3 , by Lemmas 3.10 and 3.14. Since ∆ is even, this number is odd.
This contradicts Lemma 5.1, and thus such graphs do not exist.
   The proof of the following lemma follows a similar argument used in the proof of
Lemma 5.2, and is therefore omitted.

Lemma 5.4. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4. Suppose N2 contains vertices only of Type-1 and

Lemma 5.6. Suppose ∆ ≥ 4. Suppose N2 contains vertices only of Type-1, and
suppose |V1 | = ∆ − 1. Then G must be isomorphic to a Moore graph and it is
not 3-colorable.
Proof. We show first that the graph G must be C3 -free. Within G[N2 ], each vertex
is of degree ∆ − 1 by Lemma 3.10. This implies that no vertex can cover another
vertex in another part twice, as otherwise it would fail to cover all vertices in the part.
So G[N2 ] must be C3 -free. Observe that v0 and the vertices in N1 cannot be in a C3 ,
since no two vertices of N1 nor two vertices in Vi are adjacent. It follows that G must
be C3 -free, and thus G must be C3 -free and C4 -free.
    Observe that G must be regular. Indeed, all vertices in N1 is of degree ∆ since each
part has ∆ − 1 elements. Furthermore, all vertices in N2 are Type-1 by Lemma 3.8,
and thus of degree ∆ by Lemma 3.10.
    So G is a regular graph of diameter-2 and girth 5, and thus G must be isomorphic to
a Moore graph of degree-7 or degree-57. The degree-7 variant, the Hoffman-Singleton
graph, has chromatic number 4 ([1]); using Hoffman’s bound (see for example [7]),
the chromatic number of a Moore graph of degree-57 is at least 9.
   For the final lemma, we require a stronger condition on the max degree ∆.

    We summarize the results obtained in this section.

Theorem 5.8. Let G be a C4 -free graph of diameter-2 with no universal vertex,
where ∆(G) ≥ 17. Then G is not 3-colorable.
Proof. Let v0 be a vertex with deg(v0 ) = ∆ = ∆(G), and let N2 = N2 (v0 ). Since
there is no universal vertex, N2 ̸= ∅. Every vertex in N2 is of Type-1, Type-2, or
of Type-3 by Lemma 3.9. By Lemma 3.13, if there is either a Type-1 or a Type-
2 vertex, then we may always assume that there is a Type-1 part in the canonical
partition. Furthermore, every part is of the same cardinality (Lemma 3.6) and every
part has ∆ − 2 or ∆ − 1 elements (Lemma 3.7). We have exhaustively checked that
when each part contains ∆ − 2 elements, the graph is not 3-colorable (Lemmas 5.2
to 5.5 and 5.7). On the other hand, if each part contains ∆ − 1 elements, then G must
contain only Type-1 parts (Lemma 3.8). In that case, G must be isomorphic to a
Moore graph of 57, which is not 3-colorable (Lemma 5.6). This proves the claim.


6    Proof of Theorem 1.1
We are now ready to prove Theorem 1.1.

Theorem 1.1. List 3-Coloring C4 -free Diameter-2 is polynomial-time solvable.
Proof. Let G be a C4 -free graph of diameter-2. If N2 = ∅, then we can solve the
instance in polynomial-time by Lemma 3.1. So suppose N2 ̸= ∅. By Theorem 5.8, all
graphs with maximum degree at least 17, are not 3-colorable. The list 3-colorability
of the remaining finitely many graphs with maximum degree at most 16 can be hard-
coded into a lookup table. One can determine the maximum degree in polynomial-
time, and thus deciding if G is list 3-colorable can be solved in polynomial-time.


7    Discussion
We showed that List 3-Coloring C4 -free Diameter-2 is polynomial-time solv-
able. It turns out that many C4 -free graphs of diameter-2 with maximum degree at
least 17 are not 3-colorable (Theorem 5.8).
    The key ingredient was the rigid structure arising from the C4 -free and diameter-2
conditions. By anchoring on a vertex v0 of maximum degree ∆, one can show that
every vertex in the graph must be of degree ∆ or ∆ − 1. Furthermore, one can
partition the vertices which are distance-2 away from v0 into ∆ parts. Each part
contains the same number of vertices and if they are adjacent, the vertices form a
perfect matching.
    The ∆ ≥ 17 condition for non-3-colorability arose from ensuring an impossible
number of vertices of a certain color in the proof of Lemma 5.7. Interestingly, it was
only needed in one part of the proof; the other parts required weaker conditions on ∆.
Furthermore, non-3-colorability was shown for ∆ ≥ 4 in all other results in the same
section (Lemmas 5.2 to 5.5). So a natural question is to ask if we can strengthen
the ∆ ≥ 17 result to ∆ ≥ 4. An affirmative answer would imply that the only C4 -free
graphs of diameter-2 with N2 ̸= ∅ are the three leftmost graphs in Figure 1.

   Finally, the problem remains open when we replace the C4 -free condition by
the C3 -free condition, or if we remove the C4 -free condition outright. A natural
question is to ask if any of the structural results of Section 3 remain true. The an-
swer is a definitive no. Lemma 3.2, which states that every vertex in N2 has exactly
one neighbour in N1 already breaks, as the ‘exactly’ becomes ‘at least’. So vertices
in N2 cannot be partitioned based on their adjacencies to N1 , which means that the
canonical partition would no longer exist. A new strategy is most likely needed to
tackle these problems.
