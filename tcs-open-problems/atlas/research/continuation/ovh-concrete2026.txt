                                                Abstract
                                         The Orthogonal Vectors Problem (OVn,d ) takes as input two sets A, B each containing n d-dimensional
                                         Boolean vectors, and outputs 1 if and only if there exists a ∈ A and b ∈ B such that a and b are
                                         orthogonal. The OV conjecture states that for every ε > 0, there exists a constant c ≥ 1 such that
                                         there is no algorithm deciding OVn,d for d = c log n with running time O(n2−ε ). The analogous kOV
                                         conjecture hypothesizes a lower bound of nk−ϵ for the same problem with k sets. We prove these
arXiv:2607.23799v1 [cs.CC] 26 Jul 2026




                                         results and variants unconditionally in concrete computational models.

                                              We study a natural monotone version of the kOV-conjecture and shows that it holds for monotone
                                              circuits and constant-depth (not necessarily monotone) circuits when d = nΩ(1) .
                                              We show that the monotone version of the OV conjecture holds for monotone circuits. More
                                              formally, we show that for every ϵ > 0, there exists c such that any monotone circuit family
                                              computing the negation of OVn,d with d = c log n must have size Ω(n2−ϵ ).
                                              We also prove stronger Boolean formula and branching program lower bounds for OVn,d , strength-
                                              ening a previous result of Kane and Williams (ITCS 2019). In particular, our Boolean formula
                                              lower bound of Ω(n2 d) is tight up to constant factors.

                                          1      Introduction
                                         Motivation. The Orthogonal Vectors (OVn,d ) problem is the computational problem of
                                         deciding if among two given sets A and B of n d-dimensional Boolean vectors, there exists
                                         a vector a ∈ A and a vector b ∈ B such that a and b are orthogonal. This problem has
                                         received a lot of attention over the past decade for being able to capture the hardness of
                                         several important computational problems that are seemingly unrelated. Some important
                                         examples of these include edit distance [9], subset sum [3], and longest common subsequence
                                         [1]. It has been established that if any of these problems has an algorithm that runs in truly
                                         sub-quadratic time, then so does OV. For a more thorough treatment of this line of work, we
                                         refer the reader to the survey by Vassilevska Williams [35]. This body of research has led to
                                         the following natural conjecture about the time complexity of deciding OVn,d .

                                         ▶ Conjecture 1 (OV conjecture). For every ϵ > 0, there exists c ≥ 1 such that there is no
                                         algorithm (deterministic or randomized) deciding OVn,d for d = c log n with running time
                                         O(n2−ϵ ).
2   New and Improved Concrete Lower Bounds for Orthogonal Vectors


    In particular, when d ∈ ω(log n), the conjecture states that there is no truly subquadratic
    algorithm deciding OVn,d . It should be noted that when d < log n, there are indeed sub-
    quadratic algorithms known [34]. Also, when d = c log n, Abboud, Williams and Yu [4] show
    that OVn,d can be decided by a randomized algorithm in time O(n2−1/O(log c) ). This was
    later derandomized by Chan and Williams [17].
        As positive evidence for the OV conjecture, Williams [33] showed that the Strong Ex-
    ponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) [13, 23] implies the OV conjecture. Thus, proving the
    latter can also be seen as a stepping stone to SETH.
        The fact that OV captures the hardness of a wide variety of problems makes it very
    interesting to ask if we can prove these conjectures unconditionally in restricted models of
    computation. The relevance of this line of research is twofold: the first, to rule out proposed
    algorithmic paradigms for these problems and the second, to find structural characteristics
    of the problem that will hopefully lead to an unrestricted lower bound in the future.
        A recent line of research has been studying precisely this question. Kane and Williams
    [26] showed strong lower bounds for the size of Boolean Formulas and Branching Programs
    computing OV thus proving the OV-conjecture for these two models. Choudhury and
    Sreenivasaiah [19] show that this conjecture is true when computation is restricted to depth-3
    OR ◦ AND ◦ OR circuits with constant bottom fan-in.
        This latter work even proves such a result for the more general kOV-conjecture. In the
    kOVn,d problem, the input is k lists A1 , . . . , Ak ⊆ {0, 1}d of size n each and the question is
    to decide if there exist vectors a1 ∈ A1 , . . . , ak ∈ Ak such that a1 ∩ a2 ∩ . . . ak = ∅ (here we
    identify each Boolean vector ai ∈ {0, 1}d with a subset of [d] in the natural way). The kOV
    conjecture, analogous to Conjecture 1 is as follows.

    ▶ Conjecture 2 (kOV conjecture). For every ϵ > 0 and k ≥ 2, there exists c ≥ 1 such that
    there is no algorithm (deterministic or randomized) deciding kOVn,d for d = c log n with
    running time O(nk−ϵ ).

    The complexity of kOV also has intimate connections with several important functions
    including graph diameter [10] and longest common subsequence [1]. (See [35] for more.)

    Results. We now describe the results of this paper, which yield new and stronger statements
    along these lines.

    Monotone and constant-depth Boolean circuits. A major focus of this paper is on
    monotone computation. Observe that the negation of OV (and more generally kOV) is a
    monotone function.1 We define the function kIntn,d : ({0, 1}d )n × ({0, 1}d )n → {0, 1} as the
    negation of kOVn,d .

    ▶ Definition 3 (kIntn,d ). Fix any k ≥ 2. For tuples A1 , A2 , . . . , Ak ⊆ {0, 1}d with |A1 | =
    · · · = |Ak | = n,

         kIntn,d (A1 , . . . , Ak ) = 1 ⇐⇒ ∀a1 ∈ A1 , ∀ak ∈ Ak , we have a1 ∩ a2 ∩ · · · ak ̸= ∅.

    (where we interpret each Boolean vector ai as the characteristic vector of a subset of [d].) In
    the particular case that k = 2, we use Intn,d to denote kIntn,d .

In light of Conjectures 1 and 2, it is natural to study the complexity of Intn,d and kIntn,d for
monotone circuits2 and prove that any monotone Boolean circuit for this problem must have
size at least n2−ϵ for d = c log n (where c = c(ϵ) is large enough). If Conjecture 1 is true for
general Boolean circuits, then this monotone lower bound must also hold. On the other hand,
we note that the power of monotone circuits is likely to be incomparable with non-monotone
models such as depth-3 circuits, Boolean formulas and branching programs studied in earlier
work. In particular, there are P-complete problems that have small monotone circuits and it
is unlikely that these can be simulated by formulas or branching programs in polynomial size
(this can be proved unconditionally for depth-3 circuits). This makes the monotone question
interesting.
    Another computational model we study in this paper is the class of constant-depth
circuits.3 This circuit class can implement many non-trivial algorithms such as the colour-
coding algorithms for subgraph isomorphism [6] and is incomparable with models studied in
previous work (and provably stronger than the depth-3 model studied in [18]).
    Our first result is a proof of a weak form of Conjecture 2 (and thus also a weak form of
Conjecture 1) in the above two settings. The reason this is a ‘weak form’ is that this proof
works when the dimension d of the input vectors is nΩ(1) .

▶ Theorem 4. Fix any constants k, h ≥ 2 and any constant ϵ > 0. Any monotone circuit or
any depth-h (not necessarily monotone) circuit computing kIntn,d for d = nϵ must have size
at least nk−ϵ .4

    The constant-depth circuit lower bound is a resolution (in a weaker form) of a question
due to Paturi stated in the work of Kane and Williams [26].
    While the above theorem only proves lower bounds for relatively large values of d, it is
worth noting that the OV problem is already interesting for fine-grained complexity in this
setting [22, 2].
    Our next result is a complete resolution of the monotone Boolean circuit version of
Conjecture 1.

▶ Theorem 5. For every 0 < ϵ < 1, there exists a constant c such that for all d ≥ c log n
any monotone circuit computing Intn,d requires at least Ω(n2−ϵ ) many gates.

Boolean Formulas and Branching Programs. In our third result, we revisit the Boolean
formula and Branching Program lower bounds of Kane and Williams [26]. We improve the
parameters in both results. In particular, this leads to a tight lower bound (up to constant
factors) for Boolean formulas over the full binary basis. More precisely, we show the following.

▶ Theorem 6 (Boolean formula and Branching Program lower bound for OVn,d (Informal)).
Assume d > c log n for c > 1. Any binary Boolean formula computing OVn,d has size at least
Ω(n2 d). Any Boolean branching program for OVn,d has size at least Ω(n2 d/ log(nd)).

   We note that this bound is tight up to constant factors, as OVn,d has a simple Boolean
formula of size O(n2 d). Kane and Williams [26] prove a weaker lower bound of Ω(n2 / log d)

    Circuit constructions. Kane and Williams [26] observed that the standard brute-force

    bound can be achieved using monotone circuits.



    1.1    Techniques
    Lower bounds via reductions. The proof of Theorem 4 is based on a simple split-and-list
    monotone reduction (inspired from [2]) from graph and hypergraph versions of the clique5
    problem to kIntn,d . The basic observation is that a set S of vertices forms a clique of size
    ℓ in a graph (or hypergraph) if and only if it can be partitioned into k subsets S1 , . . . , Sk
    of size ℓ/k each such that each of them is a clique and each edge contained in the union
    S1 ∪ · · · ∪ Sk is present in the graph (or hypergraph). This can be turned  into a reduction

    from clique on m-vertex (hyper-)graphs by creating k lists of size ℓ/k    where each element
    of the ith list corresponds to a set Si of ℓ/k vertices and the corresponding vectors (which
    have dimension mO(1) each) are orthogonal if and only if S1 ∪ · · · ∪ Sk form a clique.
        To obtain the required lower bound for kIntn,d from here, we need a strong lower bound
    (mℓ·(1−o(1)) ) for the clique problem on graphs or hypergraphs. In the monotone setting, such
    bounds have been known for a long time due to work of Razborov [31] (we use a statement
    from a paper of Alon and Boppana [5]). Similar results are known for hypergraph versions of
    the clique problem via a result of Amano [7]. Along with the reduction, these results imply
    the desired lower bound. The reduction and its consequences appear in Section 3.
        Abboud, Bringmann, Dell and Nederlof [2] showed how to use the same high-level idea
    to reduce the problem of deciding r-partite clique on r-partite r-uniform hypergraphs to
    kIntn,d .6 Unfortunately, the requisite circuit lower bounds are not known in the r-partite
    setting and so we need to modify their reduction. The same work also gives reductions from
    the general r-uniform hypergraph clique problem to the r-partite case but it is unclear if
    such reductions can be implemented by monotone circuits.

    OV conjecture for monotone circuits. The above reduction gives us the required lower
    bound for monotone circuits computing Intn,d when the dimension d is larger than the range
    prescribed in Conjecture 1. The reasons for this have to do with existing monotone lower
    bounds for Clique not being tight in a fine-grained sense. Unfortunately, even the most
    optimistic monotone circuit lower bound for Clique (which is not known) would not yield
    a near quadratic lower bound for all d = ω(log n). (This is described in more detail in

    monotone circuits.

    To do this, we turn to known techniques for proving strong monotone circuit lower
bounds, which have been known since the 1980s. Razborov [31] developed a method to
prove superpolynomial lower bounds on the size of monotone circuits computing Clique.
This technique is now called the ‘approximation method’. Since Razborov’s work, the
approximation method has been the main approach to proving lower bounds against monotone
circuits and has been refined and strengthened in several works such as [5, 8]. A crucial
ingredient in the original approximation method is the Sunflower Lemma by Erdös and Rado
[21]. Recently, Rossman [32] defined a more relaxed version of Sunflowers, now commonly
known as “robust Sunflowers” and showed average case lower bounds for computing Clique.
This has also been used to strengthen worst case monotone size lower bounds [16], [14].
    To achieve better results than the one we get from the above reduction (Section 4), we
apply a modified version of Razborov’s approximation method directly to the Intn,d problem.
For readers not familiar with the standard approximation method, we describe it briefly here.
The idea is to start with a monotone circuit C of size s computing the target function f ,
and show that C can be “approximated” closely using a small depth-two circuit (i.e. a DNF
or a CNF) F̃ . Here by ‘approximation’, we mean the behaviour of F̃ with respect to C on
two distributions D0 and D1 on 0-inputs and 1-inputs of f respectively. Then we show that
on the one hand, any small depth-two circuit must have large error, say δ, with respect to f .
On the other hand, the DNF F̃ that we constructed has error at most sϵ with respect to C.
Then we can conclude s ≥ δ/ϵ.
    In the typical setting where the approximation method is used, the notion of ‘small’ for
the depth-two circuits has to do with the number of terms/clauses, and their maximum
width. Ensuring that the approximator F̃ is small usually involves a closure operation that
uses the Sunflower Lemma or something similar [31, 5, 32] . In our setting, all these are
too demanding to be used. At a high level, this is because the marginal probabilities of our
chosen distribution D0 are too low for such techniques to be applicable. See Remark 18.
    The main difference between the standard approximation method and ours is in the
structure of the approximator. Instead of a DNF/CNF, our approximator is a conjunction of
arbitrary monotone Boolean functions that are each “local” to any one vector among the 2n
vectors. i.e., each function takes inputs from at most one vector. We define this in Definition
19. This allows us to avoid expensive combinatorial closures such as the Sunflower lemma
that are inapplicable. A crucial property that we maintain for our approximator is that each
function (in the conjunction) errs on D1 with a non-trivial probability. Maintaining this
property requires a technical lemma (Lemma 22) that is similar in spirit to the Kruskal-
Katona Theorem ([28, 27]) but in a much simpler setting where we work with a product
distribution.

Formula and Branching Program lower bounds. The source of improvement in the
formula and branching program lower bounds is the same, so we describe the formula case.
Consider the OVn,d problem with input lists A, B. The basic idea of Kane and Williams,
which fits into a classical framework for such lower bounds due to Nechiporuk [29], is to show
that for any formula F and each input vector a ∈ A, the bits of a label many leaves of F.
This can be done by setting all the bits of the other elements of A to 1 and7 then setting the
other elements of B from a fixed subset X ⊆ {0, 1}d of size n. For each subset X ′ ⊆ X, we
get a restricted function on the bits of a by using exactly the elements of X ′ as assignments

    1.2      Other related work
    kOVn,d when d is large. As mentioned above, the kOVn,d problem is already interesting
    in the setting when d = nΩ(1) . This problem was introduced in work of Gao, Impagliazzo,
    Kolokolova and Williams [22] who studied its connections to the fine-grained complexity of a
    large class of problems defined by first-order logic formulas. This was also further studied in
    the aforementioned work of Abboud et al. [2] who made connections to weighted versions of
    hypergraph clique problems and optimization variants of SAT.

    Jukna’s criterion. Another well-known route for proving monotone circuit lower bounds
    is via the monotone switching lemma formulated by Jukna [24] and Berg and Ulfberg [11].
    Unfortunately, the criterion used for lower bounds in these works does not seem to be
    applicable to our problem. See Section 7 for a justification.

     In this section, we show a construction of monotone circuits to compute Int. We first need
     the following characterization of 0-inputs of Intn,d .

     ▶ Lemma 29. Let x = (A, B) be an input of Intn,d . Then Intn,d (x) = 0 if and only if
     there exists an ordered partition P = (P1 , P2 ) of [d] into two parts such that there exists
     a ∈ A, b ∈ B satisfying a ∩ P1 = ∅ and b ∩ P2 = ∅

     Proof. “ =⇒ ”: Let x = (A, B) be a 0-input of Intn,d . From Definition 3 there must exist
     a ∈ A, b ∈ B such that a ∩ b = ∅. Hence for all j ∈ [d], either aj = 0 or bj = 0 or both. For
     every j ∈ [d], we include j in the set P1 if aj = 0, and in P2 if bj = 0. If both aj and bj are
     0, then we include j in only P1 . Therefore each j ∈ [d] belongs to exactly one of P1 and
     P2 . This results is an ordered partition of [d] into two parts namely P1 and P2 . It is now
     straightforward to observe that P = (P1 , P2 ) satisfies the claim.
         “ ⇐= ”: Let x = (A, B) be an input of Intn,d . Suppose P = (P1 , P2 ) is an ordered
